Learning to Drive in the UK

Learning to drive can feel like a big step, but the process is much easier when you know what comes next. This complete guide covers getting your provisional licence, choosing an instructor, practising safely, passing the theory and practical tests, understanding the costs and getting ready for your first car.

Quick answer

How do you learn to drive in the UK?

For most new drivers, the route is straightforward: get a provisional driving licence, start lessons and practice, pass the theory test, become confident driving independently, then pass the practical driving test.

1. Get your provisional

You need a valid provisional driving licence before you can learn to drive a car on public roads.

2. Learn to drive

Take lessons with an instructor and, where possible, add safe private practice with a suitable supervising driver.

3. Pass the theory

Pass both the multiple-choice and hazard perception parts of the car theory test.

4. Pass the practical

Show the examiner that you can drive safely, confidently and independently in normal road conditions.

Best advice:

Do not judge your progress by how many lessons somebody else needed. Some learners need more time than others. What matters is being able to drive safely without your instructor constantly helping you.

Step 1

Getting your provisional driving licence

Your provisional licence is the starting point. You need it before taking driving lessons on public roads or booking your theory test.

You can normally apply for your first provisional licence from the age of 15 years and 9 months, although most people cannot start learning to drive a car on public roads until they are 17.

Online application

£34
Cheapest option

Applying through the official GOV.UK service is normally the quickest and cheapest way to get your first provisional licence.

Postal application

£43
Costs more

You can also apply by post using a D1 application form.

Eyesight

20 metres
Legal requirement

You must be able to read a modern vehicle number plate from 20 metres away, using glasses or contact lenses if you normally need them.

Avoid unnecessary booking fees

Use the official GOV.UK services when applying for your licence or booking tests. Third-party websites can look official but may add their own fees.

Getting started

What should you know before your first driving lesson?

You are not expected to arrive at your first lesson already knowing how to drive. That is what the lesson is for.

Your instructor will normally begin with the basic controls and gradually introduce more difficult situations as your confidence improves.

Vehicle controls

You will learn the pedals, steering, gears if driving a manual, mirrors, indicators, parking brake and other important controls.

Moving and stopping

Early lessons usually cover moving away safely, stopping, steering and checking your surroundings properly.

Road awareness

As you improve, lessons move towards junctions, roundabouts, meeting traffic, speed control and independent decision-making.

Driving lessons

Choosing a driving instructor

A good instructor can make a huge difference. Do not choose somebody only because they offer the cheapest hourly rate.

Anyone you pay to teach you must be a qualified approved driving instructor or an authorised trainee instructor.

Look for an instructor who...

  • ✓Explains mistakes calmly
  • ✓Arrives reliably
  • ✓Gives you useful feedback
  • ✓Lets you progress at a sensible pace
  • ✓Prepares you for independent driving

Consider changing if...

  • ✓You regularly feel uncomfortable
  • ✓Lessons constantly start late
  • ✓You are not told what needs improving
  • ✓You feel stuck doing the same thing
  • ✓You feel pressured into taking the test too early
Remember:

There is no legal minimum number of professional lessons or practice hours before taking the car test. Readiness matters more than hitting a particular number.

Choosing your route

Should you learn in a manual or automatic car?

This is worth deciding early because the type of car you use for your test affects what you can drive afterwards.

Manual

You will need to learn clutch control and gear changes as well as everything else involved in driving.

Passing in a manual car gives you more flexibility because you can drive both manual and automatic cars within the relevant licence category.

Automatic

There is no clutch pedal and the car changes gear for you, so some learners find it easier to concentrate on the road.

However, passing your test in an automatic normally restricts your licence to automatic cars unless you later pass a manual test.

Which is better?

There is no single right answer. If you want the widest choice of cars, manual gives you more flexibility. If you expect to drive automatic, hybrid or electric cars and find clutch control difficult, automatic may suit you better.

Private practice

Practising outside your driving lessons

Private practice can be very useful because it gives you more time behind the wheel without paying for every hour with an instructor.

However, you cannot simply get into a family member's car and start driving. The learner, supervising driver and vehicle must meet the legal requirements.

The supervising driver

They must normally be at least 21, be qualified to drive the type of car you are using and have held a full licence for at least three years.

Insurance

You must be properly insured as a learner driver. Never assume that the car owner's normal insurance automatically covers you.

The car

It must be legal and roadworthy, with the required tax and MOT where applicable, and it must display the correct learner plates.

Insurance matters

Driving without the correct insurance can have serious consequences. Always check the policy before starting private practice.

Learner rules

Important rules while you are learning

A provisional licence lets you learn, but it does not give you the same freedom as a full licence.

  • ✓You must have the correct provisional driving licence.
  • ✓You must meet the minimum eyesight requirement.
  • ✓You must be properly supervised during private practice.
  • ✓You must have suitable learner driver insurance.
  • ✓The car must display L plates, or the permitted D plates in Wales.
  • ✓The vehicle must be legal and safe to use on the road.

Learner drivers can drive on motorways in England, Scotland and Wales, but only when accompanied by an approved driving instructor and driving a car fitted with dual controls.

Theory test

What happens in the UK theory test?

You need to pass the theory test before you can book the standard practical car driving test.

The test has two parts: multiple-choice questions and hazard perception. You take both during the same appointment and must pass both.

Test fee

£23
Official car test fee

Book through the official GOV.UK service to avoid unnecessary third-party charges.

Multiple choice

43 out of 50
Pass mark

You have 57 minutes to answer 50 multiple-choice questions.

Hazard perception

44 out of 75
Pass mark

You must recognise developing hazards early rather than simply noticing objects on the road.

What should you revise?

  • ✓The Highway Code
  • ✓Road signs and markings
  • ✓Stopping distances
  • ✓Speed limits
  • ✓Motorway rules
  • ✓Vehicle safety
  • ✓Vulnerable road users
  • ✓Hazard awareness
Do not just memorise practice questions.

Try to understand why an answer is correct. That knowledge will help you during real driving as well as during the test.

Hazard perception

How the hazard perception test works

The hazard perception section checks whether you can recognise a situation that is starting to require action from the driver.

You watch 14 video clips showing everyday road situations. Each clip contains at least one developing hazard, and one clip contains two.

Spot it early

You score more points when you correctly identify a developing hazard early.

Think ahead

Look for situations that may force you to change speed or direction, not just obvious dangers.

Do not click constantly

Clicking continuously or using a repeated pattern can result in no score for that clip.

Practical test

What happens during the practical driving test?

The practical test is designed to check whether you can drive safely and make sensible decisions without needing constant help.

The normal on-road driving part lasts around 35 minutes. You will deal with normal traffic and road situations rather than driving around a closed course.

1. Eyesight check

Before driving, you will be asked to read a number plate from the required distance.

2. Vehicle safety

You will answer two vehicle safety questions, commonly called the “show me, tell me” questions.

3. Road driving

The examiner assesses your general driving ability, observations, control and decisions.

4. Reversing

You will be asked to complete a reversing exercise during the test.

The eyesight check

For a modern number plate, you normally need to read it from 20 metres. If you fail the eyesight check, the driving test ends.

Show me, tell me

One question asks you to explain how you would carry out a safety check before driving. The other asks you to demonstrate a simple vehicle control while driving when it is safe.

These can include things such as checking the brakes, checking fluid levels, operating the windscreen washers, using the horn or demisting the windscreen.

Remember:

The examiner is not looking for a perfect drive. They are checking whether you can drive safely and whether any mistakes you make create a serious risk.

Test readiness

How do you know when you are ready for the driving test?

Being able to operate the car is not the same as being ready for the test.

A good sign is when your instructor no longer needs to regularly tell you what to do.

You are getting close when...

  • ✓You make safe decisions independently
  • ✓You check mirrors without reminders
  • ✓You handle unfamiliar roads calmly
  • ✓You manage junctions and roundabouts safely
  • ✓You can recover safely from small mistakes

You probably need more practice if...

  • ✓Your instructor regularly has to intervene
  • ✓You frequently miss observations
  • ✓Busy situations overwhelm you
  • ✓You rely heavily on instructions
  • ✓You only drive confidently on familiar routes
Do not rush because you have a test booked.

A test appointment does not make you test ready. If your driving still needs work, more practice can be cheaper and less stressful than repeatedly paying for failed tests.

Learning costs

How much does learning to drive cost in the UK?

There is no single total because driving instructors set their own lesson prices and every learner needs a different amount of practice.

However, some official costs are fixed, so you can budget for those from the beginning.

Provisional licence

£34 online
£43 by post

Your first official cost when starting the learning process.

Theory test

£23
Per attempt

You pay again if you need to retake the full theory test.

Practical test

£62–£75
Depending on appointment

The standard fee is £62 on weekdays and £75 for available evening, weekend and bank holiday tests.

Driving lessons

Varies
Instructor sets price

There is no government-set lesson price, so compare local instructors rather than relying on a national average.

Learner insurance

Varies
If privately practising

The price depends on the learner, vehicle, policy and how long you need cover.

First-year driving

Budget carefully
Often the bigger cost

After passing, you may need to budget for a car, insurance, fuel, vehicle tax, servicing, MOTs and repairs.

Budget for the whole journey

Do not spend every penny on lessons and forget what happens afterwards. For many new drivers, buying and insuring the first car can cost considerably more than the tests themselves.

If things do not go to plan

What happens if you fail?

Failing either test is frustrating, but it does not mean you are a bad driver or that you should give up.

If you fail the theory test

Look at which areas caused problems and revise those properly before booking again. You must retake the full test even if you passed one part.

If you fail the practical test

Use the result as useful feedback. Work specifically on the faults identified rather than simply repeating the same lessons and hoping for a different result.

One failed test does not define your driving.

Plenty of safe drivers did not pass first time. The important thing is to understand why you failed and correct it before the next attempt.

You passed

What happens after you pass your driving test?

Passing is a brilliant feeling, but it is also when you begin learning how to drive without an instructor beside you.

You can normally start driving as soon as you pass, but you must have insurance that covers you to drive without supervision.

Arrange the right insurance

Learner insurance may no longer cover you once you have passed. Check your cover before driving independently.

Build experience gradually

You do not need to tackle your hardest possible journey on day one. Build confidence on different roads, in darkness and in poor weather gradually.

Keep learning

Passing the test means you have reached the required standard. Experience comes afterwards, so keep good habits rather than copying bad ones from other drivers.

Your first car

Choosing your first car after passing

Your first car does not need to be exciting or expensive. For most new drivers, affordable insurance, sensible running costs and reliability matter much more.

Check insurance first

Get insurance quotes before buying the car. Two similar-looking cars can have very different insurance costs for a new driver.

Check the history

Look at the MOT history, service history, mileage and condition before handing over any money.

Keep money for repairs

Do not use your entire budget to buy the car. A used car may soon need tyres, brakes, servicing or other maintenance.

First-car rule:

Check the insurance price before falling in love with the car. For a new driver, insurance can completely change whether a particular car is affordable.

Key Takeaways

What Every Learner Driver Should Remember

Before Your Test

  • ✓Use private practice where possible.
  • ✓Learn the Highway Code properly.
  • ✓Take the test when you can drive independently.

After You Pass

Frequently Asked Questions

Learning to Drive in the UK FAQs

These are some of the most common questions new UK drivers ask when starting lessons and preparing for their tests.

What age can you start learning to drive in the UK?

Most people can start learning to drive a car on public roads from age 17. You can apply for your first provisional driving licence from age 15 years and 9 months.

How much does a provisional driving licence cost?

A first provisional driving licence currently costs £34 when applying online through GOV.UK or £43 when applying by post.

How much is the UK car theory test?

The official car theory test currently costs £23.

How much is the practical driving test?

The standard car practical test currently costs £62 on weekdays or £75 for available evening, weekend and bank holiday appointments.

How many driving lessons do I need?

There is no legal minimum number of lessons or practice hours. Everyone learns at a different speed. You should take the test when you can drive safely and independently rather than after a particular number of lessons.

How long does a theory test pass last?

Your theory test pass certificate lasts for two years. You must pass the practical test within that time or you will need to take the theory test again.

Final Thoughts

Take Your Time and Learn Properly

Learning to drive is not a race. Some people pick things up quickly while others need more time, especially with busy junctions, roundabouts, clutch control or driving independently.

Concentrate on becoming a safe driver rather than simply becoming somebody who can pass a test. Learn the Highway Code, ask questions when you do not understand something and practise different road situations whenever it is safe to do so.

Once you pass, keep the good habits your instructor taught you. Your first months of independent driving are when you start building the experience that cannot be learned from a book or a test.
