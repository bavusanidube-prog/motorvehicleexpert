Quick Answer

Will a Boot or Tailgate Fault Fail an MOT?

Yes — if the bootlid or tailgate cannot be secured in the closed position, it is a Major MOT defect. That is the clearest boot-specific failure rule in the current inspection manual.

Other boot problems need more careful interpretation. A boot stuck shut, weak gas struts, a broken electric release, cosmetic panel damage and accident damage do not automatically receive the same result.

Major

Cannot Secure Closed

MOT Failure

A bootlid or tailgate that cannot be secured in the closed position is specifically classified as a Major defect.

Access Issue

Boot Stuck Shut

Different Rule

Being stuck shut is not automatically the same Major defect. But the test may be refused or abandoned if the tester cannot open the boot where access is required for proper examination.

Body Condition

Dangerous Rear Damage

Separate MOT Route

Insecure, injurious or detachable rear-body damage is assessed under the body-condition criteria rather than simply being labelled a boot latch defect.

Cosmetic damage alone is not the key issue

A scratched or dented boot panel is not automatically an MOT failure. The important questions are whether the panel is secure, can remain closed, creates an injury risk, is likely to detach or affects another testable item.

Do not tie an insecure tailgate shut and assume the defect is solved

The tailgate should operate and secure correctly using its intended locking and fastening system. A temporary restraint does not repair a defective latch, striker, hinge or distorted body opening.

Current MOT Rule

What Is the Exact MOT Rule for a Boot or Tailgate?

Under the current DVSA body-condition inspection, a bootlid, tailgate, dropside, loading door or access panel that cannot be secured in the closed position is a Major defect.

That wording matters because it separates the actual MOT defect from many related faults that drivers often group together under “broken boot”.

Condition MOT Treatment Result Practical Meaning
Boot/tailgate cannot be secured closed Major Fail Repair the latch, striker, alignment or other cause before test.
Boot stuck shut Access dependent Test may be refused or abandoned Applies where inability to open it prevents a proper examination.
Body panel insecure or likely to cause injury Major Fail Assessed separately under body condition.
Body component likely to become detached Dangerous Fail Immediate safety concern requiring repair before driving.
Cosmetic dent or scratch only No automatic defect Depends on condition Security, sharpness, attachment and effect on other test items matter.
Mechanic distinction: latch fault vs alignment fault

A tailgate may refuse to stay shut because the latch itself has failed, the striker has moved, the hinges are worn, the panel has dropped or rear accident damage has distorted the body opening. The MOT result may be the same if it cannot secure closed, but the repair is not.

A boot stuck shut and a boot that will not stay shut are different problems

One concerns the ability to secure the panel closed. The other can become an examination-access problem. Keeping those rules separate prevents misleading advice such as saying every stuck boot is automatically a Major MOT defect.

Tester Checks

What Does the MOT Tester Check Around the Boot and Tailgate?

The boot is not examined in isolation. The tester can encounter several separate MOT items around the rear of the vehicle, including tailgate security, body condition, corrosion, rear lamps and the registration plate.

Closure

Can It Secure Closed?

The main boot-specific body rule is whether the bootlid or tailgate can be secured in the closed position.

Access

Can It Be Opened if Required?

If the tester cannot open a boot or tailgate designed to be readily opened and that prevents proper examination, the test can be refused or may need to be abandoned.

Body Security

Is Anything Loose?

Rear body panels and components are checked for insecurity and damage that could cause injury or create a detachment risk.

Corrosion

Is the Rear Structure Sound?

Corrosion is assessed according to the affected structure, component security and whether relevant safety-related areas have been weakened.

Rear Lighting

Are Lamps Still Correct?

Rear impact or tailgate damage can affect tail lamps, stop lamps, fog lamps or registration-plate lamps, which are assessed under their own lighting criteria.

Registration Plate

Is the Plate Secure and Visible?

Damage around the tailgate or rear panel can affect registration-plate mounting, visibility and illumination, creating a separate MOT issue.

One rear-impact repair can create several separate MOT defects

A rear collision can move the striker, distort the tailgate opening, crack a lamp, damage number-plate wiring and leave a sharp body edge. Each condition should be assessed separately instead of being described as one generic “boot failure”.

Check operation before MOT day

Open the boot, close it normally, confirm the latch holds securely and inspect the panel gaps, hinges, lights, registration plate and surrounding bodywork. Finding an alignment or latch problem before the test usually makes diagnosis much easier.

Latch & Security

Will a Boot That Will Not Latch Fail an MOT?

If the boot or tailgate closes against the body but the latch does not hold it securely in the closed position, the important MOT issue is panel security. A tailgate that cannot remain properly secured is a Major defect.

The latch itself is only one possible cause. A boot can fail to secure because the latch is worn, the striker is displaced, the panel has dropped on its hinges or previous rear-body damage has moved the opening out of alignment.

Latch Fault

Catch Does Not Engage

The latch mechanism may be seized, worn, damaged or stuck in the wrong position, preventing it from locking around the striker correctly.

Alignment Fault

Striker Is Out of Position

If the striker has moved or the panel no longer meets it squarely, the latch may touch the striker without locking fully.

Body Fault

Tailgate Has Dropped

Hinge wear, loose mounting points or distorted rear bodywork can lower or twist the tailgate enough to prevent proper engagement.

Do not rely on slamming the boot harder

A tailgate that only catches after repeated heavy slamming needs inspection. Excessive force can hide an alignment problem temporarily and may further damage the latch, striker or surrounding panel.

Mechanic check: separate latch failure from body alignment

With the tailgate open, inspect the latch and striker condition first. Then compare panel gaps and closing position. A correctly working latch will still struggle if the tailgate is approaching the striker at the wrong height or angle.

Access Problem

Can a Boot That Is Stuck Shut Fail an MOT?

A boot being stuck closed is not the same defect as a boot that cannot be secured closed. If it is securely shut, the latch-security requirement may not be the immediate problem.

The separate issue is access. If the tester needs to open a boot or tailgate in order to examine part of the vehicle properly and the opening mechanism does not work, the test may not be able to continue normally.

Secure Closed

Latch May Still Be Holding

A tailgate can be mechanically secure even though the handle, release switch or actuator will not open it.

Access

Tester May Need Entry

If required areas cannot be examined because the tailgate will not open, the problem becomes one of test access rather than simply latch security.

Diagnosis

Release System Needs Checking

Mechanical cables, exterior handles, microswitches, actuators, central locking and wiring can all leave a tailgate permanently locked.

Stuck shut does not automatically mean Major

The Major boot-specific defect concerns a panel that cannot be secured closed. A boot that is securely closed but cannot be opened needs a different assessment, particularly whether the fault prevents the tester from completing the required examination.

Repair the access fault before MOT day

Even where the tailgate is securely closed, resolving a failed release before the test avoids the risk of access preventing part of the inspection from being completed.

Intermittent Security

Can a Boot That Opens Unexpectedly Fail an MOT?

Yes, if the reason it opens is that the latch cannot secure the tailgate reliably in the closed position. An intermittent latch is still a security fault even if it sometimes appears to close correctly.

This type of fault deserves more attention than a simple dashboard “boot open” warning because actual panel movement means the mechanical security of the tailgate may be compromised.

Worn Mechanism

Latch Releases Under Movement

Internal wear can allow a latch to engage only partially and release again when the body flexes or the vehicle travels over bumps.

Alignment

Only Partially Engaging

A displaced striker or dropped tailgate can leave the catch sitting on the edge of its normal locking position rather than fully engaged.

Release System

Actuator or Switch Fault

An electrical release fault can also command the latch unexpectedly, particularly where moisture, damaged wiring or a faulty switch is involved.

Do not drive normally with a tailgate that can release by itself

An unsecured rear panel can open while moving and expose luggage or other contents to the road. The fault should be diagnosed and repaired before normal use.

Test the actual mechanical hold

Closing the boot and seeing the dashboard warning disappear is not enough. Confirm that the latch has fully engaged and that the panel remains secure when gentle outward pressure is applied.

Handle & Release Faults

Can a Broken Boot Handle or Electric Release Fail an MOT?

A broken handle, release switch or actuator is not automatically a standalone MOT failure. What matters is what the fault prevents the tailgate from doing.

The outcome changes if the fault means the boot cannot be secured closed, or if the tester cannot open it when access is needed to inspect the vehicle properly.

Mechanical Handle

Handle Broken

A broken exterior handle can leave the latch itself perfectly secure but prevent normal opening of the boot.

Electrical Release

Switch Does Nothing

A failed microswitch, actuator, fuse, power supply or broken wire can stop an electrically released tailgate from opening.

Central Locking

Boot Remains Locked

If other doors respond normally but the boot remains locked, the fault may be local to the tailgate actuator, wiring or latch mechanism.

Diagnose the function that has been lost

A failed release system and a failed securing system are not the same thing. One stops the boot opening; the other stops it remaining safely closed. The repair path and MOT consequence can therefore be different.

Check all available release methods

Depending on the vehicle, test the exterior switch, key or remote, dashboard release and any approved internal emergency mechanism before condemning the latch itself.

Hinge & Alignment

Can Damaged Boot or Tailgate Hinges Fail an MOT?

Hinge wear does not become an MOT failure simply because there is a small amount of age-related movement. The important question is whether the hinge condition affects panel security, closing alignment or the risk of the tailgate becoming insecure.

A badly dropped tailgate can also create a secondary latch problem because the catch no longer meets the striker in the correct position.

Wear

Hinge Pin or Joint Play

Wear in the hinge can allow the outer edge of the tailgate to move vertically and alter the way the latch approaches the striker.

Mounting

Loose Hinge Fixing

Loose bolts or damaged mounting points can let the entire hinge shift, producing poor panel gaps and unreliable closure.

Structural Damage

Roof or Rear Aperture Distortion

Previous impact damage can move the hinge mounting area itself, making adjustment impossible until the underlying body alignment is corrected.

Severe hinge movement can be more than an alignment problem

If a hinge, mounting point or surrounding body structure is insecure enough that the panel may detach, the problem becomes a serious body security issue rather than merely a difficult-to-close boot.

Mechanic check: lift the tailgate gently at the outer edge

Excessive vertical movement can help separate hinge wear from a simple striker-adjustment problem. Also compare the panel gaps on both sides for evidence that the tailgate is sitting low or twisted.

Tailgate Support

Can Weak Boot Struts Fail an MOT?

Weak gas struts are not automatically the specific Major defect that applies when a tailgate cannot be secured closed. Their normal job is to support the tailgate when it is open rather than secure it when closed.

They are still worth repairing. A heavy tailgate that falls unexpectedly can injure someone loading the vehicle and can place extra strain on hinges, wiring and trim.

Weak

Tailgate Slowly Drops

Loss of gas pressure can allow the boot to descend gradually instead of remaining fully open.

Failed

Tailgate Will Not Stay Up

Fully worn struts may provide almost no support, leaving the entire tailgate weight to be held by hand.

Mounting

Ball Joint or Bracket Loose

Sometimes the problem is not gas pressure but a loose mounting point, damaged socket or deteriorated bracket.

Never work beneath an unsupported tailgate

If the struts cannot hold the panel reliably, support the tailgate correctly before inspecting or replacing them. A large hatch or estate tailgate can be heavy enough to cause injury if it drops.

Replace struts as a matched pair where appropriate

On vehicles using two gas struts, replacing only one badly weakened unit can leave uneven support. Check both sides, their mountings and the manufacturer's replacement arrangement.

Keep strut condition separate from latch security

A tailgate can have weak struts yet latch perfectly when closed, or have strong struts but a failed latch that will not secure it. These are different faults and should be diagnosed separately.

Rear Body Condition

Can Rear Accident Damage Around the Boot Fail an MOT?

Yes, depending on what the damage has actually affected. A rear impact can create several different MOT problems around the boot area without the latch itself being the only defect.

The important distinction is whether the damage has made a body component insecure, hazardous, likely to detach, structurally weakened or responsible for another separate testable fault.

Tailgate Security

Latch No Longer Aligns

Rear impact can move the striker or distort the opening so the tailgate no longer secures correctly.

Panel Security

Rear Panel Becomes Loose

Broken fixings, torn mounting points or badly repaired bodywork can leave trim or panels insecure.

Alignment

Uneven Boot Gaps

Unequal panel gaps can suggest hinge movement, striker displacement or deeper rear-body distortion after an impact.

Lighting

Lamp Position or Housing Damaged

Rear-body movement can crack a lamp, loosen its mounting or alter its position enough to create a separate lighting defect.

Registration Plate

Plate Mounting Distorted

A damaged boot panel or rear bumper area can move, obscure or weaken the registration-plate mounting.

Boot Floor

Hidden Damage Beneath Trim

A vehicle can look acceptable externally while the boot floor, rear panel or surrounding structure remains distorted from a previous impact.

Do not judge rear accident damage from the tailgate skin alone

A replacement or repaired tailgate can hide misalignment in the striker, hinges, boot aperture, rear panel or floor. If the gaps are uneven or the latch needs excessive force, inspect the surrounding structure as well as the moving panel.

Mechanic check: compare both sides of the rear opening

Look at hinge position, lamp fit, panel gaps, striker alignment and the shape of the boot floor. Several small inconsistencies together can be more revealing than one visible dent.

Injury Risk

Can Sharp Edges Around the Boot Fail an MOT?

Yes. Rear-body damage can create a separate MOT issue if broken metal, plastic or another body component is positioned or damaged in a way that could injure someone who comes into contact with it.

This is different from the boot-latch rule. A tailgate may close and latch correctly but still have unsafe body damage elsewhere around the rear of the vehicle.

Broken Metal

Jagged Panel Edge

Torn or folded metal after a collision can leave an exposed edge that presents an injury risk.

Broken Trim

Cracked Plastic Protrusion

Damaged trim or lamp housings can also become hazardous where sharp fragments remain exposed.

Loose Component

Panel May Detach

A component that is not securely attached can create an even more serious risk if it may come away from the vehicle while driving.

Temporary tape is not a substitute for secure body repair

Covering a sharp edge may reduce immediate contact risk, but loose or structurally damaged bodywork still needs proper assessment. The repair should leave the component secure and the dangerous edge removed.

Check both the edge and the attachment

A broken panel can create two separate concerns at the same time: whether its edge is hazardous and whether the panel itself remains securely attached to the vehicle.

Corrosion

Can Rust Around the Boot or Tailgate Fail an MOT?

Yes, but the significance depends on where the corrosion is and what it has weakened. Surface rust on a cosmetic panel is different from corrosion that affects the latch mounting, hinge area, rear structure or another safety-related attachment.

This is why a blanket rule such as “boot rust fails MOT” is too vague. The effect on security and structural integrity matters more than the presence of rust alone.

Cosmetic

Surface Corrosion

Light surface rust on a non-structural outer panel does not automatically create an MOT failure.

Latch Area

Striker Mounting Corroded

Rust becomes more significant if it weakens the metal supporting the latch or striker and affects how securely the tailgate closes.

Hinge Area

Hinge Mounting Weakened

Corrosion around hinge mountings can affect panel alignment, security and the ability of the tailgate to remain properly attached.

Boot Floor

Rear Structure Corroded

Corrosion in the boot floor or rear structural area needs assessment for depth, spread and the function of the metal being weakened.

Perforation

Hole With Sharp Edges

Severe corrosion can also create jagged edges or loose metal, producing a separate body-condition concern.

Repair Quality

Previous Patch Repair

Poorly repaired rust around the rear aperture can hide continued corrosion or leave the latch and hinge areas insufficiently supported.

Corrosion near the latch or hinge deserves more attention than cosmetic rust

If weakened metal affects the component that keeps the tailgate closed or attached to the body, the issue moves beyond appearance and becomes one of security and structural condition.

Separate Lighting Checks

Can Boot or Tailgate Damage Cause a Rear-Light MOT Failure?

Yes. A rear impact can damage lamps, mountings and wiring even when the boot itself still opens and closes.

The important point is that the light is assessed under the relevant lighting requirement. It is not automatically recorded as a boot-latch failure simply because the damage started at the tailgate or rear panel.

Tail Lamp

Housing Cracked or Loose

Impact can crack the lamp body, loosen its mounting or allow water into the unit.

Stop Lamp

Brake Light Affected

Tailgate wiring or rear-panel damage can interrupt the stop-lamp circuit or damage the lamp itself.

High-Level Lamp

Tailgate-Mounted Brake Light

Many hatchbacks and estates carry the high-level brake lamp in the tailgate, making wiring and impact damage particularly relevant.

Fog Lamp

Rear Fog Light Damage

Rear impact can damage the lamp or wiring serving the required rear fog light.

Number Plate Lamp

Tailgate Wiring Failure

On many vehicles the number plate lamps are built directly into the tailgate or boot lid and share its wiring loom.

Reflector

Rear Reflector Damaged

Rear-body impact can also crack, cover or displace reflectors that are inspected separately from the boot mechanism.

One broken tailgate wire can create several rear electrical symptoms

If the number plate lamps, high-level brake light, rear wiper and boot release fail together, inspect the wiring where it flexes between the body and the tailgate before replacing several separate components.

Tailgate Electrical Faults

Can Broken Tailgate Wiring Cause MOT Problems?

Yes, indirectly. Tailgate wiring often supplies several components that can have their own MOT relevance.

Hatchbacks and estates are especially prone to loom damage where the wires repeatedly flex inside the rubber conduit between the body and the opening tailgate.

Boot Release

Latch Actuator Stops Working

Broken wiring can prevent an electric tailgate release or locking actuator from operating correctly.

Plate Lamps

Number Plate Lights Go Out

Tailgate loom faults commonly interrupt power or earth to registration plate lamps.

High-Level Brake Light

Stop Lamp Stops Working

Where the high-level brake lamp is mounted in the tailgate, damaged wiring can disable it or create an intermittent fault.

Rear Wiper

Wiper Fault Appears

The rear wiper motor often shares the same flexible loom route and may fail at the same time as the boot release or lamps.

Demister

Heated Rear Window Fails

Damaged wiring or connections can also affect the heated rear screen on vehicles where its supply passes through the tailgate.

Intermittent Fault

Works Only at Certain Angles

If a lamp or release works only when the tailgate is partly open, a fractured wire in the flexible loom becomes particularly likely.

Do not replace several components before inspecting the shared wiring

Multiple tailgate systems failing together often point towards a common power, earth or loom problem rather than several unrelated component failures.

Mechanic check: flex the loom carefully while monitoring the fault

With the vehicle safely stationary, inspect the rubber conduit and wiring near the hinge area for cracking, stretched conductors or previous repair. Intermittent operation as the tailgate moves can help localise the fault.

Pre-MOT Inspection

What Should You Check Around the Boot Before an MOT?

A useful pre-MOT inspection should do more than confirm that the boot lid moves up and down. The important checks are security, alignment, access, body condition and the rear electrical items that can be affected by the tailgate.

Start with the boot closed, then open it and work through the panel, latch, hinges, surrounding bodywork and rear lights in a logical order.

Check 1

Close the Tailgate Normally

It should close fully without repeated slamming and remain securely latched when gentle outward pressure is applied.

Check 2

Open It Again

Confirm the normal handle, switch or release operates and that the tester is unlikely to be prevented from accessing the boot area.

Check 3

Inspect the Latch and Striker

Look for wear, looseness, damaged fixings or witness marks showing that the latch is meeting the striker at the wrong height or angle.

Check 4

Compare Panel Gaps

Uneven gaps can indicate hinge wear, striker movement or previous rear impact damage affecting the body opening.

Check 5

Look for Sharp or Loose Damage

Inspect broken trim, torn metal, insecure panels and damaged lamp housings around the rear bodywork.

Check 6

Inspect Rust Around Mountings

Pay particular attention to the latch area, striker support, hinge mountings, rear panel and boot floor rather than judging surface rust alone.

Check 7

Test Rear Lamps

Confirm the tail lamps, brake lamps, rear fog lamp and number plate lamps still operate correctly after any tailgate or rear-body damage.

Check 8

Check the Registration Plate

Make sure the plate is secure, clearly visible and correctly illuminated where required.

Check 9

Test Tailgate Wiring Functions

If several rear electrical items fail together, inspect the flexible wiring loom near the tailgate hinge before replacing individual parts.

If the boot will not remain securely closed, repair it before test day

That is the clearest boot-specific Major defect on this page. Do not rely on rope, tape, repeated slamming or another temporary restraint to make an insecure tailgate appear acceptable.

Check the panel gaps before adjusting the striker

Moving the striker can sometimes make a dropped or distorted tailgate latch temporarily, but it may also hide the actual cause. If the panel sits unevenly, inspect the hinges and body alignment first.

Mechanic Diagnosis

How Should a Boot or Tailgate Fault Be Diagnosed?

The most useful diagnostic process separates the symptom from the failed part. “Boot will not shut” does not automatically mean “replace the latch”.

A good inspection works from panel position → latch engagement → release mechanism → hinges → body alignment → wiring.

Step 1

Observe the Closing Path

Watch how the tailgate approaches the body. If it sits low, twisted or off-centre, diagnose alignment before adjusting the latch.

Step 2

Inspect Latch Engagement

Confirm the latch mechanism moves freely and actually reaches its fully secured position around the striker.

Step 3

Check the Striker

Look for looseness, impact marks, displaced mounting position or body distortion around its fixing point.

Step 4

Check Hinge Movement

Excessive vertical or sideways movement can indicate hinge wear, loosened mounting points or body damage above the opening.

Step 5

Test Release Inputs

Where fitted, test the handle, microswitch, remote release, central locking actuator and relevant electrical supply.

Step 6

Inspect Rear-Impact Evidence

Check boot-floor shape, seam sealer, paint finish, lamp fit and panel gaps where poor alignment suggests previous accident repair.

Step 7

Inspect the Tailgate Loom

Multiple electrical faults around the tailgate often justify checking the flexible wiring section before replacing switches or lamps.

Step 8

Recheck Security

After adjustment or repair, close the boot repeatedly and confirm the latch remains fully engaged each time.

Step 9

Recheck Connected MOT Items

Confirm rear lamps, number plate lighting and surrounding bodywork have not been left with separate faults after the repair.

Adjustment should follow diagnosis

A striker can sometimes be moved enough to make a damaged tailgate latch. That does not prove the surrounding bodywork is correctly aligned. Diagnose why the relationship changed before relying on adjustment alone.

Rear-impact distortion can make repeated latch replacement pointless

If the tailgate opening or hinge mounting has moved, fitting another latch may not cure poor engagement. Body alignment needs to be corrected before the closure system can operate normally.

Before the MOT

What Boot or Tailgate Faults Should Be Repaired Before the MOT?

Prioritise faults that affect closure security, panel attachment, safe body condition or other testable rear equipment.

Repair

Tailgate Will Not Stay Shut

Repair the latch, striker, hinge or alignment fault so the boot can be secured correctly before test.

Repair

Boot Cannot Be Opened

Restore normal access where a failed handle, actuator or wiring fault could prevent the tester examining the vehicle properly.

Repair

Loose or Dangerous Bodywork

Secure damaged panels and remove hazardous edges or detachment risks around the rear of the vehicle.

Repair

Corroded Latch or Hinge Area

Repair corrosion where weakened metal affects the security of the tailgate or another relevant structural area.

Repair

Rear Lamp Fault

Restore damaged or inoperative lamps and any tailgate wiring responsible for the failure.

Repair

Registration Plate Fault

Make sure the rear plate and its illumination are secure and compliant after any boot or rear-body repair.

Cosmetic dents can wait if they genuinely remain cosmetic

Not every damaged boot skin needs replacing for MOT purposes. If the panel remains secure, has no hazardous edge, closes correctly and does not affect another testable item, appearance alone is a different question.

MOT Retest

What Happens After a Boot or Tailgate MOT Failure?

If the vehicle fails because the bootlid or tailgate cannot be secured closed, repair the fault and arrange the appropriate retest.

Boot lids and tailgates are particularly useful to understand because current retest rules include them among the items that can qualify for a free next-working-day partial retest.

Next Working Day

Same Test Centre

Free Partial Retest Possible

If the vehicle is taken away, repaired and returned to the same test centre before the end of the next working day for an eligible bootlid or tailgate defect, no additional retest fee is charged.

Left for Repair

Retest Within 10 Working Days

No Retest Fee

If the vehicle remains at the test centre for repair and is retested within 10 working days, only a partial retest is needed and there is no retest fee.

Taken Away

Return Within 10 Working Days

Partial Fee Can Apply

If the car is taken elsewhere for repair and returned to the same test centre later within 10 working days, a partial retest is still possible but a partial fee can be charged.

Tailgate-related failures can include more than one retest item

The current next-working-day free-return list also includes items such as lamps, registration plates, electrical wiring and sharp edges or projections. The exact retest depends on what the vehicle actually failed on and whether the return conditions are met.

A remaining MOT does not make an insecure tailgate roadworthy

If the boot cannot stay securely closed or damaged bodywork presents a serious safety risk, repair the defect before using the vehicle normally.

Used-Car Buying

Should You Buy a Used Car With Boot or Tailgate Damage?

A damaged boot does not automatically make a used car a poor purchase. The important question is whether you are looking at a simple closure repair or evidence of a larger rear-impact or corrosion problem.

Treat poor tailgate alignment as evidence to investigate, especially where several rear-body details do not line up correctly.

Panel Gaps

Compare Left and Right

Unequal gaps around the tailgate can indicate poor adjustment, hinge movement or previous rear-body distortion.

Closing Action

Does It Need Slamming?

Excessive force to close the boot can indicate latch wear or a tailgate that no longer meets the striker correctly.

Lift the Carpet

Look for distortion, creases, welding, unusual seam sealer or paint differences that may reveal previous rear impact repair.

Rear Lamps

Compare Lamp Fit

One lamp sitting differently from the other can be another clue that the rear quarter or panel alignment has changed.

Water Ingress

Check for Dampness

Poor tailgate alignment, damaged seals or previous accident repair can allow water into the boot and create electrical or corrosion problems.

Wiring

Test Every Tailgate Function

Check the lock, rear wiper, heated glass, high-level brake light and number plate lamps where those systems run through the tailgate.

Hinges

Look for Paint Disturbance

Tool marks, disturbed bolts or mismatched paint around hinge mountings can indicate previous adjustment or replacement.

Corrosion

Inspect the Rear Aperture

Rust around the striker, hinges and boot floor can turn an inexpensive latch problem into a more serious body-repair decision.

MOT History

Check Previous Rear-Body Defects

Repeat corrosion, lighting or body-security issues in the MOT history can help reveal problems that have been patched rather than properly resolved.

“It only needs a boot latch” should be proved, not assumed

If the panel gaps are uneven, the boot floor is creased or the rear lamps do not fit correctly, allow for the possibility that the latch problem is a symptom of previous accident damage.

A simple latch fault can still be a good buying opportunity

If the rear structure is straight, panel gaps are even and diagnosis confirms only a worn latch or actuator, the repair may be relatively straightforward and easy to price into the purchase.

Frequently Asked Questions

Boot & Tailgate MOT FAQs

Can a boot fail an MOT?

Yes. A bootlid, tailgate, loading door or access panel that cannot be secured in the closed position is a Major MOT defect. Other rear-body faults can also affect the result if damage is insecure, likely to cause injury, likely to detach or affects separate testable items such as lights or the registration plate.

Will a boot that will not shut fail an MOT?

Yes. If the boot or tailgate cannot be secured in the closed position, the current MOT classification is Major and the vehicle fails the test.

Will a boot that shuts but does not latch securely fail an MOT?

Yes. The important requirement is that the bootlid or tailgate can be secured in the closed position. If the latch does not hold the panel securely closed, the defect can be classified as Major.

Can a boot that is stuck shut fail an MOT?

A boot being stuck shut is not automatically the same defect as a boot that cannot be secured closed. However, if the tester cannot open a bootlid or tailgate that is designed to be readily opened and this prevents a proper examination, the test can be refused or may have to be abandoned.

Can a broken boot handle fail an MOT?

A broken handle is not automatically an MOT failure by itself. The outcome depends on whether the boot or tailgate can still be opened where required, can be secured in the closed position and does not create another safety defect.

Can an electric boot release fault fail an MOT?

An electric release fault is not automatically a standalone MOT failure. It becomes important if the tailgate cannot be secured closed or if the tester cannot open it when access is required to complete the examination.

Can a boot that opens unexpectedly fail an MOT?

If the boot or tailgate cannot remain securely latched in the closed position, it can be a Major MOT defect. A panel that can open because the latch does not secure it properly should be repaired before the test.

Can damaged boot hinges fail an MOT?

Damaged or insecure hinges can affect the result if they prevent the tailgate from being secured closed, make a body component insecure or create a risk that the panel could detach or cause injury. Minor wear that does not affect security is not automatically a failure.

Can weak boot struts fail an MOT?

Weak gas struts are not automatically a specific boot-lid MOT failure. They should still be replaced if the tailgate drops unexpectedly because that creates a practical safety risk when the boot is being used.

Can accident damage to the boot fail an MOT?

Yes, depending on the damage. Accident damage can cause an MOT failure if the tailgate cannot be secured closed, a body component is insecure or likely to cause injury, a panel is likely to become detached or another testable item such as a rear lamp or registration plate is affected.

Can sharp edges around the boot fail an MOT?

Yes. A damaged or corroded body panel or component that is likely to cause injury when grazed or contacted is a Major defect. A component likely to become detached is classified as Dangerous.

Can rust around the boot fail an MOT?

Yes, depending on location and severity. Corrosion can affect the MOT if it makes a body component insecure or dangerous, weakens relevant structure or affects the security of the boot latch, hinge area or another safety-related component.

Can boot damage cause a rear light MOT failure?

Yes. Boot or rear-impact damage can damage, obscure or misalign rear lamps. The lamp is then assessed under the relevant lighting rules rather than being treated simply as a boot defect.

Can boot damage affect the rear number plate MOT?

Yes. Rear damage can affect the registration plate, its mounting or its illumination. Those are separate MOT items and should be checked whenever the tailgate or rear panel has been damaged.

Can broken tailgate wiring affect the MOT?

Yes, indirectly. Wiring through a hatchback or estate tailgate can supply number plate lamps, rear lighting, locks, wipers and other equipment. If broken wiring causes a separate testable item to stop working, that item can affect the MOT result.

What should I check before the MOT if the boot is faulty?

Check that the boot or tailgate opens where required, closes fully, latches securely and does not move unexpectedly. Inspect the hinges, striker, body damage, sharp edges, corrosion, rear lamps, number plate, number plate lights and visible wiring around the tailgate.

Can you drive with a boot that will not latch?

A boot or tailgate that cannot remain securely closed should be repaired before normal driving. An unsecured tailgate can open unexpectedly, allow luggage to escape and create a hazard for the vehicle and other road users.

Should you buy a used car with boot or tailgate damage?

Boot or tailgate damage is not automatically a reason to reject a used car, but inspect panel gaps, latch alignment, hinges, boot floor, rear lights, number plate area, water ingress and signs of previous accident repair. Poor alignment or recurring latch problems can indicate more extensive rear-body damage.
