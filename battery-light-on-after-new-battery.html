Quick answer

Why is the battery light on after fitting a new battery?

If the battery warning light stays on after a new battery has been fitted, the battery itself may never have been the main problem. The warning is primarily telling you that the vehicle has detected a charging-system problem.

Common causes include a weak or failed alternator, alternator regulator fault, loose battery terminal, poor engine or body earth, damaged charging cable, blown fusible link, slipping or damaged auxiliary belt, battery monitoring sensor fault, incorrect battery specification or a battery replacement that requires registration on a vehicle equipped with battery energy management.

Battery starts the engine

A fresh battery may make the car start perfectly because it contains plenty of stored energy. That does not prove the charging system is working.

Alternator replenishes it

Once the engine is running, the charging system should supply the vehicle's electrical demand and replenish energy removed from the battery during starting.

The warning needs diagnosis

If the warning remains on, test the complete charging circuit rather than assuming that either the new battery or alternator must automatically be replaced.

Mechanic insight:

A new battery can temporarily disguise a charging fault because it arrives with stored energy. If the alternator, belt drive, wiring or control system cannot replenish that energy, the new battery will gradually discharge and the car can eventually fail to restart.

Best first principle:

Do not diagnose this fault from the age of the battery. Diagnose it from what the charging system is actually doing.

Understand the warning

Why a new battery may not fix the battery warning light

Replacing a weak battery can improve starting immediately, but it does not repair the system responsible for charging that battery. This is why a car can start strongly with a new battery yet still display the battery warning light.

Think of the battery and charging system as two connected parts of the same electrical system. The battery provides stored electrical energy for starting and supports the vehicle when required. Once the engine is running, the alternator and charging-control system must supply electrical demand and restore energy used during starting.

Old battery was genuinely weak

The original battery may have reached the end of its life, but another charging-system problem can exist at the same time. Replacing one weak component does not automatically prove the rest of the system is healthy.

Battery was the symptom

A weak alternator, poor connection or charging fault may have repeatedly left the old battery undercharged. In that situation, replacing the battery treats the consequence rather than the underlying cause.

Something changed during fitting

A terminal, earth connection, battery sensor plug or nearby cable may have been disturbed during replacement. Always check installation-related faults before condemning expensive parts.

Car starts well but light stays on

A strong start mainly tells you that the new battery currently has enough stored energy. It does not confirm that the alternator is replenishing it correctly.

Car was fine briefly, then battery went flat

This pattern makes a charging fault particularly important to investigate. The new battery may simply have been supplying the car until its stored charge was depleted.

Useful diagnostic question:

Why did the previous battery become discharged or fail? Age and normal deterioration may be the answer, but if the vehicle has an unresolved charging fault, fitting another battery can start the same cycle again.

Battery replacement is not a charging-system test:

A new battery can make the engine crank faster and temporarily remove low-voltage symptoms. The charging circuit still needs to be tested if the warning light remains illuminated.

Driving safety

Is it safe to drive with the battery light on after a new battery?

Do not assume the car is safe to keep driving simply because the battery is new. If the warning is caused by loss of charging, the vehicle may be operating from energy stored in the battery. As that energy falls, electrical systems can begin shutting down and the engine may eventually stop or fail to restart.

The exact risk depends on the fault. A poor connection or control problem may behave differently from a failed alternator or broken auxiliary belt, so the warning needs diagnosing rather than judging it only by how normally the car appears to drive.

Warning light only

If the car still drives normally but the battery warning remains on, arrange charging-system diagnosis promptly. Normal driving behaviour does not prove that the battery is being recharged.

Electrical symptoms appearing

Dimming lights, flickering instruments, slow wipers, warning messages or multiple electrical faults can indicate falling system voltage. Find a safe place to stop rather than trying to complete a long journey.

Belt problem or overheating

Stop the engine safely if the battery warning appears with overheating, unusual belt noise, a burning smell or evidence that the auxiliary belt has failed. On some engines the same belt system can drive other important components.

  • ! Avoid unnecessary electrical loads if you suspect the alternator is no longer charging.
  • ! Do not repeatedly switch the engine off and restart it just to see whether the warning disappears.
  • ! Treat dimming lights, flickering displays or several warning lights appearing together as signs that system voltage may be deteriorating.
  • ! If steering assistance, engine operation or other essential systems begin behaving abnormally, stop in a safe location.
  • ! If the auxiliary belt is visibly damaged or missing, do not continue driving until the belt system and affected components have been checked.
Important:

There is no reliable mileage or time limit for how long a vehicle will continue running without effective charging. Battery state, electrical load, vehicle design and the severity of the fault all affect how quickly voltage falls.

If the engine is already switched off:

A vehicle with an unresolved charging fault may not restart. Repeated starting attempts can use the remaining battery energy very quickly.

Fault finder

Common causes of a battery light after fitting a new battery

The warning does not identify one failed component. It tells you that the vehicle has detected a problem associated with charging or electrical-system operation. Diagnosis should therefore move from simple installation checks to alternator, wiring and control-system testing.

1. Alternator not charging

A failed alternator can leave the vehicle running from battery power alone. A new battery may temporarily make the car appear fixed until its stored charge begins falling.

Alternator not charging signs →

2. Alternator regulator fault

The regulator helps control alternator output. A regulator or alternator-control fault can produce insufficient, unstable or inappropriate charging even when the alternator itself still generates some electrical output.

3. Loose battery terminal

A terminal clamp that is loose, contaminated or not fully seated can create resistance and cause starting, charging or voltage problems immediately after battery replacement.

4. Poor engine or body earth

The charging circuit needs a sound return path. Corroded, damaged or loose earth straps can cause voltage drop, poor charging and apparently unrelated electrical symptoms.

5. Auxiliary belt problem

If the belt driving the alternator is loose, slipping, damaged or missing, alternator speed may be insufficient or the alternator may stop turning completely.

6. Belt tensioner or pulley fault

A worn tensioner, overrunning alternator pulley or other belt drive fault can cause slip, vibration or poor alternator drive even when the belt itself appears present.

7. Charging cable resistance

The alternator may generate current correctly but a damaged, loose or corroded high-current cable can prevent that energy reaching the battery efficiently.

8. Fuse or fusible-link fault

Some charging circuits contain high-current fuses or fusible links. A fault here can interrupt the charging path even when the alternator and battery themselves are serviceable.

9. Battery monitoring sensor

Many modern vehicles monitor battery current, voltage and temperature through a sensor fitted near a battery terminal. A disconnected, damaged or incorrectly fitted sensor can affect charging-system operation.

10. Incorrect battery specification

Capacity, technology and vehicle requirements matter. Cars designed for AGM or EFB batteries, especially those with stop-start and energy management, should receive the correct replacement specification.

11. Battery registration not completed

Some vehicles require a replacement battery to be registered or adapted in the battery-management system. Whether this is required depends on the specific vehicle and charging strategy.

12. Faulty or discharged new battery

New does not guarantee fully charged or fault-free. A battery can be discharged through storage, incorrect charging or a vehicle fault, and a defective new battery is possible even though it should not be assumed first.

Do not replace the alternator from the warning light alone:

Alternator failure is an important possibility, but a loose terminal, poor earth, belt-drive fault, damaged charging cable or control-system problem can create similar symptoms. Test the circuit before condemning the alternator.

Symptom-to-cause diagnosis

What the symptoms can tell you

The battery warning light is more useful when it is considered alongside what happened before and after the battery was replaced. The patterns below do not prove a component has failed, but they can tell you where diagnosis should start.

Light came on immediately after battery replacement

Start with anything disturbed during the job: battery clamps, earth connections, battery sensor plugs, battery specification and any required battery-management procedure. Then verify the charging system.

New battery worked for days, then went flat

Investigate whether the battery is being recharged correctly. Alternator output, belt drive, charging cables and voltage drop become important. If charging proves healthy, investigate excessive key-off electrical drain separately.

Battery light gets brighter or appears at idle

Check charging performance at different engine speeds and loads, but also inspect belt condition, tensioner operation, alternator control and electrical connections. Do not diagnose from warning brightness alone.

Battery light appears with squealing

Inspect the auxiliary belt system promptly. Belt slip, poor tension, pulley problems or contamination may prevent the alternator being driven correctly.

Battery light plus dim headlights

Falling system voltage becomes more concerning. Charging output, belt drive and the high-current charging circuit should be checked before the battery is allowed to discharge further.

Battery light plus several warning lights

Low system voltage can cause multiple control modules to report faults. Diagnose supply voltage first before assuming that every warning represents a separate failed component.

Light comes and goes over bumps

Intermittent terminals, earths, wiring or connectors deserve attention. A visual inspection alone may not reveal excessive resistance, so connection and voltage-drop testing may still be required.

Charging seems normal but warning stays on

Do not stop diagnosis at one voltage measurement. Modern charging systems may require scan data, battery-sensor checks, alternator communication testing and confirmation that the correct battery has been registered where applicable.

First diagnostic checks

What to check first when the battery light stays on

Start with anything that may have been disturbed during battery replacement before assuming the alternator has failed. A loose terminal, disconnected battery sensor or poor earth can create charging problems immediately after fitting a new battery.

The aim is to work from simple physical checks towards electrical measurements. Do not replace another component until the charging fault has been demonstrated.

1. Confirm the warning

Switch the ignition on without starting the engine. The battery warning light should normally illuminate as part of the lamp check. Start the engine and observe whether it goes out, stays illuminated or returns intermittently.

2. Recheck both battery terminals

Make sure the positive and negative clamps are correctly seated, clean and secure. A terminal should not rotate freely on the battery post when correctly tightened.

3. Inspect the earth path

Check the negative battery connection to the body and the main engine or gearbox earth strap. Look for looseness, corrosion, broken strands, overheating or damaged mounting points.

4. Inspect the auxiliary belt

Confirm that the belt is present and driving the alternator. Look for cracking, contamination, obvious slack, abnormal movement, glazing or signs of belt and pulley damage.

5. Check plugs and battery sensors

Inspect electrical connectors disturbed during battery replacement. On vehicles with a battery monitoring sensor, check that the sensor and its small electrical connector have not been damaged or left disconnected.

6. Confirm the battery specification

Check battery technology, capacity, physical fitment and terminal arrangement against the vehicle requirement. Stop-start and energy-managed vehicles may require AGM or EFB technology rather than a conventional flooded battery.

7. Check battery condition

A new battery can still be undercharged. Establish its state of charge and condition before using charging-system measurements to condemn another component.

8. Measure charging behaviour

Test the system with the engine running and observe how it responds to engine speed, electrical load and vehicle operating conditions. The correct interpretation depends on the charging system fitted.

Start where the work was done:

If the warning appeared immediately after battery replacement, installation-related checks deserve priority. If the warning existed before the battery was replaced, investigate why the original battery became discharged or weak.

Keep clear of moving components:

Charging tests may require the engine to run. Keep hands, clothing, test leads and tools clear of belts, pulleys, fans and other moving or hot components. If you cannot test the system safely, use a competent garage or auto electrician.

Electrical testing

How to interpret charging voltage correctly

Voltage testing is useful, but one multimeter reading should not be treated as a complete alternator diagnosis. Older conventional charging systems and modern computer-controlled charging systems do not always behave in the same way.

The important question is whether the charging system can maintain the battery and support the vehicle's electrical demand under the conditions commanded by the vehicle.

Engine off

Resting battery voltage helps establish state of charge before testing begins. A substantially discharged battery can distort the rest of the diagnosis and may need charging before reliable conclusions can be made.

Engine running

On a conventional system, voltage would normally rise above the battery's resting level once the alternator begins charging. The exact value depends on system design, temperature, battery condition and electrical load.

Smart charging

On an energy-managed vehicle, charging voltage may deliberately rise and fall. A reading that appears unusually low or high in isolation is not enough to condemn the alternator.

Why a single voltage number can mislead:

Temperature, battery state of charge, electrical demand and vehicle energy-management strategy can all change alternator output. Diagnosis should consider the system design and operating conditions, not just compare one reading with one universal number.

Do not forget voltage-drop testing

An alternator can generate electrical power while resistance in the cables or earth path prevents that power reaching the battery correctly. This is why measuring only across the battery terminals can leave part of the story unexplained.

Positive-side voltage drop

Testing between the alternator output and battery positive side while current is flowing can reveal excessive resistance in charging cables, terminals, connections, fuses or fusible links.

Earth-side voltage drop

Testing the ground path can expose resistance between the alternator or engine, vehicle body and battery negative terminal. A poor earth can imitate alternator or battery problems.

Mechanic-style rule:

Measure the charging source, measure what reaches the battery and measure what is being lost through the circuit. That is more useful than replacing an alternator because one battery-terminal reading looked suspicious.

Modern charging systems

Why smart charging changes the diagnosis

Many modern vehicles no longer operate the alternator as a simple fixed-output charging source. Engine management, battery monitoring and energy-management systems can influence how much charging is requested at different times.

The system may consider battery state of charge, temperature, electrical demand, engine operating conditions and other vehicle strategies. Alternator output can therefore change deliberately during normal driving.

Battery monitoring

A battery sensor may monitor current, voltage and temperature so the control system can estimate battery condition and state of charge.

Controlled alternator output

The vehicle may increase or reduce alternator demand according to operating conditions rather than maintaining one constant charging voltage at all times.

Energy management

If battery state becomes poor, some vehicles can manage electrical loads or disable convenience functions to protect starting ability and essential systems.

Why this matters after battery replacement

On an energy-managed vehicle, fitting the wrong battery technology, disturbing the battery sensor or failing to complete a required battery replacement procedure can affect how the charging system behaves. That does not mean every vehicle requires coding, and it does not mean coding fixes a genuine alternator or wiring fault.

Traditional charging system

Diagnosis relies heavily on battery condition, alternator output, regulator operation, belt drive and circuit integrity. Charging behaviour is generally more predictable.

Smart charging system

The same physical checks still matter, but scan data, battery sensor information, alternator communication and commanded charging behaviour may also be needed.

Do not condemn a smart alternator from one idle reading:

If the vehicle deliberately reduces alternator output, a basic voltage test can be misinterpreted. Confirm whether the vehicle is requesting charge and whether the alternator is responding as expected.

Useful scan-data comparison:

Where the vehicle provides suitable data, compare battery voltage, battery state information, charging request and actual alternator behaviour. A fault becomes much easier to prove when commanded and actual operation can be compared.

Correct replacement battery

Does AGM, EFB or battery type matter?

Yes. A replacement battery should meet the specification required by the vehicle. Physical size and terminal position are not the only considerations. Battery technology, capacity and compatibility with the vehicle's charging strategy also matter.

Conventional flooded battery

Common on vehicles without demanding stop-start or energy- management requirements. It should only be used where the vehicle specification allows it.

EFB battery

Enhanced Flooded Batteries are designed for greater cycling demands than a basic flooded battery and are used on some stop-start applications.

AGM battery

Absorbent Glass Mat batteries tolerate demanding cycling and electrical loads and are commonly specified on more advanced stop-start and energy-managed vehicles.

Problems caused by fitting the wrong battery

  • ✓ The battery may not tolerate the vehicle's intended charging and cycling demands.
  • ✓ Stop-start operation may be reduced or unavailable.
  • ✓ Battery life can be shortened if the replacement technology is unsuitable for the application.
  • ✓ Energy-management behaviour may not match what the vehicle expects from the fitted battery.
  • ✓ An incorrect capacity or specification can complicate diagnosis of an existing charging problem.
Before blaming the new battery:

Confirm the replacement against the vehicle's required battery specification. Do not assume that a battery is correct simply because it physically fits the tray and starts the engine.

Battery management

Does a new battery need coding or registration?

Some vehicles require a battery replacement procedure after a new battery is fitted. Depending on the manufacturer, this may be described as battery registration, battery adaptation, battery reset or coding.

The purpose is generally to tell the vehicle's energy-management system that a replacement battery has been installed and, on some vehicles, confirm information such as battery technology or capacity. Requirements vary by manufacturer, model, year and electrical-system design.

Registration

Records that a replacement battery has been fitted so the vehicle can manage the new battery appropriately where its strategy requires this information.

Battery specification

Some systems may also need confirmation when battery capacity or technology differs from the previous configuration.

No procedure required

Many vehicles do not require battery registration at all. Always determine the requirement for the specific vehicle rather than applying one rule to every car.

Can missed battery registration cause the warning light?

It can contribute to incorrect battery-management behaviour on vehicles that require a replacement procedure, but it should not be used as a convenient explanation for every battery warning light. A genuine alternator, belt, wiring, connection or sensor fault still needs proper diagnosis.

Registration does not repair hardware:

Coding or registering a battery cannot repair a failed alternator, loose terminal, damaged earth strap, broken belt or high-resistance charging cable. Complete the required procedure where applicable, but continue diagnosis if the warning remains.

Best approach:

Verify whether the specific vehicle requires battery registration, confirm the correct battery has been fitted and then assess the charging system normally. Do not pay for unnecessary coding on a vehicle that does not require it.

Mechanic diagnostic workflow

How a garage should diagnose a battery light after a new battery

A proper diagnosis should establish why the warning is present before another battery, alternator or control component is replaced. The process becomes more reliable when physical inspection, electrical measurements and scan data are considered together.

1. Confirm the complaint

Establish when the warning started, whether it existed before battery replacement and whether it is constant or intermittent. Ask why the original battery was replaced.

2. Verify the replacement battery

Check battery specification, installation, terminal security, state of charge and whether a vehicle-specific registration procedure was required.

3. Inspect the belt drive

Inspect the auxiliary belt, tensioner and relevant pulleys. Confirm that the alternator is being driven correctly without obvious slip, damage or abnormal movement.

4. Inspect the charging circuit

Check alternator connections, battery terminals, high-current cables, earth straps, relevant fuses, fusible links and battery monitoring connections.

5. Test battery condition

Establish battery state of charge and condition. Even a recently fitted battery may need charging before the rest of the system can be assessed reliably.

6. Test charging performance

Observe charging behaviour with the engine running and under suitable electrical load. Interpret the result according to whether the vehicle uses conventional or controlled charging.

7. Perform voltage-drop tests

Test the positive charging path and earth return while current is flowing. Excessive resistance can make a healthy alternator look weak at the battery.

8. Scan relevant control modules

Read charging-system, engine, body and battery-management faults where relevant. Fault codes are clues and should be supported by measurements and live data before parts are condemned.

9. Compare command with actual operation

On smart-charging vehicles, determine what the control system is requesting and whether alternator output and battery-sensor data respond appropriately.

10. Prove the failed part

Only recommend an alternator, wiring repair, sensor, belt-system repair or battery-management procedure after the evidence points to that part of the system.

The most valuable test is the one that proves the fault:

A warning light, fault code or unusual voltage reading can point the diagnosis in the right direction. The repair decision should come from confirming which part of the charging system cannot do its job.

Motor Vehicle Expert diagnostic principle:

Battery light after a new battery does not automatically mean “bad new battery” or “replace the alternator.” Confirm battery condition, alternator drive, charging output, circuit integrity and system control — then repair the fault that the evidence proves.

Repair decision

What actually needs repairing?

The correct repair depends on what testing proves. A battery warning light after battery replacement does not automatically mean the new battery is faulty, and it does not automatically mean the alternator needs replacing.

Start with the cheapest credible faults — installation, connections, belt drive and battery-management requirements — but do not stop there if measurements show a genuine charging-system failure.

Low repair risk

Loose or poor connection

A loose battery clamp, dirty connection or disturbed sensor plug may only require cleaning, securing or repairing the affected connection.

Low–medium repair risk

Earth or charging cable fault

Excessive resistance may require cleaning an earth point, replacing an earth strap, repairing a terminal or replacing a damaged charging cable.

Medium repair risk

Auxiliary belt problem

A worn or slipping belt may need replacement. If the tensioner, idler or alternator pulley is responsible, those components need checking rather than fitting a belt alone.

Medium repair risk

Battery sensor fault

A damaged battery monitoring sensor, connector or associated wiring may require electrical repair or sensor replacement after the fault has been confirmed.

Procedure

Battery registration required

Where the vehicle requires battery registration or adaptation, the correct procedure should be completed and the charging system retested before further parts are condemned.

High repair risk

Alternator failure

If testing proves that the alternator cannot provide the required output despite a sound belt drive, wiring and control system, alternator repair or replacement may be necessary.

Do not approve an alternator from a guess:

Alternators can be expensive and access can add substantial labour. Before replacement, there should be evidence that the alternator or its regulator cannot perform correctly and that the belt drive, charging cables, earth path and control system are not the real cause.

Ask the garage what failed the test:

A useful diagnosis should be explainable. Ask what was measured, whether circuit voltage drop was checked and why the recommended component has been identified as the fault.

UK repair costs

How much does it cost to fix a battery light after a new battery?

The cost depends on what caused the warning. A loose connection or battery-management procedure can be relatively inexpensive, while a difficult-to-access alternator or more involved electrical fault can cost several hundred pounds.

The figures below are broad UK planning ranges rather than quotations. Vehicle make and model, labour rate, alternator design, battery technology, access and the amount of diagnosis required can all change the final bill.

Used car buying

Should you buy a used car with the battery light on?

Treat an illuminated battery warning light as an unresolved fault, not as a minor dashboard annoyance. The repair could be simple, but without diagnosis you do not know whether the car needs a connection repair, belt work, alternator, battery-management diagnosis or more involved electrical work.

Be particularly cautious if the seller says the car has just had a new battery but cannot explain why the warning remains illuminated. A recently fitted battery can temporarily allow a vehicle with a charging fault to start and drive normally.

Lower buying risk

Documented simple fault

A known loose connection or clearly identified minor repair is less concerning if the diagnosis is credible and the price reflects the work still required.

Medium buying risk

New battery but no diagnosis

Ask why the battery was replaced and whether charging-system tests were performed. A battery fitted by trial and error does not establish what the real fault is.

High buying risk

Warning plus electrical symptoms

Dimming lights, repeated flat batteries, starting problems, multiple warnings or obvious belt noise increase the risk of a more significant unresolved charging fault.

Used-car checks before you agree to buy

  • ✓ Confirm that the battery warning light illuminates during the ignition lamp check and goes out correctly after starting.
  • ✓ Ask when and why the battery was replaced and request the invoice if the seller says it is new.
  • ✓ Check whether the correct battery technology and specification has been fitted.
  • ✓ Look at the battery terminals, earth connections and visible charging wiring for poor-quality recent work.
  • ✓ Listen for belt squeal, bearing noise or abnormal sounds from the auxiliary drive.
  • ✓ Operate headlights, heater blower, heated screens and other electrical loads and watch for obvious voltage-related symptoms.
  • ✓ Ask whether battery registration was completed if the specific vehicle requires it.
  • ✓ Arrange an independent charging-system diagnosis if the warning is present or the seller's explanation does not make sense.

New battery repeatedly goes flat High Diagnose charging and parasitic drain before buying
Battery warning plus belt noise or dimming electrics High Do not buy without diagnosis and a realistic repair quote
Seller cannot explain why battery was recently replaced Medium Treat the replacement as a clue, not reassurance
Multiple electrical faults and charging warning High Independent inspection strongly recommended before purchase
Seller says “it only needs an alternator”?

If the repair were already proven and straightforward, ask why it has not been completed before sale. Price the car on the basis of the fault you can verify, not the cheapest explanation offered for the warning light.

Buying rule:

A warning light with a proven diagnosis is a repair decision. A warning light with no diagnosis is an unknown financial risk.

FAQs

Battery Light After New Battery FAQs

Does the battery light mean the alternator is bad?

Not automatically. Alternator or regulator failure is an important possibility, but a loose battery terminal, poor earth, auxiliary belt problem, charging-cable fault, fuse or fusible-link problem, battery sensor fault or charging-control issue can produce similar symptoms. Test the charging system before replacing the alternator.

Can a brand-new battery still be faulty?

Yes. A new battery can be undercharged, incorrectly specified or occasionally defective. However, if the battery warning light remains on, do not assume the new battery is responsible. Check battery condition and then diagnose the complete charging system.

Does a new battery need coding or registration?

Some vehicles require battery registration, adaptation or coding after replacement, particularly where battery energy management is used. Many vehicles do not. Check the requirement for the specific make, model, year and charging system rather than applying one rule to every car.

Can I drive with the battery light on after replacing the battery?

Continuing to drive can be risky if the vehicle is no longer charging correctly. A new battery may keep the engine and electrical systems operating temporarily, but once its stored energy falls the vehicle can develop electrical problems, stop running or fail to restart. Diagnose the warning promptly.

Can a weak alternator damage a new battery?

Repeated undercharging can leave a new battery at a low state of charge and shorten its useful life. Abnormal charging behaviour can also be harmful. If the previous battery failed because of an unresolved charging fault, fitting a new battery without repairing that fault can start the same problem again.

Can the wrong battery type cause charging problems?

Yes. The replacement should meet the vehicle's required specification, including battery technology and capacity. Vehicles designed for AGM or EFB batteries, stop-start operation or battery energy management should receive a compatible replacement rather than a battery chosen only because it physically fits.

Why did the battery light come on immediately after replacement?

Start by checking anything disturbed during the battery change: terminal security, earth connections, battery sensor plugs, battery specification and any required battery-management procedure. If those checks are correct, test the charging system rather than assuming the timing proves the new battery is faulty.

Why does the new battery keep going flat?

The battery may not be receiving enough charge, there may be excessive resistance in the charging circuit, or the vehicle may have an electrical drain while switched off. Test charging performance first and, if that is correct, investigate battery condition and parasitic current draw separately.
