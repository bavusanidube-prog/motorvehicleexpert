UK MOT suspension advisory guide

Suspension Advisory Explained

A suspension advisory means a suspension component has wear, deterioration or another developing condition worth recording, but it had not reached the relevant MOT rejection threshold when the vehicle was tested. The important next step is to identify exactly which component is affected, how much movement or damage exists and whether the problem is already affecting steering, handling, tyre wear or vehicle stability.

Quick answer

What Does a Suspension Advisory Mean?

A suspension advisory means the MOT tester found a condition worth bringing to your attention, but it had not deteriorated enough to justify rejection at the time of the test. The vehicle can therefore pass with an advisory, but the affected part should be monitored and repaired if necessary.

Suspension advisories deserve more attention than the word “advisory” can sometimes suggest. Bushes, joints, springs, shock absorbers, suspension arms and mountings all help control the position and movement of the wheel. If wear progresses, handling, braking stability, tyre contact and steering behaviour can be affected.

What does the advisory tell you?

A developing suspension condition has been identified but it had not reached the applicable MOT rejection threshold when inspected.

Does every suspension advisory mean failure is close?

No. Some defects progress slowly while others can worsen quickly. The component involved, amount of deterioration, vehicle use and any accompanying symptoms determine urgency.

What should you do next?

Identify the exact suspension component, inspect the amount and type of wear, compare the opposite side and check whether tyres, alignment or neighbouring components have also been affected.

Advisory, Minor, Major and Dangerous are not interchangeable

The MOT system distinguishes between advisory items and classified defects. An advisory can be used when deterioration has not reached the point of rejection. A Minor defect still allows a pass, while Major and Dangerous defects cause the MOT to fail.

Mechanic insight

Do not diagnose the repair from the MOT wording alone. “Bush worn”, “joint deteriorated” or “suspension component corroded” describes the observed condition, but a workshop inspection should establish the amount of movement, whether the wear is progressive, whether the opposite side is similar and whether tyres or wheel alignment have already been affected.

A pass does not guarantee the suspension for another year

The MOT records the condition at the time of the inspection. Suspension components continue to experience road loads, pothole impacts, steering movement, braking forces and corrosion after the test. An advised component can therefore deteriorate before the next MOT.

Understanding the advisory

What Does a Suspension MOT Advisory Actually Mean?

A suspension advisory records a developing condition that the MOT tester believes should be brought to the vehicle owner's attention, but which had not deteriorated enough to justify rejection when the vehicle was inspected.

The wording matters because suspension components can deteriorate in several different ways. Rubber can crack or separate, joints can develop movement, springs and arms can corrode, mountings can weaken and shock absorbers can begin to deteriorate. The correct response depends on the exact component and the severity of the condition.

Developing wear

Wear has started

A bush, joint or other suspension component may show deterioration without yet having the excessive movement, insecurity or loss of function required for rejection.

Deterioration

Condition is worsening

Rubber, protective covers, metal components and mountings can deteriorate gradually. The part may still function today while giving clear evidence that future repair is becoming more likely.

Monitor and inspect

It needs a current assessment

An MOT advisory does not tell you exactly how many miles remain. A workshop inspection should establish the present condition and whether deterioration has progressed since the test.

Advisory does not mean “nothing is wrong”

One of the most common mistakes is treating an advisory as though the tester found no defect. The opposite is true: something was observed and recorded. The condition simply had not reached the relevant rejection threshold at that particular inspection.

This distinction is particularly important with suspension because deterioration can alter wheel control gradually. A driver may become accustomed to a small knock, slightly vague steering or slowly increasing tyre wear without noticing how much the vehicle has changed.

Read the exact MOT wording

Do not reduce every suspension advisory to “worn suspension”. Record which axle and side are mentioned, the component involved and whether the wording refers to wear, deterioration, corrosion, play, a dust cover, a spring, a shock absorber or another specific condition. That wording gives the workshop a much better starting point.

Compare the MOT date and mileage

A suspension advisory recorded recently at almost the vehicle's current mileage is more useful than one recorded many months and thousands of miles ago. The older the inspection, the less safely you can assume the component remains in the same condition.

Suspension components

Which Suspension Parts Commonly Receive Advisories?

“Suspension” describes a complete system rather than one component. Depending on the vehicle design, the system can include control arms, bushes, ball joints, anti-roll bars and links, coil springs, shock absorbers, struts, top mountings, subframes and other locating parts.

Identifying the exact component is essential because the consequence of deterioration is different for each part. A noisy anti-roll-bar link is not mechanically equivalent to a suspension joint developing significant movement.

Rubber components

Suspension bushes

Bushes isolate vibration while allowing controlled movement between suspension components. Age, load and movement can cause cracking, splitting, softening or separation from the bonded sleeve.

Articulating joints

Ball joints and suspension joints

These joints allow suspension movement while maintaining wheel location. Wear can produce free play, knocking, steering changes and increasingly poor control of wheel position.

Wheel location

Control arms and suspension arms

Suspension arms connect the wheel assembly to the vehicle structure through bushes and joints. Problems can involve the arm itself, its bushes, integral ball joints, corrosion or mounting points.

Roll control

Anti-roll bars and drop links

Anti-roll-bar links connect the bar to the suspension. Worn link joints and bushes commonly produce rattling or knocking over uneven roads, although the complete suspension should still be checked before blaming the link.

Vehicle support

Coil springs

Springs support vehicle weight and maintain ride height. They can corrode, crack or fracture, particularly around lower spring seats where road contamination and moisture collect.

Damping

Shock absorbers and struts

Dampers control spring movement and help keep the tyre in contact with the road. Leakage, damaged mountings or inadequate damping can reduce suspension control.

Upper mounting

Strut top mounts and bearings

Top mounts locate the upper end of a strut and may incorporate a bearing on a steering axle. Wear can contribute to knocking, creaking, poor steering return or unwanted movement.

Structural mounting

Subframes and suspension mountings

Subframes and mounting structures provide the foundation for suspension components. Corrosion, damage or insecure mounting requires careful assessment because multiple suspension loads can pass through these areas.

Vehicle-specific

Other suspension components

Suspension design varies considerably between vehicles. Multi-link systems, torsion beams, leaf springs, air suspension and other arrangements introduce additional joints, links and mounting points that may also deteriorate.

Diagnose the named component, then inspect the system around it

If the MOT identifies one suspension component, do not stop the inspection there. Check the opposite side, neighbouring joints and bushes, tyre wear, spring condition and wheel movement. One worn component can alter loads elsewhere in the suspension.

Bushes and joints

Worn Suspension Bushes, Ball Joints and Joint Advisories

Bushes and articulating joints are among the most important areas to interpret correctly because the words “worn”, “deteriorated” and “play” can describe very different stages of component life.

Suspension bush deterioration

A suspension bush normally contains rubber or another compliant material bonded or fitted between metal components. Its job is to permit controlled movement while keeping the suspension located and isolating vibration.

Surface deterioration

Age-related surface cracking does not automatically mean the bush has lost control of the suspension. The important question is whether the material still locates the component correctly under load.

Splitting or separation

As deterioration progresses, rubber can split or separate from its sleeve. This can allow greater suspension movement and change wheel geometry during acceleration, braking or cornering.

Excessive movement

Once a bush allows excessive movement at a suspension component, the condition becomes more significant than cosmetic rubber cracking and can meet an MOT rejection criterion.

Ball joints and other suspension joints

Ball joints use a spherical joint to allow movement while locating the wheel assembly. The amount of permitted movement depends on the design, so a technician must distinguish normal movement from wear. Some joints are spring-loaded or designed with characteristics that make simple visual judgement unreliable.

Early stage

Developing joint wear

A joint can begin wearing before it develops movement severe enough for rejection. This is where inspection history and comparison with the manufacturer's design become useful.

MOT concern

Excessive wear

Under the current MOT criteria, excessive wear in a suspension pin, bush, joint or bearing is a Major defect. It should therefore no longer be treated as a routine advisory once the movement is excessive.

Safety critical

Likely to detach

If a suspension component is worn to the extent that it is likely to detach, the defect is Dangerous. Loss of suspension location can result in serious loss of wheel control.

What about ball-joint dust covers?

A dust cover protects the joint from water, grit and road contamination. A damaged cover matters because once contamination reaches the lubricated joint, wear can accelerate even if the joint itself has not yet developed excessive movement.

Minor

Severely deteriorated dust cover

Under the current MOT inspection criteria, a suspension-joint dust cover that is severely deteriorated is classified as a Minor defect.

Major

Missing or ineffective dust cover

If the dust cover is missing or no longer prevents the ingress of dirt, the defect is Major. The joint itself should also be checked carefully for contamination and wear.

Do not replace only the cover without checking the joint

If a protective cover has failed, establish whether contamination has already entered the joint and whether free play has developed. Fitting a new cover to a worn joint does not restore the joint itself.

Knocking does not automatically identify a ball joint

Similar noises can come from anti-roll-bar links, bushes, top mounts, springs, dampers, steering joints and loose components. Reproduce the symptom and physically locate the movement before ordering parts.

Springs and damping

Coil Spring and Shock Absorber Suspension Advisories

Springs and shock absorbers perform different jobs but work together. The spring supports the vehicle and allows suspension movement, while the shock absorber controls that movement. Problems with either can affect ride height, tyre contact and vehicle stability.

Coil spring deterioration

Surface corrosion

Road salt and moisture can attack the spring coating and expose the steel underneath. Surface corrosion should be distinguished from serious weakening, cracking or fracture.

Cracked or fractured spring

Springs commonly fracture near an end coil, where the break may be difficult to see while the vehicle is sitting normally. A fractured spring can alter ride height and may move out of its correct seating.

Incorrect seating

A spring should sit correctly in its mounting and seat. A broken or displaced coil can create noise, affect suspension movement and, in severe cases, threaten nearby components such as the tyre.

A fractured spring is beyond routine advisory territory

Under the current MOT criteria, a spring that is fractured or seriously weakened is a Major defect. If a spring or spring component is insecure to the extent that it is likely to become detached, the classification can become Dangerous.

Shock absorber deterioration

Shock absorbers contain hydraulic fluid and internal valves that control suspension movement. A damper can therefore deteriorate internally even when it still looks reasonably clean externally. Diagnosis should consider leakage, mounting condition, damping performance and comparison across the axle.

Monitor correctly

Slight fluid seepage

A light film or slight seepage should not automatically be called a failed shock absorber. Current MOT guidance specifically states that slight seepage from a shock absorber is not a reason for rejection.

Major

Severe fluid leakage

Severe fluid leakage from a shock absorber is a Major defect. Significant loss of damper fluid can reduce the component's ability to control spring movement.

Major

Negligible damping

A shock absorber providing negligible damping is also a Major defect. A damper can therefore fail because of poor operation even without an obvious external fluid leak.

Symptoms that may point towards spring or damper problems

  • ✓ Vehicle sits lower on one corner.
  • ✓ Knocking or metallic noise over bumps.
  • ✓ Excessive bouncing after road undulations.
  • ✓ Vehicle feels less settled during braking or cornering.
  • ✓ Uneven or unusual tyre wear develops.
  • ✓ Damper body shows significant wet leakage.
  • ✓ Spring appears fractured, displaced or incorrectly seated.
Compare both sides of the axle

Springs and dampers influence vehicle balance. Even where only one side is mentioned on the MOT record, inspect the corresponding component on the opposite side and consider age, condition and manufacturer repair guidance before deciding whether one or both sides should be renewed.

Wear interpretation

Suspension Wear and Deterioration Patterns

The appearance of a worn suspension component often provides clues about why it deteriorated. A good diagnosis therefore looks beyond whether the component is simply “worn” and considers the direction of movement, loading, corrosion, impact damage and condition of neighbouring parts.

Rubber deterioration

Cracked or split bush

Rubber can harden and crack with age. More advanced deterioration may allow the inner sleeve to move excessively or separate from the rubber, reducing control of the suspension arm.

Joint wear

Increasing free play

Wear between a joint's moving surfaces can create progressively greater movement. The result may be knocking, wheel-position changes and eventually excessive wear for MOT purposes.

Contamination

Failed protective cover

Once a joint's protective cover stops excluding dirt and water, lubricant can become contaminated and abrasive material can accelerate internal joint wear.

Corrosion

Spring or component corrosion

Surface corrosion may initially be limited, but progressive corrosion can weaken springs, arms and mounting areas. The location and loss of material matter more than colour alone.

Impact

Bent or damaged component

Potholes, kerb impacts and collision damage can bend suspension parts or disturb their mounting position. This can alter wheel geometry even if the vehicle still drives.

Damping

Damper deterioration

Internal valve wear, fluid loss or mounting deterioration can reduce damping control. Do not judge damper condition from a light external film alone.

Alignment clue

Abnormal tyre wear

Inner-edge wear, feathering or different wear across the axle can indicate geometry problems. Worn suspension is one possible cause, but tyre pressure, alignment settings and steering faults must also be considered.

Repeated load

One side deteriorates faster

Road conditions, repeated pothole impacts, kerb contact, contamination, vehicle loading and previous repairs can cause one side to deteriorate sooner than the other.

Multiple faults

Several worn components together

Older suspension systems may have wear across several bushes, joints and dampers. Replacing only the noisiest component can leave the vehicle with poor geometry or another knock immediately afterwards.

What the wear pattern can tell a mechanic

Normal ageing

Similar deterioration on both sides with no evidence of impact damage may simply reflect age, mileage and repeated suspension movement.

Localised problem

Severe wear on one component or one side can justify checking for impact damage, incorrect installation, seizure, geometry changes or another fault concentrating load in that area.

System-wide wear

Multiple worn components, poor tyre wear and unstable handling may justify a complete suspension and steering inspection rather than replacing parts one at a time.

Do not use noise alone to decide which component to replace

Suspension noise travels through the body and subframe. A knock that sounds as though it comes from one corner can originate from an anti-roll-bar link, bush, joint, spring, top mount, damper, steering component or even a loose non-suspension part. Confirm physical movement before replacing components.

The next step is a symptom-led inspection

After identifying the component and wear pattern, compare the MOT wording with what the driver actually experiences. Knocking, pulling, vague steering, vibration, uneven tyre wear and abnormal ride height help determine whether the advisory is still a monitoring issue or has progressed into a repair priority.

Driver symptoms

Symptoms That Can Accompany a Suspension Advisory

A suspension advisory may exist without any obvious symptom from the driver's seat. Early bush deterioration, developing joint wear or slight component deterioration can be found during an MOT inspection before the vehicle starts knocking, pulling or handling noticeably differently.

When symptoms are present, however, they provide useful diagnostic clues. The important point is to combine the symptom with a physical inspection rather than assuming that every knock, vibration or tyre wear pattern is caused by the component mentioned on the MOT record.

Common symptom

Knocking over bumps

Knocking or rattling over broken road surfaces can come from anti-roll-bar links, bushes, ball joints, top mounts, dampers, springs or loose components. The noise must be physically traced before parts are replaced.

Steering clue

Vague or wandering steering

Excessive movement in suspension bushes or joints can allow wheel geometry to change while driving. The vehicle may feel less precise, require frequent steering corrections or respond inconsistently to road surfaces.

Directional change

Pulling to one side

Pulling can be associated with suspension or geometry problems, but tyre condition, tyre pressure, braking faults, road camber and steering alignment must also be considered before blaming the suspension.

Tyre evidence

Uneven tyre wear

Worn bushes, joints or damaged suspension components can alter wheel geometry and contribute to abnormal tyre wear. Inner-edge wear, feathering or different wear across the axle should prompt checks of both suspension condition and alignment.

Damping clue

Excessive bouncing

A vehicle that continues oscillating after a bump may have poor damping control. Shock absorber condition, springs, tyres and mountings should be assessed rather than relying on a simple bounce test alone.

Cornering

Unsettled handling

Excessive body movement, instability or a vehicle that feels poorly controlled through bends can indicate deteriorated damping or suspension location. Tyres and steering components must be checked at the same time.

Braking behaviour

Vehicle moves or feels unstable under braking

Worn suspension bushes can allow geometry to change as braking loads transfer through the suspension. Brake imbalance, tyre faults and alignment problems can produce similar symptoms, so a combined chassis inspection is required.

Ride height

One corner sits lower

Abnormal ride height can indicate a fractured or weakened spring, incorrect spring installation or another suspension problem. Compare both sides on level ground and inspect the complete spring.

May be silent

No noticeable symptoms

A developing suspension defect can be present without noise or obvious handling changes. Lack of symptoms does not cancel an MOT advisory or replace a physical inspection.

A sudden handling change needs more urgent attention

A new severe pull, major steering instability, sudden change in ride height, loud metallic impact, wheel-position change or loss of vehicle control is not something to monitor until the next MOT. Stop using the vehicle if safe control is in doubt and have the suspension, steering, wheels and tyres inspected.

Noise severity and defect severity are not always the same

A worn link can sometimes make a surprisingly loud knock while a more important suspension joint may deteriorate with relatively little noise. Repair priority should be based on the actual defect, movement and safety consequence rather than noise volume alone.

Workshop inspection

How a Suspension Advisory Should Be Inspected

A useful suspension inspection does more than confirm that the MOT advisory exists. The technician should identify the exact component, reproduce any reported symptom, assess movement under appropriate load and determine whether another fault is causing or accelerating the deterioration.

The inspection method depends on the suspension design. Some joints need to be loaded, unloaded or moved in a particular direction before wear becomes apparent, so vehicle-specific repair information can be important.

Step 1

Read the exact MOT advisory

Confirm the component, axle and side recorded on the MOT history. Note whether the wording refers to deterioration, play, corrosion, a dust cover, spring condition, damping or another specific issue.

Step 2

Ask what the driver has noticed

Establish whether there is knocking, pulling, steering movement, vibration, tyre wear, poor ride control or a change in ride height. Ask when the symptom occurs rather than accepting a general description such as “suspension noise”.

Step 3

Inspect tyres and ride height

Compare tyre pressures, tread condition and wear patterns before concentrating on individual suspension parts. Check whether the vehicle sits evenly and look for evidence of a fractured or displaced spring.

Step 4

Check bushes and mounting points

Inspect bushes for splitting, separation and excessive movement. Check suspension arms, subframes and mounting areas for damage, insecurity and significant corrosion.

Step 5

Check joints for movement

Assess ball joints, suspension joints and bearings using the correct loading method for the vehicle. Distinguish designed movement from genuine wear and inspect protective covers at the same time.

Step 6

Inspect springs and dampers

Examine the complete spring where accessible, including the end coils and seats. Check shock absorbers for significant leakage, damaged mountings and evidence of poor damping.

Step 7

Compare the opposite side

Suspension components work as part of an axle and chassis system. Compare the same component on the opposite side for movement, deterioration, ride height and general condition.

Step 8

Confirm the root cause

Decide whether the advisory represents normal progressive wear or whether impact damage, corrosion, poor geometry, incorrect parts, previous repairs or another worn component is contributing to the problem.

Step 9

Decide the repair priority

Classify the component as suitable for monitoring, due for planned replacement or requiring prompt repair. If deterioration has progressed beyond advisory condition, treat the present defect rather than the wording on the old MOT certificate.

Step 10

Recheck after repair

After replacement, confirm fastener security, component movement, ride height and road behaviour. Check whether wheel alignment is required because suspension work can alter or disturb geometry.

What should be recorded after the inspection?

  • ✓ Exact component and side affected.
  • ✓ Amount and direction of abnormal movement where relevant.
  • ✓ Condition of bushes, joints and protective covers.
  • ✓ Spring condition and seating.
  • ✓ Shock absorber leakage, mounting and damping condition.
  • ✓ Tyre wear and any geometry concerns.
  • ✓ Condition of the corresponding component on the opposite side.
  • ✓ Whether the defect should be monitored, repaired soon or treated as urgent.
Ask for a specific diagnosis, not just “suspension worn”

A useful garage report should identify the component and why it needs attention. “Nearside front lower arm rear bush split with excessive movement” is far more useful than a vague recommendation to replace the front suspension.

Why suspension wears

What Causes Suspension Components to Wear or Deteriorate?

Suspension parts operate under repeated vertical, braking, cornering and steering loads. Normal ageing is therefore unavoidable, but road conditions, vehicle use, corrosion and previous repairs can significantly alter how quickly individual components deteriorate.

Normal wear

Age and mileage

Bushes flex repeatedly, joints articulate and dampers operate on every journey. Rubber hardens, lubricated joints wear and internal damper components deteriorate as mileage and age increase.

Road conditions

Potholes and broken surfaces

Repeated impacts increase loads through tyres, wheels, joints, bushes, springs and dampers. A severe pothole strike can also bend components or disturb wheel alignment.

Impact damage

Kerb strikes

Hitting a kerb can damage a wheel or tyre, bend suspension or steering components and alter geometry. Localised wear after an impact deserves closer investigation.

Environment

Water and road salt

Moisture and winter road contamination can attack springs, suspension arms, subframes and mounting areas. Corrosion can also make fasteners difficult to remove during later repairs.

Vehicle loading

Heavy loads and towing

Regular heavy loading increases the forces carried by springs, dampers, bushes and joints. Vehicles frequently used for towing or carrying heavy loads deserve careful rear-suspension inspection.

Protective failure

Damaged joint covers

A failed dust cover can allow water and abrasive contamination into a joint. Once lubricant is contaminated or lost, internal wear can accelerate rapidly.

Geometry

Incorrect wheel alignment

Incorrect geometry can contribute to tyre wear and poor handling. However, alignment can also be the result of worn or damaged suspension, so the mechanical condition should be checked before repeatedly adjusting geometry.

Previous work

Incorrect installation

Incorrect parts, poor-quality components, unsuitable tightening procedures or incorrect bush installation can shorten component life and create abnormal movement or noise.

Connected wear

Another worn component

Suspension works as a system. Excessive movement in one bush, joint or mounting can change loading elsewhere, so replacing only the first visible worn part may not resolve the complete problem.

Alignment is not a substitute for repairing worn suspension

If a wheel changes position because a bush or joint has excessive movement, an alignment adjustment cannot make that component mechanically sound. Repair the wear first, then measure and correct wheel geometry where required.

Diagnostic workflow

Mechanic-Style Suspension Advisory Diagnosis

The best way to approach a suspension advisory is to move from the recorded MOT observation to the vehicle's current mechanical condition. This prevents unnecessary parts replacement and, more importantly, reduces the risk of treating a safety-relevant defect as though it were still only an old advisory.

Stage 1

Confirm

Read the advisory, confirm the component and side, compare the MOT mileage with the current mileage and establish whether any symptoms have developed since the test.

Stage 2

Reproduce

Where safe, reproduce the reported knock, pull, instability or vibration. Note whether it occurs during bumps, braking, acceleration, steering input or constant-speed driving.

Stage 3

Inspect

Examine the named component and the surrounding suspension, steering, tyres and mountings. Compare both sides and look for evidence of impact, corrosion or previous repair.

Stage 4

Load and test

Check joints and bushes using the correct loading method for the suspension design. Assess movement, security, protective covers, spring seating and damper condition.

Stage 5

Find the cause

Determine whether the condition is normal wear or whether another problem such as impact damage, failed protection, geometry error, corrosion or incorrect previous work is accelerating it.

Stage 6

Classify the risk

Decide whether the vehicle is suitable for monitored use, should be booked for repair soon or should not continue in normal use until the suspension fault has been corrected.

Three useful diagnostic outcomes

Monitor

Developing deterioration only

The component remains secure and functional, movement is not excessive, no serious symptoms are present and inspection confirms that planned monitoring is appropriate.

Repair

Replacement should be planned

Wear is clearly progressing, symptoms are developing, tyre wear is being affected or the component is approaching the point where continued use is likely to create additional repair cost or MOT risk.

Urgent

Defect has progressed significantly

Excessive movement, serious weakening, insecurity, fractured components, negligible damping or another safety-relevant defect means the old advisory should no longer determine the repair priority.

Diagnose today's vehicle, not last month's MOT certificate

An advisory describes what the tester found on the test date. If mileage has increased, symptoms have appeared or a component has deteriorated further, the current condition takes priority over the historical MOT classification.

Check wheel geometry after the mechanical repair where appropriate

Replacing control arms, bushes, joints, struts or other locating components can alter wheel geometry. Once the suspension is mechanically sound, alignment should be checked when the repair or vehicle design makes it appropriate.

Driving safety

Can You Drive With a Suspension MOT Advisory?

A suspension advisory does not automatically mean the vehicle must be taken off the road. At the MOT inspection, the condition had not reached the relevant rejection threshold. That does not establish how long the component will remain acceptable or guarantee that it is unchanged today.

Whether continued driving is sensible depends on the component, present amount of wear, rate of deterioration, mileage since the MOT and whether the vehicle now has symptoms affecting steering, stability, tyre contact or suspension security.

Suspension advisory severity guide

Lower immediate risk

Monitor after inspection

The advisory is recent, a current inspection confirms only developing deterioration, the component remains secure and functional, there is no excessive movement and the vehicle has no concerning handling symptoms.

  • ✓ Record the current condition.
  • ✓ Monitor for progression.
  • ✓ Recheck at servicing or an appropriate inspection interval.
Increasing risk

Arrange repair soon

Wear is clearly progressing, knocking is increasing, tyre wear is developing, the vehicle feels less precise or the component is approaching the point where continued deterioration could affect safety or create additional repair cost.

  • ✓ Limit unnecessary mileage.
  • ✓ Avoid delaying until the next MOT.
  • ✓ Obtain a proper repair diagnosis and quote.
Urgent

Do not rely on the advisory

A sudden handling change, excessive joint movement, serious component damage, fractured or insecure suspension, major ride height change or another condition threatening wheel control requires urgent professional assessment.

  • ✓ Stop normal use if vehicle control is uncertain.
  • ✓ Do not wait for another MOT.
  • ✓ Arrange recovery if driving cannot be done safely.

Have the vehicle checked promptly if you notice:

  • ✓ A new or rapidly worsening suspension knock.
  • ✓ Steering that suddenly feels vague, unstable or unpredictable.
  • ✓ A significant new pull or change in wheel position.
  • ✓ One corner of the vehicle sitting noticeably lower.
  • ✓ A visible broken, displaced or incorrectly seated spring.
  • ✓ Severe shock absorber leakage or clearly uncontrolled bouncing.
  • ✓ Rapid or severe abnormal tyre wear.
  • ✓ Instability during braking, cornering or over road undulations.
  • ✓ Evidence that a suspension component or mounting is insecure.
There is no universal safe mileage after a suspension advisory

One vehicle may have minor deterioration that changes very slowly, while another may have a joint, bush or spring condition that worsens rapidly because of mileage, road impacts, contamination or another fault. A statement such as “it should be fine for another 5,000 miles” is not reliable without inspecting the actual vehicle.

A valid MOT is not a suspension guarantee until the next test

The MOT records the vehicle's condition against the inspection requirements at the time it is tested. Components continue to wear afterwards, and the responsibility to keep the vehicle roadworthy continues between MOT tests.

Use the advisory as an early maintenance opportunity

Investigating developing suspension wear before it becomes severe can help prevent secondary tyre wear, poor wheel geometry, damaged neighbouring components and a more expensive repair later.

Current MOT rules

Suspension Advisories and Current UK MOT Rules

Suspension defects are assessed according to their actual condition when the vehicle is tested. The MOT does not reject a component simply because it is old or because slight movement exists. The tester must determine whether wear, deterioration, insecurity, fracture, corrosion or loss of function has reached the relevant rejection threshold.

This is particularly important with rubber suspension bushes because some designs intentionally allow considerable movement. Current MOT guidance tells testers to reject rubber or synthetic bushes only when serious deterioration of the bonding or flexible material can be seen.

Advisory does not equal MOT failure

A developing suspension condition can be recorded as an advisory when it is worth bringing to the driver's attention but has not deteriorated enough to justify rejection. Once the defect reaches a Minor, Major or Dangerous classification, the official MOT criteria determine the result.

A suspension pin, bush, joint or bearing that is excessively worn is classified as a Major defect under the current MOT inspection criteria.

Suspension-joint dust covers

Minor

Dust cover severely deteriorated

A suspension-joint dust cover that is severely deteriorated is classified as Minor. A Minor defect does not itself cause the vehicle to fail the MOT.

Cover missing or no longer excludes dirt

Joint protection lost

If a suspension-joint dust cover is missing or no longer prevents dirt from entering the joint, the current classification is Major.

Check the joint itself as well as the cover

A damaged protective cover can allow road contamination into a lubricated joint. Replacing the cover is only appropriate if the joint itself remains serviceable. If excessive wear has already developed, the joint requires repair or replacement as well.

Springs

Major

Spring insecurely attached

MOT Result: FAIL
Suspension security

A spring that is insecurely attached to the chassis or axle is a Major defect.

Major

Spring fractured or seriously weakened

MOT Result: FAIL

A fractured or seriously weakened spring or spring component is classified as Major.

Dangerous

Severe spring insecurity

MOT Result: FAIL
Serious safety risk

Where spring fixings are loose to the extent that relative movement is visible, the defect is Dangerous. Certain missing or failed spring conditions can also become Dangerous where directional control is affected.

Shock absorbers

Not automatically a failure

Slight shock absorber seepage

MOT Result: Not a rejection reason alone
Inspect condition

Current MOT guidance specifically states that slight seepage causing a film of fluid on a shock absorber is not a reason for rejection.

Damper repair required

A shock absorber damaged to the extent that it does not function, or one showing signs of severe leakage, is a Major defect.

MOT Result: FAIL
Suspension control reduced

A shock absorber that provides negligible damping is classified as Major even if a dramatic external fluid leak is not visible.

Arms, rods, struts, subframes and anti-roll bars

Major

Component insecure

A suspension component that is insecurely attached to the chassis or axle is a Major defect.

Major

Excessively damaged or corroded

A suspension arm, rod, strut, subframe, anti-roll bar or related component that is excessively damaged or corroded is classified as Major.

Dangerous

Fractured or likely to fail

A suspension component that is fractured or likely to fail is Dangerous. A component that is missing, likely to become detached or causing impaired directional stability can also receive a Dangerous classification.

Air, gas and fluid suspension

Major

Obvious system leak

An obvious leak from a gas, air or fluid suspension system is classified as Major.

Major

Function adversely affected

A damaged, modified or deteriorated air, gas or fluid suspension component is Major where the condition adversely affects system operation.

Dangerous

System inoperative or seriously affected

An inoperative gas, air or fluid suspension system is Dangerous, as is deterioration that seriously affects the function of the system.

Suspension mounting areas and structural strength

The MOT inspection also considers the strength and continuity of the vehicle structure around spring, subframe and suspension component mountings. Significant weakening within a prescribed suspension mounting area can therefore cause an MOT failure even when the replaceable suspension component itself is not the primary problem.

Major

Structural strength significantly reduced

Significant reduction in the strength or continuity of the load-bearing structure within the prescribed area around a suspension mounting is a Major defect.

Dangerous

Structure severely weakened

If the structure is weakened to the extent that control of the vehicle is likely to be adversely affected, the defect is Dangerous.

Do not wait for an advisory to become a failure

MOT rejection limits are legal inspection thresholds, not ideal maintenance targets. If a component is clearly deteriorating, causing symptoms or damaging tyres, there is no benefit in waiting until it crosses the Major or Dangerous threshold before repairing it.

Repair decision

Should You Replace a Suspension Component After an Advisory?

Not every suspension advisory requires immediate replacement, but the decision should be based on the component's current mechanical condition rather than the fact that the vehicle passed its MOT.

The correct approach is to establish how much deterioration exists, whether the component remains secure, whether abnormal movement is present and whether the defect is affecting tyres, steering, geometry, braking stability or neighbouring suspension parts.

Monitor

Keep under inspection

Monitoring may be appropriate where a current inspection confirms only early deterioration, there is no excessive movement, the component remains secure and no associated symptoms or tyre wear are present.

Planned reinspection
Replace

Repair developing wear

Replacement becomes sensible when deterioration is progressing, knocking has developed, geometry is changing, tyre wear is occurring or the component is clearly approaching the end of its useful life.

Plan repair soon
Urgent

Repair significant defects

Excessive movement, fracture, serious weakening, insecurity, severe damper failure or a component likely to detach should not be treated as something to monitor until the next MOT.

Immediate priority

Should suspension parts be replaced in pairs?

It depends on the component, vehicle design, age and manufacturer instructions. Some suspension components are commonly renewed in axle pairs because matched condition can help maintain predictable vehicle behaviour, while other components can be replaced individually when the opposite side remains serviceable.

Shock absorbers

Dampers on the same axle are often considered together because a significant difference in damping condition can affect vehicle balance. Follow vehicle and component manufacturer guidance.

Coil springs

When one spring has failed through age or corrosion, compare the opposite spring carefully. Pair replacement may be sensible to maintain comparable ride height and spring characteristics.

Arms, bushes and joints

These do not automatically require pair replacement in every case. Inspect both sides and base the decision on condition, design, manufacturer procedure and the economics of the repair.

Fix the cause, not only the advised component

If a suspension arm has been damaged by impact, a joint has worn after its cover failed or a tyre has been damaged by poor geometry, deal with the connected problem as part of the repair. Replacing one component without investigating why it failed can leave the vehicle with the same underlying issue.

Geometry and tyres

Suspension Advisories, Wheel Alignment and Tyre Wear

Suspension components control where the wheel sits relative to the vehicle. When a locating bush, ball joint, arm, strut or mounting develops excessive movement or becomes distorted, wheel geometry can change while the vehicle is moving.

This is why suspension advisories and uneven tyre wear often appear together. However, tyre wear alone does not prove that a suspension component is faulty. Tyre pressure, wheel alignment, steering wear, wheel damage and driving conditions must also be considered.

Inner-edge wear

Geometry may be incorrect

Heavy wear on one tread edge can indicate excessive camber, toe error, worn suspension location or impact damage. Inspect the chassis mechanically before relying on alignment adjustment.

Feathering

Check toe and suspension movement

Feathered tread can develop when a tyre scrubs sideways as it rolls. Incorrect toe is one possible cause, but worn bushes or joints can allow toe to change dynamically.

Different side-to-side wear

Compare both corners

A large difference between tyres on the same axle can justify checking pressures, alignment, suspension wear, previous impact damage and whether components differ between sides.

Repair the suspension before final alignment

If a bush, arm or joint is allowing the wheel to move excessively, carrying out wheel alignment first can produce misleading results. The geometry may appear correct while stationary and then change under braking or cornering as the worn component moves.

Step 1

Mechanical inspection

Check tyres, wheel bearings, steering joints, suspension bushes, ball joints, arms and mountings before adjusting geometry.

Step 2

Repair worn components

Replace or repair components that cannot locate the wheel correctly. Confirm fasteners and bushes are installed using the correct procedure.

Step 3

Measure alignment

Once the suspension is mechanically sound, measure wheel geometry and adjust the available settings according to vehicle specification.

New tyres will not fix worn suspension

If suspension movement is causing abnormal tyre wear, fitting new tyres without correcting the mechanical fault can damage the new tread in the same way. Diagnose suspension and alignment before assuming the tyres were the original problem.

Wheel alignment itself is not the same as suspension condition

Alignment describes wheel angles. Suspension condition describes the mechanical components that maintain those angles. A vehicle can have incorrect alignment without a worn suspension component, and a vehicle with worn suspension can produce changing geometry that cannot be corrected properly until the wear is repaired.

DIY vs garage

Should You Repair Suspension Yourself or Use a Garage?

Suspension work ranges from relatively straightforward link replacement to jobs involving heavily loaded springs, structural mountings, seized fasteners and components that directly locate the wheel. The consequences of incorrect assembly can therefore be much greater than the apparent simplicity of the replacement part.

Experienced DIY

Suitable only with the right knowledge and equipment

  • ✓ Vehicle can be supported safely at approved lifting points.
  • ✓ Correct vehicle-specific repair information is available.
  • ✓ Correct replacement parts and fasteners are identified.
  • ✓ Torque specifications and tightening procedures are known.
  • ✓ Bush loading position can be set correctly where required.
  • ✓ The repair can be inspected before the vehicle returns to use.
Garage recommended

Professional repair is preferable when...

  • ✓ Coil springs require compression or specialist equipment.
  • ✓ A suspension joint has excessive or uncertain movement.
  • ✓ Structural corrosion or mounting damage is present.
  • ✓ Fasteners are heavily corroded or seized.
  • ✓ Pressed-in bushes or bearings require workshop equipment.
  • ✓ Air or hydraulic suspension requires diagnosis or depressurising.
  • ✓ Alignment must be measured after the repair.
  • ✓ You cannot confidently verify that wheel location is safe.
Coil springs can store dangerous amounts of energy

A compressed coil spring can release considerable force if handled incorrectly. Strut dismantling and spring compression should not be attempted without suitable equipment, correct positioning and the competence to use it safely.

Suspension fastener tightening can be position-sensitive

Some bonded rubber suspension bushes must be tightened at the specified normal ride position rather than with the suspension hanging. Incorrect tightening can preload the rubber and shorten bush life. Always follow the vehicle manufacturer's procedure.

Do not guess alignment after a suspension repair

Where the repaired component affects wheel location, use suitable alignment equipment rather than relying on steering-wheel position or a short road test alone.

Next MOT risk

Will a Suspension Advisory Fail the Next MOT?

Not automatically. A suspension advisory records the condition found at the previous MOT inspection. The vehicle's next MOT result will depend on how that component has changed by the time it is tested again.

Suspension components are continually exposed to vehicle weight, steering movement, braking forces, cornering loads, potholes, vibration, water and road salt. A bush, joint, spring, damper or mounting that showed developing deterioration at one MOT can therefore reach a rejection threshold before the next annual test.

Lower next-MOT risk

Advisory inspected or repaired

If the advised component has been professionally inspected, repaired where necessary and any related geometry or tyre issue has been corrected, the previous advisory itself does not cause the next MOT to fail.

Best preparation
Increasing risk

Advisory left unresolved

If the same worn component remains in service for another year, further deterioration can turn developing wear into excessive movement, insecurity, fracture or another rejectable defect.

Reinspect before MOT
High risk

Symptoms have appeared

Increasing knocking, poor steering control, severe tyre wear, abnormal ride height or obvious suspension deterioration suggests that the condition has progressed and should be diagnosed before the next MOT.

Do not delay diagnosis

How an advisory can become an MOT failure

Bush or joint wear progresses

Developing wear can progress until a suspension pin, bush, joint or bearing becomes excessively worn and meets the Major defect threshold.

Corrosion or damage becomes severe

A suspension component that was previously showing limited deterioration can become excessively damaged, corroded, weakened or fractured.

Damper performance deteriorates

A shock absorber may progress from an early condition to severe leakage, damage that prevents correct operation or negligible damping.

Spring condition worsens

Corrosion or deterioration can progress until a spring becomes fractured, seriously weakened, displaced or insecure.

Protective cover fails completely

A deteriorating joint dust cover can eventually stop protecting the joint from dirt and water, while contamination can accelerate wear inside the joint itself.

Related faults develop

Suspension wear can contribute to poor geometry and abnormal tyre wear, creating additional repair work and potentially separate MOT defects.

Do not use the next MOT as the repair deadline

The correct time to repair a suspension component is determined by its mechanical condition and safety implications, not by the expiry date of the MOT certificate. If deterioration has progressed, repair it when necessary rather than trying to make it last until the next test.

Arrange a pre-MOT suspension check if an old advisory remains

If a suspension advisory from the previous MOT has never been inspected or repaired, checking it before the next test gives you time to identify parts, compare quotes, arrange alignment and deal with any associated tyre wear without the pressure of an MOT failure.

Used car buying

Should You Buy a Used Car With a Suspension Advisory?

A suspension advisory is not automatically a reason to reject a used car. Many suspension components are normal wear items and a single developing bush, link or joint can be straightforward to repair. What matters is the exact component, its current condition and whether the MOT history suggests continuing neglect.

The risk becomes greater when several suspension advisories appear together, the same advisory is repeated across multiple MOT tests, tyres are wearing abnormally or the vehicle knocks, pulls, wanders or sits unevenly during inspection.

Manageable

Normal suspension wear

A clearly identified wear item with no serious symptoms can be manageable if the repair cost is known and appropriately reflected in the purchase price.

Inspect and price
Negotiate

Several components are due

Arms, joints, springs, dampers, alignment and tyres can add up quickly. Obtain a realistic repair estimate before deciding what the vehicle is worth.

Allow for full repair cost
Investigate carefully

Repeated or abnormal suspension wear

Repeated advisories, severe tyre wear, obvious impact damage, corrosion or several worn suspension components can point towards long-term neglect or a more expensive chassis problem.

Independent inspection recommended

Suspension advisory used-car checklist

  • ✓ Read the complete MOT history rather than only the latest test.
  • ✓ Check whether the same suspension advisory appears repeatedly.
  • ✓ Ask whether the advised component has since been repaired.
  • ✓ Look for invoices confirming suspension replacement and wheel alignment.
  • ✓ Inspect all four tyres for uneven or inner-edge wear.
  • ✓ Listen for knocking, rattling, creaking or metallic noises during the test drive.
  • ✓ Check whether the steering feels stable and returns consistently.
  • ✓ Check whether the vehicle pulls or wanders on a suitable road.
  • ✓ Compare ride height from side to side on level ground.
  • ✓ Look for related spring, shock absorber, tyre or corrosion advisories.
  • ✓ Obtain a repair quote before negotiating the purchase price.
Repeated MOT advisories tell a useful story

One suspension advisory does not necessarily indicate poor vehicle maintenance. The same defect appearing repeatedly over several MOT tests without evidence of repair is more significant because it suggests the component may have remained in service while continuing to deteriorate.

A fresh MOT does not mean the suspension needs no money spending

A used vehicle can pass its MOT while still carrying developing suspension advisories. Budget according to the actual condition of the vehicle rather than assuming that a new MOT certificate means there will be no suspension expenditure during ownership.

Frequently Asked Questions

Suspension Advisory FAQs

These answers cover the questions UK drivers most commonly ask after seeing a suspension advisory on an MOT certificate or MOT history.

What does a suspension advisory mean?

A suspension advisory means the MOT tester identified wear, deterioration or another developing suspension condition worth recording, but the defect had not reached the relevant MOT rejection threshold when the vehicle was tested.

Is a suspension advisory serious?

It can be. Some suspension advisories represent early deterioration that can be monitored, while others involve components that can affect wheel location, steering, tyre wear and vehicle stability as the condition worsens. The exact component and current amount of wear determine the urgency.

Can I drive with a suspension advisory?

You may be able to continue driving if the vehicle remains roadworthy and a current inspection confirms the component is still serviceable. There is no universal safe mileage after a suspension advisory, and new knocking, instability, severe pulling, abnormal ride height or rapid tyre wear requires prompt investigation.

Will a suspension advisory fail the next MOT?

Not automatically. The next MOT result depends on the condition of the suspension when the vehicle is tested again. Continued wear can turn a previous advisory into a Minor, Major or Dangerous defect depending on the component and severity.

Can a worn suspension bush fail an MOT?

Yes. Under the current MOT criteria, a suspension pin, bush, joint or bearing that is excessively worn is a Major defect. Rubber or synthetic bushes must be assessed according to their design because some movement can be normal.

Can a worn ball joint fail an MOT?

Yes. Excessive wear in a suspension joint is a Major defect. If the joint is worn to the extent that it is likely to become detached, the defect is classified as Dangerous.

Can a damaged ball-joint dust cover fail an MOT?

It depends on the condition. A suspension-joint dust cover that is severely deteriorated is a Minor defect, while one that is missing or no longer prevents dirt from entering the joint is a Major defect.

Can a broken coil spring fail an MOT?

Yes. A fractured or seriously weakened spring or spring component is a Major defect under the current MOT criteria. Severe insecurity or a condition that threatens vehicle control can receive a more serious classification.

Does a leaking shock absorber fail an MOT?

Severe shock absorber leakage is a Major defect, but slight seepage that only leaves a film of fluid is not by itself a reason for rejection. A shock absorber with negligible damping or damage that prevents correct operation is also a Major defect.

What causes suspension knocking over bumps?

Knocking can come from worn anti-roll-bar links, bushes, ball joints, top mounts, dampers, springs, steering components or loose mountings. The noise should be physically traced because several different faults can produce very similar symptoms.

Can suspension wear cause uneven tyre wear?

Yes. Worn or damaged suspension components can allow wheel geometry to change and contribute to inner-edge wear, feathering or other abnormal tyre patterns. Tyre pressures, wheel alignment and steering condition should also be checked before confirming the cause.

Do I need wheel alignment after suspension repairs?

It depends on which component was replaced and the vehicle design. Repairs involving suspension arms, joints, struts or other parts that locate the wheel often justify an alignment check after the mechanical repair has been completed.

Should suspension parts be replaced in pairs?

Not every suspension component must automatically be replaced in pairs. Springs and shock absorbers are often considered across the axle to maintain comparable behaviour, while arms, bushes and joints may be replaced individually when appropriate. Follow the vehicle and component manufacturer's repair guidance.

How much does suspension repair cost in the UK?

Costs vary widely with the component and vehicle. A simple anti-roll-bar link may cost around £50 to £150 or more per side, while arms, springs, dampers, multiple components and wheel alignment can take a suspension repair into several hundred pounds or more.

Should I repair a suspension advisory before the next MOT?

Repair should be based on the component's current condition rather than the next MOT date. If wear is progressing, symptoms are developing, tyres are being damaged or the component is approaching a rejection threshold, repairing it earlier is usually the safer decision.

Can potholes cause suspension advisories?

Yes. Repeated pothole impacts can accelerate wear in bushes, joints, springs, dampers, wheels and tyres, while a severe impact can bend a suspension or steering component and alter wheel geometry.

Should I buy a used car with a suspension advisory?

It can still be a sensible purchase if the advisory represents normal wear and the repair cost is understood and reflected in the price. Repeated advisories, several worn components, severe tyre wear, impact damage or poor handling deserve closer inspection before buying.

Does a valid MOT mean the suspension is safe until the next test?

No. An MOT records the vehicle's condition against the inspection requirements at the time of the test. Suspension components continue to wear afterwards, so servicing, inspection and maintaining the vehicle in a roadworthy condition remain important throughout the year.
