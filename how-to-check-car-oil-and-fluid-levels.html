How to Check Your Car Oil & Fluid Levels

Checking your car's fluid levels is one of the simplest ways to prevent avoidable breakdowns, overheating, engine damage and expensive repairs. This practical UK guide shows you how to check engine oil, coolant, brake fluid, power steering fluid and screen wash, understand the level markings and recognise when a low level needs more than a simple top-up.

Quick answer

How do you check your car's oil and fluid levels?

Park your car on level ground, switch off the engine and follow the manufacturer's instructions for checking each fluid. Engine oil is usually measured using a dipstick or an electronic oil-level display. Coolant, brake fluid and hydraulic power steering fluid are commonly checked using marked reservoirs. Screen wash is topped up through its dedicated filler neck.

Most fluid reservoirs have MIN and MAX markings. The fluid should normally sit between these marks when checked under the conditions specified by the manufacturer. A low level may mean the fluid needs topping up, but repeated fluid loss can indicate a leak, wear or another mechanical problem.

What should you check?

Engine oil, coolant, brake fluid, screen wash and power steering fluid if your vehicle uses hydraulic steering. Some modern cars have electric power steering and no steering fluid reservoir.

How often?

Check oil and other accessible fluid levels regularly, ideally around once a month and before long journeys. Follow your vehicle handbook for exact checking conditions and intervals.

What needs urgent attention?

A red oil-pressure warning, overheating, rapidly falling coolant, low brake fluid with braking symptoms or visible fluid leaks should be investigated promptly. Do not continue driving with a suspected serious fault.

Understanding MIN and MAX fluid level marks

The MIN mark normally represents the lower acceptable level and MAX the upper limit for the specified checking conditions. Being slightly below MAX does not automatically mean a fluid needs topping up. Never assume that filling every reservoir to the very top is correct.

Between MIN and MAX

Usually acceptable

The level is normally within the intended range, provided the vehicle is being checked under the correct conditions and there are no related symptoms.

At or below MIN

Check the cause

Check the handbook before topping up. Brake fluid, in particular, may fall as brake pads wear and should not be repeatedly topped up without inspection.

Above MAX

Avoid overfilling

Excess fluid can cause problems. Engine oil overfilling can damage components, while coolant requires expansion space as the engine warms.

Mechanic's tip

A fluid level tells you how much fluid is present, not necessarily whether the system is healthy. If you repeatedly need to add oil, coolant, brake fluid or steering fluid, investigate the reason instead of making topping up a routine substitute for repair.

Important note for hybrid and electric vehicles

Hybrid and electric cars may have different reservoir layouts, additional cooling circuits or no conventional engine oil. Never open high-voltage system components or unfamiliar coolant circuits. Follow the manufacturer's maintenance instructions for your exact vehicle.

Preparation and safety

Before You Start: How to Check Car Fluids Safely

Before checking your car's oil or fluid levels, take a few minutes to prepare the vehicle properly. Incorrect checking conditions can produce misleading readings, while opening the wrong reservoir or handling hot components can cause injury or damage.

You do not need professional workshop equipment for most basic fluid checks. However, you should know where the reservoirs are located, what the correct fluid specifications are and which checks require the engine to be completely cold.

Park on level ground

Choose a safe, flat parking area, apply the parking brake and switch off the engine. A slope can affect oil readings and make reservoir levels appear higher or lower than they really are.

Check the owner's handbook

Your handbook identifies the correct fluid specifications, checking procedures, reservoir locations and manufacturer-approved top-up methods. Different engines and vehicle models may require different fluids.

Allow hot components to cool

Keep clear of hot exhaust parts, moving belts and cooling fans. The coolant system must be allowed to cool before opening its pressure cap. Some electric cooling fans can operate unexpectedly.

What you need for a basic car fluid check

  • ✓Your vehicle owner's handbook or manufacturer maintenance instructions
  • ✓A clean, lint-free cloth or disposable paper towel for the oil dipstick
  • ✓A torch to help identify reservoir markings and fluid levels
  • ✓Protective gloves where appropriate
  • ✓A clean funnel suitable for the fluid being added
  • ✓The correct manufacturer-approved replacement fluid, if topping up is necessary

Identify the correct fluid reservoirs

Open the bonnet using the interior release and secure it using the bonnet stay or built-in support system. Identify each reservoir before removing any cap. Many modern cars use symbols on the caps rather than written labels, so compare them with the handbook.

Engine oil

The oil filler cap normally has an oil-can symbol. A separate dipstick may have a brightly coloured handle, although some modern engines use electronic oil-level measurement instead.

Coolant expansion tank

Usually a translucent plastic tank with level markings and a warning symbol on its pressure cap. It contains coolant that can become extremely hot and pressurised.

Brake fluid reservoir

Commonly positioned near the rear of the engine bay on the brake master cylinder. The cap may display a brake symbol and the required fluid specification.

Power steering reservoir

Found only on vehicles with a serviceable hydraulic power steering system. Many modern vehicles use electric power steering and have no steering fluid to check.

Screen wash reservoir

Usually identified by a windscreen and spray symbol on the filler cap. Its main reservoir may be hidden lower down in the engine compartment.

Other vehicle fluids

Automatic transmission fluid, clutch hydraulic fluid and specialist cooling circuits may require additional procedures. Do not open unfamiliar reservoirs or attempt specialist checks without the correct instructions.

Safety warning: never guess which reservoir is which

Adding the wrong fluid can damage an engine, braking system, steering system or cooling circuit. Never pour engine oil into a coolant tank, screen wash into a brake fluid reservoir or an unspecified hydraulic fluid into the power steering system. If you are unsure, stop and confirm the reservoir and fluid specification first.

Mechanic's tip

Before checking levels, look around the engine bay for fresh wet patches, stained reservoir seams, damaged hoses and signs of fluid collecting underneath the car. These clues may explain a low level before you add anything.

Step 1 — Engine oil

Step 1: How to Check Your Car Engine Oil Level

Engine oil lubricates moving engine components, helps manage heat, reduces friction and protects internal surfaces from wear. Running an engine with insufficient oil can quickly cause expensive damage, while overfilling can also create serious mechanical problems.

Checking the oil level is a straightforward maintenance task on most petrol and diesel cars. The exact method depends on whether your vehicle uses a traditional dipstick or an electronic oil-level display.

How to check engine oil with a dipstick

1. Park safely

Park on level ground, switch off the engine and follow the handbook's instructions for checking temperature and waiting time. Many vehicles require several minutes for oil to drain back into the sump.

2. Locate the dipstick

Open the bonnet and identify the engine oil dipstick. It often has a yellow or orange handle, but its appearance and location vary between manufacturers.

3. Wipe and reinsert

Pull out the dipstick, wipe it clean with a lint-free cloth and insert it fully back into its tube. Remove it again to obtain a clear oil-level reading.

4. Read the level

Check where the oil film reaches between the MIN and MAX marks or the manufacturer's marked measuring area. Recheck if the reading is smeared or unclear.

What do the MIN and MAX marks mean on an oil dipstick?

The dipstick markings identify the manufacturer's intended operating range. Oil between MIN and MAX is normally acceptable. A reading at or below MIN means you should follow the manufacturer's top-up guidance before further use.

Near MAX

Normal range

The engine normally has sufficient oil. There is no need to add more simply because the level is not exactly on the maximum line.

Near MIN

Top-up may be needed

Check the handbook and add the correct oil in small amounts if required. A level near the lower mark should be monitored for consumption or leakage.

Above MAX

Overfilled

Excess oil can contribute to foaming, crankcase pressure problems, seal damage and other engine faults. Do not continue adding oil once the correct level is reached.

How much oil is between MIN and MAX?

On many passenger-car engines, the difference between the minimum and maximum dipstick marks is approximately one litre, but this is not universal. Some engines have a smaller or larger measuring range. Always check your handbook rather than assuming that one litre is the correct top-up amount.

How to top up your engine oil correctly

  • ✓Confirm the exact oil specification and viscosity required for your engine.
  • ✓Remove the engine oil filler cap, not the dipstick tube or another reservoir cap.
  • ✓Add a small quantity of the correct oil using a clean funnel.
  • ✓Allow time for the oil to settle and check the level again using the correct procedure.
  • ✓Repeat gradually until the level is safely within the specified range.
  • ✓Refit the filler cap securely and clean away any spilled oil.

For a small top-up, adding approximately 100–200 ml at a time can help prevent overfilling, provided this is suitable for your vehicle. Do not use this as a substitute for the manufacturer's instructions, particularly where electronic measurement systems require a specific procedure.

How to check engine oil without a dipstick

Some modern cars measure engine oil electronically. The oil level may appear in the dashboard menu, vehicle settings screen or infotainment system. Depending on the vehicle, the engine may need to be warm, switched off for a set period or running at idle while the car is stationary.

Follow the exact instructions shown by the vehicle or in the handbook. Never assume that an electronic oil-level reading uses the same checking conditions as a traditional dipstick.

What colour should healthy engine oil be?

Fresh engine oil is often amber or golden, but its appearance changes during use. Oil can become brown or black as it collects combustion by-products and contaminants. Dark oil alone does not prove that the oil is overdue for replacement, particularly in diesel engines.

Signs your engine may be losing or burning oil

Frequent top-ups

Repeated oil loss can indicate consumption, external leakage or a mechanical fault. Some consumption may be within manufacturer limits, but a sudden increase requires investigation.

Blue exhaust smoke

Persistent blue or blue-grey exhaust smoke can indicate engine oil entering the combustion chambers, potentially through worn seals, piston rings or turbocharger components.

Oil patches underneath

Fresh oil around the sump, oil filter housing, rocker cover or underneath the vehicle may indicate an external leak. The source should be confirmed before replacing parts.

Red oil-pressure warning: stop the engine

A red oil-pressure warning is not simply a reminder to top up. It may indicate that the engine is not receiving sufficient lubrication pressure. Stop safely, switch off the engine and do not restart or continue driving until the cause has been assessed. Even an apparently normal dipstick reading does not rule out a serious oil-pressure fault.

Mechanic's tip

Record the mileage whenever you add engine oil. If you need another top-up soon afterwards, you will have a useful record of how quickly the level is falling. This helps distinguish gradual consumption from an emerging leak or mechanical fault.

Step 2 — Coolant and antifreeze

Step 2: How to Check Your Car Coolant Level

Engine coolant helps control engine temperature and protects the cooling system against freezing, corrosion and overheating. Most petrol and diesel cars use a mixture of water and antifreeze, although the correct formulation and concentration depend on the manufacturer.

A healthy cooling system should maintain a stable coolant level when checked under consistent conditions. Repeated coolant loss is not normal and should be investigated, even when there is no obvious puddle beneath the vehicle.

How to check the coolant expansion tank

1. Let the engine cool

Switch off the engine and allow it to cool completely. The safest routine level check is normally performed before the first journey of the day.

2. Find the expansion tank

Locate the coolant expansion tank using the handbook. It is usually translucent and may contain pink, red, orange, blue, green or another manufacturer-specified coolant.

3. Read the level marks

Check the coolant level against the COLD, MIN and MAX markings, as applicable. The fluid should be within the manufacturer's specified cold range.

4. Inspect for problems

Look for staining, wet hose joints, cracks, crusty deposits or fluid around the reservoir. A level that repeatedly drops may indicate a leak or another cooling-system fault.

What should the coolant level be when the engine is cold?

On most vehicles with a conventional expansion tank, the cold coolant level should be between the marked minimum and maximum limits. Some vehicles have a separate COLD FILL line or a specific inspection procedure.

Coolant expands as it heats up, so the level can rise when the engine is running. This is why a hot reading should not be compared directly with the manufacturer's cold-level specification.

Within the cold range

Usually normal

The level is acceptable when checked under the correct conditions, provided there are no leaks, overheating symptoms or recurring losses.

Below MIN

Investigate

Coolant may need topping up, but the reason for the loss should also be considered. A sudden drop or an empty reservoir can indicate a significant fault.

Above MAX

Check for overfill

Too much coolant can reduce the intended expansion space. Check the level when cold and correct any overfill using the manufacturer's procedure.

How to top up engine coolant safely

  • ✓Allow the engine and cooling system to cool completely.
  • ✓Confirm the correct coolant specification in the owner's handbook.
  • ✓Use the specified ready-mixed coolant or approved concentrate diluted as instructed.
  • ✓Only open the correct filler cap when the system is cold and safe to open.
  • ✓Add coolant gradually to the specified cold-level mark without overfilling.
  • ✓Secure the cap and monitor the level over subsequent journeys.

Many ready-mixed coolants are supplied at approximately 50% antifreeze and 50% water, but the correct mixture varies by product and vehicle. Never assume that a universal coolant is compatible with every car. Use the manufacturer-approved formulation and follow the product's dilution instructions.

Does coolant colour tell you which antifreeze to use?

No. Coolant colour is not a reliable universal guide to chemical compatibility. Different manufacturers can use similar colours for different formulations, and compatible products may have different dyes.

Common signs of a coolant leak or cooling-system fault

Coolant level keeps dropping

Repeated coolant loss may come from hoses, radiator joints, the water pump, thermostat housing, expansion tank or internal engine faults.

Sweet smell or coloured stains

Some coolants have a distinctive sweet smell. Dried coloured deposits around hose connections or under the bonnet can help identify an external leak.

Temperature warning

A rising temperature gauge, overheating warning or steam from the engine bay can indicate a serious cooling-system problem. Stop safely and switch off the engine.

Coolant bubbling

Persistent bubbling may indicate trapped air, boiling coolant, combustion gases entering the system or another pressure-related problem. The pattern and checking conditions matter.

Coolant pushed out

Coolant escaping from the expansion tank overflow can result from overfilling, overheating, cap failure or excessive cooling-system pressure.

Heater performance changes

A cabin heater that suddenly blows cold air alongside a falling coolant level or rising engine temperature may indicate low coolant or circulation problems.

Overheating warning: do not keep driving

If the temperature warning illuminates, the gauge rises into the danger zone or steam appears, stop somewhere safe and switch off the engine. Do not remove the coolant cap while the system is hot. Continuing to drive an overheating engine can cause severe engine damage.

Mechanic's tip

Check the coolant level under the same conditions each time, ideally with the engine fully cold. Take note of where the level sits against the reservoir markings. A gradual downward trend can reveal a developing leak before the dashboard displays a warning.

Hybrid and electric vehicle coolant systems

Some hybrid and electric vehicles have separate cooling circuits for the engine, battery, power electronics or electric drive components. These systems may use specialist coolants and servicing procedures. Do not open unfamiliar cooling circuits or high-voltage components.

Step 3 — Brake fluid

Step 3: How to Check Your Car Brake Fluid Level

Brake fluid is one of the most important safety fluids in your car. When you press the brake pedal, hydraulic pressure travels through the brake fluid to operate the brakes. If the fluid level becomes dangerously low, the system develops a leak or air enters the hydraulic circuit, braking performance can be seriously affected.

Unlike engine oil or screen wash, brake fluid should not normally need frequent topping up. A gradual fall in reservoir level can occur as brake pads wear, but a sudden or repeated loss may indicate a hydraulic leak requiring urgent inspection.

How to check the brake fluid reservoir

1. Park safely

Park on level ground, apply the parking brake and switch off the engine. Follow any manufacturer-specific instructions before inspecting the brake fluid reservoir.

2. Locate the reservoir

The brake fluid reservoir is commonly positioned towards the rear of the engine bay, mounted on or near the brake master cylinder. Consult the handbook to identify it correctly.

3. Read MIN and MAX

Inspect the translucent reservoir from the outside and compare the fluid level with its markings. Use a torch if necessary, without opening the cap.

4. Check for warning signs

Look for a level below MIN, dampness around the reservoir, visible hydraulic leaks or an illuminated brake warning light. Any abnormality should be investigated.

What should the brake fluid level be?

On most vehicles, the brake fluid should remain between the MIN and MAX markings. A level slightly below MAX is not automatically a fault. As brake pads wear, caliper pistons extend further and more fluid remains within the braking system, which can gradually lower the reservoir level.

Between MIN and MAX

Usually acceptable

The level is normally within the intended range, provided there are no leaks, braking symptoms or warning lights.

Approaching MIN

Inspection recommended

Brake pad wear may explain a gradual reduction, but the hydraulic system should be inspected before assuming the level only needs topping up.

Below MIN or falling rapidly

Urgent safety concern

A low or rapidly falling level may indicate a leak or another brake system fault. Do not continue driving if braking performance is affected or a hydraulic leak is suspected.

What colour should healthy brake fluid be?

Many conventional glycol-based brake fluids are clear, pale yellow or light amber when new. Brake fluid can darken with age, but colour alone cannot reliably determine moisture content, boiling point or whether replacement is necessary.

Brake fluid appearance Possible meaning Recommended action
Clear or pale amber Common appearance of fresh conventional brake fluid Check level and follow the manufacturer's replacement schedule
Dark amber or brown May indicate ageing or contamination, but colour is not a reliable condition test Check service history and arrange appropriate fluid testing or replacement
Cloudy or visibly contaminated Possible contamination or deterioration Arrange professional inspection and fluid replacement as required
Unusually low level Possible brake pad wear, hydraulic leakage or another fault Inspect the braking system before topping up

Should you top up brake fluid yourself?

Brake fluid is different from ordinary maintenance fluids because the reservoir level can reflect brake pad wear or a hydraulic fault. Repeatedly adding fluid without finding the cause can hide an important safety problem.

If topping up is specifically required by the manufacturer or following a professional inspection, only the exact approved fluid specification should be used. Common specifications include DOT 3, DOT 4 and DOT 5.1, but the correct type depends on the vehicle. DOT 5 silicone-based fluid is not interchangeable with conventional glycol-based fluids.

Brake fluid safety warning

Never add an unidentified fluid to the brake reservoir. Brake fluid can damage paintwork and must be protected from moisture and contamination. If the brake pedal feels spongy, sinks unusually far, a brake warning remains illuminated or a hydraulic leak is suspected, do not drive the vehicle until the braking system has been assessed.

How often should brake fluid be replaced?

Many manufacturers recommend brake fluid replacement approximately every two years, although some specify different intervals. Conventional glycol-based brake fluid absorbs moisture over time, which can lower its boiling point and contribute to corrosion within the hydraulic system.

A brake fluid change involves more than adding fresh fluid to the reservoir. The correct service procedure replaces fluid throughout the hydraulic circuit and may require specialist equipment, particularly on vehicles with certain ABS or electronically controlled braking systems.

Mechanic's tip

If brake fluid is near MIN, inspect brake pad thickness and check for leaks before topping up. Filling the reservoir to MAX while the pads are heavily worn can cause the level to rise excessively when the caliper pistons are pushed back during future brake servicing.

Step 4 — Power steering fluid

Step 4: How to Check Power Steering Fluid

Power steering reduces the effort required to turn the steering wheel, especially when parking or manoeuvring at low speeds. Older vehicles and some modern models use hydraulic power steering fluid to transfer pressure from a pump to the steering mechanism.

However, many modern cars use electric power steering, which does not have a conventional hydraulic fluid reservoir. Before attempting a fluid check, confirm which steering system your vehicle uses.

Hydraulic versus electric power steering

Hydraulic power steering

Uses hydraulic pressure, a steering pump, hoses and a fluid reservoir. The system may require routine level checks and inspection for leaks.

Fluid level check required

Electric power steering

Uses an electric motor and electronic controls to provide steering assistance. Conventional electric power steering normally has no hydraulic fluid reservoir.

No conventional fluid top-up

How to check hydraulic power steering fluid

1. Identify the system

Check the handbook to confirm that your car has hydraulic power steering and locate the correct reservoir.

2. Check the temperature instructions

Some reservoirs have separate HOT and COLD markings. Follow the manufacturer's checking procedure and required engine operating conditions.

3. Inspect the level

Read the markings on the reservoir or its built-in dipstick. Keep the filler area clean and avoid opening the system unnecessarily.

4. Look for leaks

Inspect accessible hoses, connections and the area around the steering pump and rack for dampness, staining or fresh fluid.

What do HOT and COLD steering fluid marks mean?

Hydraulic steering fluid expands as its temperature increases. Some reservoirs therefore provide separate level ranges for a cold system and a system at normal operating temperature.

Always compare the level with the correct marking for the specified checking conditions. Using the HOT mark when the fluid is cold, or vice versa, can lead to an incorrect top-up.

What colour should power steering fluid be?

Power steering fluid can be red, amber, green or another colour depending on the fluid specification. Colour alone does not identify the correct replacement fluid. Some systems use automatic transmission fluid, while others require a dedicated hydraulic fluid.

Fluid condition Possible meaning Recommended action
Clear red, amber or green May be normal for the specified hydraulic fluid Confirm the manufacturer's required specification
Very dark or burnt smell Possible fluid deterioration, overheating or contamination Arrange inspection of fluid condition and steering components
Foamy or aerated Possible air entering the system, low fluid or another hydraulic fault Check for leaks and arrange diagnosis
Repeatedly low Possible leak from hoses, pump, reservoir or steering rack Locate and repair the cause rather than repeatedly topping up

How to top up power steering fluid

Only top up a hydraulic steering system after confirming the correct reservoir, fluid specification and checking conditions. Use a clean funnel and add small amounts, rechecking the level against the appropriate HOT or COLD mark.

Never assume that any red automatic transmission fluid or universal steering fluid is suitable. Incorrect fluid can damage seals, hoses, pumps or steering components.

Signs of low power steering fluid or a hydraulic fault

Whining when turning

A whining noise that changes with steering input can indicate low fluid, aeration or a failing hydraulic pump.

Heavy steering

Increased steering effort can indicate loss of hydraulic assistance, pump problems or other steering faults.

Fluid around the steering rack

Wet steering components, hoses or rack gaiters may indicate hydraulic leakage requiring professional diagnosis.

Steering safety warning

If steering suddenly becomes very heavy, assistance is lost or a significant hydraulic leak is present, stop safely and arrange assistance. Do not continue driving with a steering system that cannot be controlled safely.

Mechanic's tip

A healthy hydraulic power steering system should not require frequent fluid top-ups. If the reservoir keeps dropping, inspect the system for leaks before replacing the pump. A leaking hose or steering rack seal may be the actual cause.

Step 5 — Screen wash

Step 5: How to Check and Top Up Screen Wash

Screen wash helps remove dirt, road salt, insects and oily deposits from your windscreen. Maintaining an adequate supply is particularly important during UK winter driving, when road spray and salt can quickly reduce visibility.

Unlike engine oil, coolant or brake fluid, screen wash is normally consumed during everyday use. Regular topping up is expected and does not usually indicate a mechanical problem.

How to top up your screen wash

1. Locate the filler cap

Open the bonnet and find the screen wash filler neck. The cap normally displays a windscreen symbol with water spray.

2. Choose suitable screen wash

Use a screen wash product suitable for your vehicle and the expected UK weather conditions, including appropriate freeze protection in winter.

3. Fill carefully

Add the correct ready-mixed product or dilute concentrate according to the label. Fill to the manufacturer's recommended level without forcing extra liquid into the neck.

4. Test the washers

Secure the cap and operate the washers briefly. Confirm that fluid reaches the windscreen and the wipers clear it effectively.

Can you use water instead of screen wash?

Plain water may provide limited cleaning in mild conditions, but it does not offer the cleaning performance or freeze protection of a suitable screen wash product. In cold weather, water can freeze inside the reservoir, hoses or washer jets.

Use a suitable screen wash mixture throughout the year and follow the product's dilution instructions. Avoid using household washing-up liquid because it is not formulated for vehicle washer systems and may cause excessive foaming or leave unwanted residues.

Choosing screen wash for UK weather

Summer driving

Choose a product designed to remove road film, insects and everyday windscreen contamination.

Routine maintenance

Winter driving

Select a mixture with freeze protection appropriate for the expected temperatures. Concentrates must be diluted according to the label.

Cold-weather priority

Long journeys

Check the reservoir before travelling, especially during motorway journeys where road spray can rapidly use up screen wash.

Pre-journey check

Can empty screen wash cause an MOT failure?

Yes. For a vehicle required to have windscreen washers, the washers must operate and provide sufficient fluid to clear the windscreen effectively. An empty reservoir that prevents the washers from working can result in an MOT failure.

The MOT also considers whether the windscreen wipers can clear the swept area adequately. Poor washer performance, blocked jets or damaged wipers can affect visibility and should be corrected before the test.

Visibility and road safety

Do not continue driving if your windscreen is too dirty to see through safely and the washers or wipers cannot clear it. This is particularly important on wet, salty roads and during winter motorway driving.

Mechanic's tip

Top up screen wash before long journeys and before an MOT. Test both the washers and wipers rather than checking the reservoir alone. A full reservoir is no help if the pump, hoses or washer jets are not working.

Fluid condition and diagnosis

Car Fluid Colours, Low Levels and Warning Signs Explained

Checking a fluid level tells you how much fluid is present, but it does not always tell you whether the fluid or the system is healthy. Mechanics also look at fluid appearance, smell, contamination, leaks, warning lights and how quickly the level changes.

A fluid that looks dark is not automatically defective, and a fluid that looks clean is not automatically safe. The correct specification, service history and symptoms are more reliable than colour alone.

Avoid expensive mistakes

Common Mistakes When Checking or Topping Up Car Fluids

Many fluid-related problems begin with simple maintenance mistakes. Using the wrong product, overfilling a reservoir or ignoring repeated fluid loss can turn an inexpensive check into a costly repair.

1. Using the wrong fluid

Different engines, cooling systems, braking systems and steering systems require specific formulations. Never select a fluid based only on colour or a generic label.

2. Overfilling engine oil

Adding too much oil can cause foaming, crankcase pressure problems and mechanical damage. Top up gradually and recheck using the correct procedure.

3. Opening hot coolant caps

A hot cooling system can release pressurised coolant and steam. Never remove the expansion tank or radiator pressure cap while the system is hot.

4. Ignoring repeated fluid loss

Regularly adding coolant, steering fluid or excessive amounts of engine oil without investigating the cause can allow a developing fault to become more serious.

5. Mixing incompatible coolant

Coolants with similar colours may use different additive technologies. Mixing incompatible formulations can reduce corrosion protection or create deposits.

6. Treating brake fluid like screen wash

Brake fluid should not normally be consumed. A falling level may indicate pad wear or leakage, so repeated topping up without inspection is unsafe.

7. Checking on uneven ground

Parking on a slope can distort dipstick readings and some reservoir measurements. Follow the manufacturer's checking conditions.

8. Contaminating reservoirs

Dirty funnels, cloth fibres, water or incorrect fluids can contaminate vehicle systems. Use clean equipment and keep filler openings protected.

9. Ignoring dashboard warnings

A red oil-pressure warning or overheating alert may indicate an active fault rather than a routine low-level reminder. Stopping safely is more important than continuing the journey.

Accidentally added fluid to the wrong reservoir?

Do not start the engine or operate the affected system. Incorrect fluid can damage seals, hydraulic components, cooling passages or lubrication systems. Arrange professional advice and correction before using the vehicle.

Mechanic's tip

Keep a record of the exact oil and coolant specifications used during servicing. A service invoice, handbook reference or manufacturer-approved product code is more useful than remembering that the fluid was simply blue, green or red.

UK MOT guidance

Car Fluid Levels and the MOT Test: What Can Fail?

An MOT is a roadworthiness inspection, not a full mechanical service. Testers do not routinely assess every fluid level or determine whether your engine oil is due for replacement. However, fluid leaks, braking defects, washer faults and other safety-related problems can affect the MOT result.

The result depends on the specific defect, its severity and the applicable DVSA inspection criteria. A vehicle can also have a serious mechanical problem that is not detected by an MOT.

Which fluid-related faults can affect an MOT?

Fluid or system What the MOT checks Possible outcome
Engine oil Relevant fluid leaks and their severity; oil level itself is not a routine service check Excessive relevant leaks can cause failure; an unsafe vehicle may also be refused testing
Engine coolant Coolant level and ordinary coolant leakage are not assessed under the general fluid-leak defect item Low coolant alone is not a specific MOT failure, but overheating or unsafe conditions may prevent testing
Brake fluid Brake fluid level, hydraulic leaks, relevant warning indicators and braking performance Insufficient fluid or a hydraulic defect can cause a Major or Dangerous failure depending on severity
Power steering fluid Relevant hydraulic leaks, steering operation and associated steering components Significant leaks or steering defects can cause failure
Screen wash Whether required washers provide sufficient fluid to clear the windscreen effectively Inoperative or inadequate required washers can cause failure

Engine oil leaks and the MOT

Under section 8.4.1 of the DVSA MOT inspection manual, relevant fluid leaks are assessed according to their severity. The inspection criteria include a continuous leak that creates a pool on the floor greater than 75 mm in diameter within five minutes, as well as leaks likely to pose a risk to road users or the environment.

A leak that is not excessive may be recorded as an advisory. A leak meeting the Major or Dangerous criteria can result in an MOT failure. Multiple leaks may be considered collectively, so several small leaks should not automatically be treated as harmless.

Minor seepage

May receive an advisory

A small oil seepage that does not meet the failure criteria may still require repair before it becomes more serious.

Excessive fluid leakage

MOT failure

A relevant leak meeting the DVSA Major defect criteria can cause failure, even if the driver has not noticed a dashboard warning.

Dangerous leakage

Do not drive

A leak presenting a serious risk to road users or the environment can be classified as Dangerous and requires immediate attention.

Can low brake fluid fail an MOT?

Yes. The MOT includes checks of the braking system, including the brake fluid reservoir and hydraulic components. Insufficient brake fluid, hydraulic leakage or other serious brake defects can result in failure.

A brake warning light, poor braking efficiency or abnormal brake pedal operation may also indicate a fault that requires repair. Do not assume that topping up the reservoir will correct the underlying problem.

Can empty screen wash fail an MOT?

Yes. If a vehicle is required to have windscreen washers, they must operate and supply sufficient fluid to clear the windscreen. An empty reservoir that prevents the system from working can result in an MOT failure.

Does an MOT check coolant and engine oil levels?

An MOT is not designed to replace routine fluid maintenance. Testers do not normally carry out a full engine oil or coolant service inspection. However, an engine with insufficient oil or coolant may be unsafe to run during the test, and testers can refuse or discontinue testing where the vehicle's condition makes it unsafe or unsuitable to proceed.

Important MOT advice

A valid MOT certificate does not guarantee that the vehicle has sufficient engine oil, healthy coolant, correctly serviced brake fluid or no developing leaks. Routine fluid checks remain the owner's responsibility between MOT tests.

Five-Minute Car Fluid Check: Before a Journey or MOT

A short routine inspection can help you identify low fluid levels before they become breakdowns or safety problems. The following checklist is suitable for many everyday petrol and diesel cars, although the manufacturer's instructions always take priority.

Quick under-bonnet inspection checklist

  • ✓Park on level ground and secure the vehicle.
  • ✓Check engine oil using the correct dipstick or electronic procedure.
  • ✓Inspect coolant level when the engine is fully cold.
  • ✓Check brake fluid level without unnecessarily opening the reservoir.
  • ✓Check hydraulic power steering fluid if fitted.
  • ✓Top up screen wash using a suitable product.

  • ✓Confirm all reservoir caps are correctly secured.
  • ✓Check the dashboard for relevant warning lights.
  • ✓Record repeated top-ups or unusual level changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Car Oil & Fluid Levels FAQs

These are some of the most common questions UK drivers ask about checking engine oil, coolant, brake fluid, power steering fluid, screen wash, safe topping-up procedures and MOT requirements.

How often should I check my car's oil and fluid levels?

As a practical routine, check accessible fluid levels approximately once a month and before a long journey. Older vehicles, high-mileage cars and engines with known oil consumption may require more frequent checks. Always follow the intervals and checking procedures in your vehicle handbook.

Should I check engine oil when the engine is hot or cold?

Follow the manufacturer's instructions. Many vehicles require the engine to be switched off for several minutes so the oil can drain back into the sump. Others use electronic oil-level monitoring with specific operating-temperature requirements. Park on level ground and use the procedure recommended for your engine.

What happens if my engine oil is below the MIN mark?

An oil level below MIN may reduce the engine's available oil reserve and increase the risk of lubrication problems. Use the correct manufacturer-approved oil and restore the level according to the handbook before driving. If the red oil-pressure warning appears, stop safely, switch off the engine and do not continue driving until the problem has been assessed.

Can too much engine oil damage an engine?

Yes. Excessive engine oil can cause crankshaft aeration, increased crankcase pressure, oil entering the intake system and potential damage to engine components or emissions equipment. Add oil gradually, allow the level to settle and avoid filling beyond the manufacturer's maximum level.

Can I top up engine oil with a different brand?

The most important requirement is that the oil meets the viscosity and manufacturer approval specified for your engine. Different brands may produce oils that meet the same specification, but do not assume that oils are suitable simply because they have the same viscosity printed on the bottle. Check the vehicle handbook before topping up.

Can I open the coolant expansion tank when the engine is hot?

No. A hot cooling system may be pressurised, and removing the cap can release boiling coolant or steam that causes serious burns. Allow the engine to cool fully before inspecting or opening the reservoir, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Can I use water instead of coolant?

The correct manufacturer-approved coolant mixture should be used for normal operation because it provides freeze protection, corrosion resistance and appropriate cooling-system performance. Plain water may be an emergency temporary measure for some conventional systems, but it is not a suitable long-term replacement. Never add fluid to a dangerously hot pressurised system.

Does the colour of coolant tell me which type to use?

No. Coolant colours vary between manufacturers and do not reliably identify chemical compatibility. Two coolants of the same colour can have different specifications. Use the formulation approved for your vehicle and avoid mixing unknown coolant types.

Why does my coolant level keep dropping?

Repeated coolant loss may be caused by a leaking hose, radiator, water pump, expansion tank, pressure cap or an internal engine problem. A leak is not always visible beneath the car. If the level repeatedly falls, arrange a cooling-system inspection rather than continually topping it up. See our Car Losing Coolant but No Leak guide.

Is it safe to top up low brake fluid?

A low brake fluid level should first be investigated because it may be caused by worn brake pads or a hydraulic leak. Repeatedly adding fluid can hide a serious braking fault. If the brake warning light is illuminated, the pedal feels abnormal or a leak is suspected, do not continue driving until the braking system has been checked.

Can I mix different DOT brake fluids?

Always use the brake fluid specification approved by the vehicle manufacturer. Although conventional glycol-based DOT 3, DOT 4 and DOT 5.1 fluids have some chemical compatibility, they are not automatically suitable substitutes for one another. Silicone-based DOT 5 must not be mixed with conventional glycol-based brake fluids.

Does every car have a power steering fluid reservoir?

No. Many modern vehicles use electric power steering and do not have a hydraulic fluid reservoir. Vehicles with hydraulic or electro-hydraulic power steering may have a reservoir with MIN and MAX or HOT and COLD markings. Check the handbook before attempting to top up steering fluid.

Can I use plain water in my windscreen washer bottle?

Suitable screen wash is recommended because it helps remove road dirt, grease and insect residue and can provide protection against freezing. Plain water alone offers poor winter protection and may allow the washer system to freeze in cold conditions.

Can low fluid levels cause an MOT failure?

Some fluid-related defects can cause an MOT failure. Examples include insufficient brake fluid, serious fluid leaks and windscreen washers that fail to deliver sufficient fluid where required. The MOT is not a full vehicle service, and engine oil or coolant levels are not routinely checked in the same way as during scheduled maintenance.

What should I do if I accidentally put the wrong fluid in a reservoir?

Do not start the engine or operate the affected system. Contact a qualified garage and explain exactly which fluid was added and where. Depending on the system, professional draining, flushing or component inspection may be necessary to prevent damage.

Should I check fluid levels before buying a used car?

Yes. Check accessible fluid levels, look for leaks or contamination, inspect the service history and investigate warning lights. Very low fluids, repeated topping-up or evidence of mixed fluids may indicate poor maintenance or a developing fault. Use these checks alongside our Used-Car Buying Guides and consider an independent inspection.

Final Thoughts

Looking After Your Car's Oil and Fluid Systems

Checking your car's oil and fluid levels is one of the most useful basic maintenance skills any driver can learn. Regular inspections can help identify low engine oil, coolant loss, brake hydraulic problems, power steering leaks and empty screen wash reservoirs before they develop into more serious concerns.

However, maintaining a vehicle involves more than simply keeping every reservoir full. Engine oil must meet the correct specification, coolant must protect the cooling system, brake fluid must remain suitable for safe hydraulic braking and power steering fluid must be compatible with the system fitted to the vehicle. Correct levels cannot compensate for contaminated fluid, damaged components or neglected servicing.
