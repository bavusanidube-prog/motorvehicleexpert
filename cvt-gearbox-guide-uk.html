Quick answer

Is a CVT gearbox good or bad?

A CVT, or continuously variable transmission, is a type of automatic gearbox designed to change its driving ratio smoothly instead of moving through a fixed set of gears. This can make driving more comfortable and help an engine operate efficiently, particularly in town traffic and during gentle acceleration.

However, a CVT is not automatically better or worse than a conventional automatic. Some drivers dislike the way engine revs behave during acceleration, while certain belt-and-pulley designs can develop expensive internal problems. Reliability depends on the exact gearbox design, vehicle model, maintenance history and how the car has been used.

Biggest advantage

Smooth acceleration without noticeable traditional gear changes. A CVT can be particularly pleasant in slow-moving traffic and everyday commuting.

Biggest drawback

Some CVTs feel noisy or unfamiliar under hard acceleration. Internal failures can also be costly, especially when specialist parts or a replacement unit are needed.

Best buying advice

Check the exact gearbox fitted, follow the manufacturer's servicing requirements and test drive the car from cold and when warm. Avoid unexplained juddering, slipping or warning lights.

Mechanic insight:

The term CVT covers more than one transmission design. Many conventional CVTs use a metal belt or chain running between variable pulleys. Toyota-style hybrid e-CVT systems generally use a different power-split arrangement without that conventional CVT belt. Their maintenance needs, driving behaviour and potential failure points should not be treated as identical.

CVT gearbox introduction

Introduction — Understanding CVT Gearboxes in the UK

Automatic cars are increasingly popular with UK drivers, especially people who spend a lot of time commuting, driving through towns or sitting in stop-start traffic. One type of automatic transmission you may come across is the CVT gearbox.

CVT stands for continuously variable transmission. Unlike a conventional automatic gearbox, which normally moves between a fixed number of gears, a CVT can continuously adjust its driving ratio to suit road speed, engine load and acceleration.

This allows the engine to operate at a suitable speed without the noticeable gear changes associated with many traditional automatic transmissions. For everyday driving, that can mean smoother progress, less interruption during acceleration and potentially better fuel efficiency.

CVT gearboxes have been fitted to various UK-market models from manufacturers including Nissan, Honda and Subaru. You may also see the term e-CVT used for certain Toyota and Lexus hybrid vehicles, although these systems generally use a different mechanical arrangement from conventional belt-and-pulley CVTs.

Town and city driving

A CVT can make frequent stopping and starting easier because there is no clutch pedal and no need to select gears manually.

Motorway journeys

The gearbox can adjust its ratio to maintain a suitable engine speed while cruising, although the driving experience varies between models.

Used car ownership

A CVT can be a sensible choice, but the exact transmission design, service history and condition matter more than the CVT label alone.

Practical UK buying tip:

Two cars advertised as having a CVT may use very different gearbox systems. Before buying, identify the exact transmission fitted to the vehicle using its model, engine, year and manufacturer information. Do not assume every CVT has the same reliability record or servicing requirements.

How CVT transmissions work

What Is a CVT Gearbox?

A CVT gearbox is an automatic transmission that can change its driving ratio continuously rather than relying on a fixed set of forward gear ratios. This means it can adjust how engine power reaches the wheels without making the usual step-by-step gear changes.

In a common conventional CVT design, two adjustable pulleys are connected by a strong metal belt or chain. As the pulleys change their effective size, the gearbox changes the relationship between engine speed and wheel speed.

You do not need to understand the internal mechanism to drive one. The important difference is how the car responds when you accelerate.

1. You accelerate

You press the accelerator pedal, and the car's control system calculates how much power is needed.

2. The ratio adjusts

The transmission changes its ratio smoothly to match acceleration, road speed and engine load.

3. Engine speed changes

The engine may rise to a steady number of revs while the car continues gaining speed.

4. The car gains speed

The vehicle accelerates without the obvious gear-change interruptions found in many conventional automatics.

Does a CVT gearbox have gears?

A conventional CVT does not normally use a fixed set of forward gears in the same way as a traditional automatic or manual gearbox. Instead, it provides a continuous range of ratios.

Some manufacturers programme simulated gear changes into their CVTs. This can make the car feel more familiar to drivers who are used to traditional automatics. Certain designs also include a physical launch gear or other additional components, so the phrase "no gears" is a useful simplification rather than a rule that applies to every design.

Why does a CVT feel different from a normal automatic?

Conventional automatic

The engine revs usually rise, then drop as the gearbox changes to the next gear. You may notice small changes in engine sound or acceleration.

CVT automatic

The engine may reach a steady speed while the car continues accelerating. There may be no obvious change between individual forward gears.

Under harder acceleration, some CVTs hold the engine at higher revs for longer. This can produce a sustained engine noise that some drivers describe as droning. Although it may sound unusual, it is not necessarily a mechanical fault.

Conventional CVT vs hybrid e-CVT

One of the most important differences for UK buyers is the distinction between a conventional belt-and-pulley CVT and the e-CVT systems found in many Toyota and Lexus hybrids.

Conventional belt-and-pulley CVT

Usually changes ratios using adjustable pulleys and a metal belt or chain. Wear, fluid condition, hydraulic pressure and internal components can affect performance and reliability.

Hybrid power-split e-CVT

Commonly uses a planetary gear arrangement and electric motor-generators to manage power delivery. It does not rely on the same adjustable belt-and-pulley mechanism.

Important reliability distinction

Do not apply conventional CVT belt-failure warnings or belt-replacement costs automatically to Toyota-style hybrid e-CVT systems. Their construction is different, and the faults a specialist investigates will also differ.

Is high engine revving normal with a CVT?

It can be. During firm acceleration or when climbing a hill, a healthy CVT may allow the engine to maintain higher revs while road speed increases.

However, there is a difference between normal CVT behaviour and genuine slipping. If engine speed rises but the car struggles to accelerate, particularly with juddering, overheating, warning lights or loss of drive, the gearbox should be checked.

CVT advantages

Pros of a CVT Gearbox

A CVT gearbox can offer genuine benefits for everyday driving. Its main strengths are smoothness, convenience and the ability to adjust the engine's operating speed without conventional stepped gear changes.

However, these advantages depend on the vehicle, engine, transmission design and driving conditions. A CVT is not guaranteed to be more economical or better to drive than every other automatic gearbox.

1. Potentially better fuel economy

A CVT can help keep the engine operating efficiently by selecting a suitable ratio for the driving conditions. Fuel savings vary by model and are not guaranteed.

2. Smooth acceleration

Because a CVT does not normally move through a fixed sequence of forward gears, acceleration can feel smooth and uninterrupted.

3. Easy to drive

There is no clutch pedal and no need to change gears manually. This makes everyday driving straightforward, particularly for drivers who prefer automatics.

4. Potential emissions benefits

More efficient engine operation can help reduce fuel use and associated CO2 emissions in some vehicles. Actual emissions depend on the complete vehicle and how it is driven.

5. Comfortable in city traffic

A CVT can work well in stop-start traffic because the driver does not have to operate a clutch or deal with frequent manual gear changes.

6. Flexible engine response

The transmission can adjust its ratio continuously, helping the engine respond to changing road speeds, gradients and acceleration demands.

Where a CVT gearbox works particularly well

  • Urban commuting: smooth progress through busy streets and repeated traffic stops.
  • Relaxed everyday driving: comfortable acceleration without traditional gear-change interruptions.
  • Drivers who prefer automatics: simple operation without a clutch pedal.
  • Gentle motorway cruising: the transmission can select a suitable ratio for steady-speed driving.
  • Some efficiency-focused vehicles: a CVT may help the engine operate economically when combined with a suitable engine and vehicle design.

Does a CVT always use less fuel?

No. A CVT can improve efficiency in some applications, but it is not automatically more economical than a modern torque-converter automatic, dual-clutch gearbox or manual transmission.

Vehicle weight, engine size, driving style, road conditions and the gearbox's calibration all influence fuel consumption. When comparing cars, use the official fuel economy figures for the exact model and consider real-world driving conditions.

Practical ownership advice:

If you mainly drive around town and prefer relaxed acceleration, a well-maintained CVT may suit you very well. If you regularly tow heavy loads, drive enthusiastically or prefer the feeling of traditional gear changes, compare the CVT with other transmission options before buying.

Remember that comfort and fuel economy are only part of the ownership picture. Servicing history, reliability, warranty coverage and possible repair costs should also influence your decision.

CVT disadvantages and faults

Cons & Common Problems With CVT Gearboxes

CVT gearboxes have genuine advantages, but they also have drawbacks that UK drivers should understand before buying. Some are simply differences in driving feel. Others involve mechanical wear, maintenance requirements and potentially expensive repairs.

It is important not to assume that every CVT is unreliable. Different manufacturers use different designs, and a well-maintained gearbox can provide many years of service. However, certain conventional CVTs have developed reputations for costly failures, particularly when servicing has been neglected or problems have been ignored.

1. Repairs can be expensive

One of the biggest disadvantages of a conventional CVT is the potential cost of internal gearbox repairs. Components such as the metal belt or chain, adjustable pulleys, hydraulic control system and bearings must operate precisely.

When serious internal damage occurs, some garages may recommend replacing the gearbox rather than repairing individual components. Specialist knowledge and parts availability can also affect the final bill.

Before authorising an expensive repair, compare the quotation with our Automatic Gearbox Repair Costs UK guide.

2. Shuddering or juddering when accelerating

Some conventional CVTs can develop vibration or judder when pulling away, accelerating gently or driving at lower speeds. The car may feel as though it is shaking, hesitating or repeatedly grabbing and releasing.

Possible causes include incorrect or degraded transmission fluid, hydraulic pressure problems, clutch-related faults in certain designs or internal transmission wear.

However, juddering does not always mean the gearbox is failing. Engine misfires, worn engine mounts and other drivetrain faults can create similar symptoms.

For related symptoms, see Automatic Gearbox Shudders When Accelerating.

3. Belt, chain or pulley wear

Many conventional CVTs use a metal belt or chain between adjustable pulleys. These parts depend on correct hydraulic pressure, suitable transmission fluid and properly controlled operating conditions.

Excessive wear or damage can affect the gearbox's ability to transfer power. In severe cases, the vehicle may lose drive altogether.

Damage involving the belt, chain or pulleys can be particularly expensive because other internal components may also be affected. A simple fluid change will not repair a physically damaged belt or pulley.

Important CVT design distinction

Belt and pulley failure is relevant to conventional CVTs that use these components. It does not describe the usual operating mechanism of Toyota-style hybrid power-split e-CVT systems. Always identify the actual gearbox before discussing likely failures or repair costs.

4. High engine revs and droning noise

Some drivers dislike the way a CVT sounds during firm acceleration. Instead of hearing the engine rise and fall through several gear changes, you may hear a sustained engine note while the car gains speed.

This is often a normal characteristic of CVT operation rather than a fault. It can be more noticeable when accelerating onto a motorway, climbing a steep hill or overtaking.

However, a new whining, grinding, rattling or buzzing noise should not automatically be dismissed as normal CVT behaviour. A noise that becomes progressively louder or is accompanied by poor acceleration deserves investigation.

Read our Automatic Gearbox Whining Noise guide for more information.

5. Not every CVT is suitable for heavy towing

Towing places additional demands on a transmission because the gearbox must handle increased vehicle load and heat. Some CVT-equipped cars have limited towing capacities, while others are not approved for towing at all.

This does not mean every CVT is unsuitable for towing. The correct answer depends on the exact vehicle, gearbox design and manufacturer's approved towing limits.

Before towing a caravan or trailer, check the vehicle handbook, approved towing capacity and any manufacturer restrictions. Never assume a CVT car can tow the same weight as a similar model fitted with a different gearbox.

6. Heat and transmission fluid problems

Conventional CVTs depend on the correct transmission fluid to provide lubrication, cooling and suitable operating characteristics. Some designs also use hydraulic pressure to control pulley operation.

Incorrect fluid, low fluid levels, leaks, overheating or neglected servicing can contribute to poor operation and internal wear.

Warning signs may include delayed response, unusual noises, shuddering, reduced performance or a gearbox temperature warning.

See our related guides: Automatic Gearbox Overheating Symptoms and Automatic Gearbox Oil Leaks.

7. Reliability and lifespan can vary significantly

There is no reliable rule that every CVT lasts fewer miles than every traditional automatic. Some conventional CVTs develop expensive problems, while others remain dependable with appropriate maintenance. Hybrid e-CVT systems also have different internal designs and should be assessed separately.

A CVT's lifespan depends on its design, manufacturing quality, service history, driving conditions, cooling performance and whether developing faults are repaired promptly.

For used car buyers, a documented service history and a healthy test drive are more useful than assuming that a particular mileage automatically means the gearbox is worn out.

CVT warning signs

Signs Your CVT Gearbox Is Failing

CVT problems do not always appear suddenly. Some begin with a small change in acceleration, an occasional vibration or a warning light that comes and goes.

Recognising these symptoms early can help prevent further damage. However, none of the signs below proves that the CVT itself has failed. A proper inspection is needed to identify the cause.

1. Shuddering or juddering

The vehicle shakes, vibrates or repeatedly surges when pulling away or accelerating. It may feel similar to a manual car with a juddering clutch.

Pay attention to whether the problem occurs when the gearbox is cold, once it is warm, during gentle acceleration or when climbing hills. These details can help a garage narrow down the fault.

2. Hesitation when accelerating

You press the accelerator, but the car takes longer than expected to respond. It may pause before accelerating or feel unusually sluggish.

Possible causes include transmission control problems, hydraulic issues or engine-related faults. A delay should not be dismissed simply because the car eventually moves.

3. Slipping or poor power transfer

The engine speed rises, but the vehicle does not gain speed as expected. This can indicate a problem transferring power through the transmission.

Remember that a healthy CVT can hold steady or high engine revs during acceleration. Genuine slipping is more concerning when acceleration is weak, inconsistent or accompanied by other symptoms.

4. Whining, buzzing or grinding noises

A new or increasingly loud whining noise may indicate a transmission-related problem. Grinding or harsh mechanical noises are more concerning and should be investigated promptly.

Try to notice whether the sound changes with vehicle speed, engine speed or acceleration. These observations can help distinguish gearbox noise from wheel bearings or other components.

5. Jerky or uneven acceleration

A CVT is generally designed to deliver smooth acceleration. Repeated jerking, surging or sudden changes in drive can therefore be a warning sign.

Some CVTs simulate gear changes, so a small programmed change in engine speed is not necessarily a fault. New, harsh or unpredictable jerking is different and should be checked.

6. Gearbox warning light or dashboard message

Some vehicles display a transmission warning symbol, temperature warning or message when a problem is detected. Others may illuminate the engine management light because the engine and gearbox control systems communicate.

A warning light does not automatically mean the gearbox needs replacing. It means the vehicle has detected a condition that requires attention.

For further information, read Automatic Gearbox Warning Light and Automatic Gearbox Temperature Warning Light.

7. Delayed engagement or loss of drive

The car may hesitate after selecting Drive or Reverse, struggle to move or unexpectedly stop transferring power to the wheels.

These symptoms can indicate a serious problem. Do not try to overcome them by repeatedly pressing the accelerator or switching rapidly between Drive and Reverse.

See Automatic Gearbox Delay Selecting Drive and Automatic Gearbox Won't Change Gear for related guidance.

Transmission comparison

CVT vs Traditional Automatic vs Manual Gearbox

Choosing the right gearbox depends on how you drive, what you expect from the car and how much you are comfortable spending on maintenance and possible repairs.

A CVT, a traditional automatic and a manual gearbox can all be good choices. None is automatically the most reliable, economical or affordable in every vehicle.

Which gearbox is best for city driving?

A CVT can be an excellent choice for city driving because it removes the need for manual gear changes and can deliver smooth progress in traffic.

However, a conventional automatic can be equally convenient. The better choice depends on how each particular car behaves during a test drive.

Which gearbox is best for motorway driving?

Both CVTs and traditional automatics can work well on motorways. At steady speeds, a CVT may maintain an efficient engine operating point, while a traditional automatic may offer a more familiar response when accelerating or overtaking.

If motorway driving makes up most of your mileage, compare cabin noise, acceleration response and real-world fuel economy rather than choosing solely by gearbox type.

Which gearbox is best for towing?

The best transmission for towing is the one approved by the manufacturer for the required load. Some CVT-equipped cars have restrictive towing limits, while others are designed to tow within specified capacities.

A traditional automatic may be preferable in certain towing applications, but gearbox type alone does not determine towing suitability. Check the exact vehicle's approved limits and operating instructions.

Which gearbox is cheapest to own?

There is no universal winner. A manual gearbox may have lower routine transmission servicing costs, but clutch replacement can be expensive. A conventional automatic or CVT may cost more to repair if major internal components fail.

Compare the exact model's service requirements, known reliability issues, insurance, fuel economy and repair estimates before making a decision.

Best buying approach:

Choose the car and transmission combination that suits your driving needs, not simply the gearbox type with the best reputation. A well-maintained CVT with a clear service history may be a better purchase than a neglected traditional automatic.

CVT maintenance and ownership

CVT Gearbox Servicing & Running Costs UK

Regular maintenance is one of the most important parts of owning a car with a conventional CVT gearbox. The transmission depends on the correct fluid, suitable operating temperatures and properly functioning internal components.

A CVT can feel perfectly normal even when its servicing history is incomplete. Unfortunately, that does not mean the gearbox has been maintained correctly. Poor fluid condition, incorrect servicing procedures and ignored leaks can contribute to expensive problems later.

However, servicing requirements vary considerably between manufacturers and gearbox designs. A conventional Nissan or Subaru CVT should not automatically be serviced in the same way as a Toyota hybrid e-CVT.

How often should CVT gearbox fluid be changed?

There is no single fluid-change interval that applies to every CVT gearbox. Some manufacturers specify mileage or time-based replacement intervals, while others recommend inspections, condition-based servicing or different schedules for demanding use.

Depending on the vehicle and manufacturer, you may encounter servicing recommendations around 30,000–60,000 miles. This is a broad planning range, not a universal service interval.

The correct approach is to check the official maintenance schedule for the exact vehicle, model year and transmission. Driving conditions such as frequent towing, heavy traffic, steep hills or repeated high-load use may affect the manufacturer's recommendations.

Do not follow a generic CVT fluid-change interval blindly

Always confirm the manufacturer's specification and procedure before changing CVT fluid. Using the wrong fluid or following an unsuitable filling procedure can cause poor operation and potentially serious transmission damage. Hybrid e-CVT systems may have different fluid and servicing requirements.

Why CVT gearbox fluid matters

Transmission fluid does more than lubricate moving parts. In many conventional CVTs, it also helps with cooling, hydraulic control and the operating characteristics needed by the belt or chain and pulley system.

As fluid ages, its condition can change. Excessive heat, contamination and mechanical wear can also affect how well the gearbox operates.

Lubrication

Correct fluid helps protect bearings, gears and other moving components from excessive friction and wear.

Temperature control

Transmission fluid helps carry heat away from working components. Excessive heat can accelerate fluid deterioration.

Transmission operation

Many conventional CVTs depend on carefully controlled hydraulic pressure and fluid characteristics for correct operation.

How much does a CVT gearbox service cost in the UK?

The price depends on the vehicle, fluid specification, quantity required, whether a filter or strainer is serviced, and the labour involved. Some transmissions also require specialist equipment to set the correct fluid level at a specified temperature.

The following are indicative UK budgeting ranges, not fixed quotations. Main dealers, specialist transmission workshops and independent garages may charge different amounts.

What happens if you skip CVT servicing?

Ignoring required maintenance can increase the risk of transmission problems, particularly where fluid condition and temperature are important to the gearbox's operation.

  • Fluid deterioration: old or overheated fluid may no longer provide the intended operating characteristics.
  • Increased wear: poor lubrication or contamination can contribute to damage inside the transmission.
  • Juddering or hesitation: some CVTs may develop poor engagement or inconsistent acceleration.
  • Overheating: fluid and cooling-related problems can affect transmission temperature control.
  • Expensive repairs: continued use with a developing fault may increase the extent of internal damage.

However, changing the fluid is not a guaranteed cure for an already damaged gearbox. If the transmission is slipping, making harsh mechanical noises or losing drive, a proper diagnosis is needed before deciding whether servicing is appropriate.

Should you change the fluid in a high-mileage CVT?

High mileage alone does not automatically mean a fluid change is unsafe. The correct decision depends on the gearbox's condition, maintenance history, manufacturer's instructions and whether existing symptoms suggest internal damage.

If a used car has no evidence of CVT maintenance, ask a transmission specialist to assess it rather than immediately ordering an aggressive flush or assuming fresh fluid will solve every problem.

Mechanic insight:

Keep invoices showing the exact transmission fluid used, the mileage, date and work completed. A general service stamp does not prove that the gearbox fluid was changed. Detailed records are particularly valuable when selling or buying a used CVT car.

Other CVT running costs to consider

CVT ownership costs are not limited to gearbox servicing. Fuel consumption, insurance, road tax, tyres, general maintenance and possible transmission repairs all contribute to the total cost of running the vehicle.

Before buying, compare the exact car's official fuel consumption, manufacturer service schedule and available warranty cover. A low purchase price can quickly become less attractive if a major gearbox repair is needed.

UK transmission repair prices

CVT Gearbox Repair & Replacement Costs UK

One of the biggest concerns about buying a CVT-equipped car is the potential cost of repairs. Minor electrical faults or external leaks may be relatively affordable, but serious internal damage can cost thousands of pounds.

The price depends on the transmission design, vehicle model, availability of replacement parts, labour time and whether the gearbox can be repaired or must be replaced.

Some conventional CVTs are supported by specialist rebuilders, while others may be more economical to replace with a reconditioned or exchange unit. Toyota-style hybrid e-CVT systems have different components and repair requirements, so conventional CVT belt-replacement estimates should not be applied to them.

Important repair-cost reality

If a conventional CVT has suffered severe internal belt, chain or pulley damage, replacing one damaged part may not be enough. Metal debris and secondary damage can affect other components. A specialist may recommend a complete rebuild or replacement rather than a cheaper partial repair.

Why are CVT gearbox repairs sometimes expensive?

Specialist components

Internal CVT parts must work within precise operating limits. Some components are expensive or not supplied separately.

Specialist labour

Internal diagnosis and rebuilding may require transmission-specific tools, technical information and experience.

Secondary damage

A major internal failure can damage multiple components, increasing the cost beyond the original fault.

Can a CVT gearbox belt be replaced on its own?

Sometimes, but it depends on the gearbox design, availability of parts and the condition of the pulleys and other internal components.

If the belt or chain has slipped badly or suffered mechanical damage, the specialist must check for scoring, wear and contamination elsewhere in the transmission.

A belt-only repair may not be suitable if the pulleys, bearings or hydraulic components are also damaged. In some cases, a complete reconditioned unit offers a more practical repair.

Is it worth repairing a CVT gearbox?

It can be, particularly if the car is otherwise in good condition and the repair comes with a suitable warranty. However, spending several thousand pounds on a low-value vehicle may not make financial sense.

Consider the car's current market value, mileage, maintenance history, other upcoming repairs and how long you intend to keep it.

Before approving major work, ask for a written diagnosis and itemised quotation. It should explain what has failed, what is being replaced, whether the price includes VAT and labour, and what warranty applies.

Questions to ask before approving a CVT repair

  • Has the fault been confirmed through proper testing rather than fault codes alone?
  • Is the problem electrical, hydraulic, mechanical or related to another vehicle system?
  • Does the quotation include removal, fitting, fluid, VAT and any programming?
  • Is the replacement gearbox new, used, reconditioned or fully rebuilt?
  • What warranty is included, and what exclusions apply?
  • Will the garage investigate the cause of the original failure before fitting another gearbox?
  • Could a second opinion from a transmission specialist change the repair decision?
Money-saving advice:

Do not approve a complete gearbox replacement solely because a dashboard warning light is on. Some symptoms are caused by wiring, sensors, fluid leaks or other faults. Equally, do not repeatedly pay for fluid changes or fault-code clearing when internal damage has already been confirmed.

Used CVT car buying advice

Should You Buy a Car With a CVT Gearbox?

Yes, a CVT-equipped car can be a good purchase. A healthy, properly maintained CVT may offer smooth driving, everyday convenience and competitive fuel economy.

However, buying the wrong car with a neglected or already failing transmission can be expensive. The key is to assess the exact gearbox design, vehicle history and condition rather than deciding that every CVT is good or bad.

Which CVT brands are more reliable?

Reliability varies by manufacturer, gearbox generation, vehicle model and maintenance history. It is not accurate to give every vehicle from one brand the same reliability rating.

Toyota and Lexus hybrids

Many use power-split e-CVT systems rather than conventional belt-and-pulley CVTs. These are mechanically different and should be researched separately for the exact hybrid model.

Honda CVT models

Honda has used CVT technology in various vehicles. Check the exact transmission, maintenance requirements and any model-specific reliability concerns before buying.

Nissan and Subaru CVT models

Both have used conventional CVT designs across different vehicles. Reliability can vary by generation, so model-specific research and documented servicing are important.

Do not assume that a particular brand guarantees reliability or that another brand should always be avoided. Check the exact year, engine, gearbox and service history. A specialist familiar with that model may be able to identify known issues and inspection priorities.

1. Check the exact gearbox fitted

Start by confirming whether the vehicle uses a conventional CVT, a hybrid e-CVT or another automatic transmission entirely.

Sellers sometimes use the word automatic without identifying the actual gearbox type. Check the manufacturer specification, vehicle documentation and exact model details before making assumptions.

2. Check the service history carefully

Look for evidence that the transmission has been maintained according to the manufacturer's requirements. Where fluid changes are specified, ask for invoices confirming the date, mileage, correct fluid and work completed.

Missing service records do not automatically prove the gearbox is damaged, but they increase uncertainty. This matters more when the vehicle is high mileage or the particular transmission has known maintenance sensitivities.

3. Start the engine and test the gearbox from cold

A cold start can reveal symptoms that may become less obvious once the vehicle warms up.

  • Check whether any gearbox or engine warning lights remain illuminated.
  • Listen for unusual whining, rattling or grinding noises.
  • With the brake pedal firmly applied, check that Drive and Reverse engage normally.
  • Look for excessive delay, harsh engagement or unexpected movement.
  • Check beneath the parked vehicle for signs of fluid leaks.

Do not rev the engine aggressively while selecting gears. Follow the manufacturer's normal operating instructions.

4. Carry out a proper test drive

A short drive around a car park is not enough to assess a used CVT properly. Where safe and permitted, test the car on a mixture of slower roads and normal driving routes.

1. Pull away gently

Check for smooth movement without repeated juddering, hesitation or unusual vibration.

2. Accelerate normally

Confirm that the car gains speed consistently without unexplained slipping or surging.

3. Drive when warm

Check whether symptoms appear after the gearbox reaches normal operating temperature.

4. Check warning messages

Watch for transmission warnings, overheating messages or sudden reductions in performance.

5. Understand normal CVT behaviour

A CVT may allow engine revs to remain steady while road speed increases. Some models also produce a noticeable engine drone under firm acceleration.

These characteristics are not automatically faults. The warning signs are unexpected loss of performance, harsh juddering, inconsistent drive, new mechanical noises or warning lights.

6. Arrange an independent inspection

If you are unsure about the gearbox, arrange an inspection by a competent independent garage or transmission specialist before buying.

A useful inspection may include checking the service history, looking for leaks, scanning relevant control modules, reviewing available fault information and assessing transmission behaviour during a suitable road test.

A basic fault-code scan cannot guarantee that the gearbox is healthy. Some mechanical problems may not trigger a warning light, and recently cleared codes can make diagnosis more difficult.

What about buying a high-mileage CVT car?

High mileage does not automatically mean a CVT is near failure. A properly maintained car that has covered mainly steady motorway miles may be in better condition than a lower-mileage vehicle that has suffered poor maintenance or repeated overheating.

However, higher mileage can increase the importance of checking maintenance records, transmission behaviour and the likely cost of future repairs.

If the gearbox has already been replaced or rebuilt, ask for the invoice, details of the work and any transferable warranty. A replacement gearbox is only reassuring when the repair was carried out properly and the original cause of failure was addressed.

  • Compare potential repair costs with the purchase price and vehicle value.
  • Walk away if serious symptoms remain unexplained or the seller prevents proper checks.
Never buy based only on a cleared warning light

A seller who says the gearbox warning light was simply reset has not demonstrated that the underlying fault was repaired. Ask for evidence of diagnosis and completed work. If the transmission still judders, slips or behaves unpredictably, do not assume the problem is minor.

Dealer vs private seller — why it matters

Buying from a motor trader and buying privately involve different legal protections in the UK. When buying from a trader, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 provides important rights concerning the vehicle's condition, description and fitness for purpose, taking account of factors such as age, mileage and price.

Private sales generally provide fewer protections. The car must still match its description, but you should be particularly careful about checking condition and obtaining independent advice before paying.

A good CVT car should drive smoothly, respond predictably and have maintenance records appropriate to its exact transmission. Do not reject every CVT automatically, but do not overlook warning signs simply because the purchase price looks attractive.

Frequently Asked Questions

CVT Gearbox FAQs

These are some of the most common questions UK drivers ask about CVT gearbox reliability, maintenance, driving behaviour, repair costs and buying used cars.

Is a CVT gearbox reliable?

A CVT gearbox can be reliable, but durability varies by manufacturer, model, transmission design and maintenance history. Some conventional CVTs have experienced expensive internal faults, while others provide dependable service. Hybrid e-CVT systems may use a different mechanical design and should not automatically be grouped with belt-driven CVTs.

How long does a CVT gearbox last?

There is no guaranteed CVT lifespan. Some transmissions provide many years and high mileages of service, while others develop problems earlier. Lifespan depends on gearbox design, maintenance, operating conditions, overheating and whether faults are addressed promptly.

Are CVT gearboxes expensive to repair?

Major CVT gearbox repairs can be expensive, particularly when internal belts, chains, pulleys or other mechanical components are damaged. Minor electrical or external faults may cost considerably less. The final price depends on the exact transmission and repair required.

How often should CVT gearbox fluid be changed?

CVT fluid-change requirements vary by manufacturer, vehicle and transmission design. Some vehicles have specified service intervals, while others use different inspection or maintenance schedules. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions and use the correct approved fluid.

Why does my CVT gearbox whine when accelerating?

Some CVTs produce a noticeable operating sound during acceleration, and a steady engine note can be normal. However, a new or worsening whine may indicate bearing, hydraulic or internal transmission problems. A professional inspection is advisable if the noise is unusual or accompanied by poor performance.

Is CVT juddering a sign of gearbox failure?

Juddering can indicate a CVT-related problem, but it does not prove that the gearbox has failed. Possible causes include fluid problems, hydraulic faults, internal wear, engine misfires and other drivetrain issues. Correct diagnosis is necessary before replacing transmission parts.

Is a CVT better than a normal automatic gearbox?

Neither gearbox type is universally better. A CVT can provide smooth acceleration and efficient operation, while a traditional automatic may offer a more familiar driving feel. Reliability, fuel economy, servicing and repair costs depend on the specific vehicle and transmission design.

Is a Toyota hybrid e-CVT the same as a conventional CVT?

No. Many Toyota hybrid e-CVT systems use a power-split arrangement rather than the metal belt and adjustable pulleys found in many conventional CVTs. Their mechanical construction, operating principles and potential faults are different.

Can you drive with a slipping CVT gearbox?

Continued driving with genuine transmission slipping can cause further damage and may lead to loss of drive. Avoid unnecessary use and arrange professional diagnosis. If the vehicle loses drive, behaves unpredictably or displays a serious overheating warning, stop safely and arrange recovery.

Should I buy a used car with a CVT gearbox?

A used CVT car can be a sensible purchase if the transmission operates correctly, the maintenance history is appropriate and there are no unexplained warning signs. Test the car from cold and when warm, research the exact gearbox generation and consider an independent inspection before buying.

Final Thoughts

Is a CVT Gearbox the Right Choice for You?

CVT gearboxes offer a different driving experience from traditional automatic and manual transmissions. Their smooth acceleration and automatic convenience can make them particularly appealing for urban journeys, commuting and everyday family motoring.

However, potential repair costs, specialist servicing requirements and differences between gearbox designs mean buyers should research the exact vehicle rather than rely on general opinions about CVT reliability.

If you already own a CVT-equipped car, following the correct maintenance schedule and investigating unusual symptoms promptly are sensible ways to reduce avoidable risks. If you are buying used, a documented service history, proper test drive and independent inspection can help you make a more informed decision.
