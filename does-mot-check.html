Does MOT Check? Complete UK MOT Inspection Checklist Explained

Many UK drivers wonder whether an MOT checks wheel alignment, tracking, wheel balancing, clutch wear, gearbox condition, engine oil, service history, air conditioning or other mechanical problems. Understanding what the MOT does—and does not—inspect can help you prepare your vehicle properly and avoid unnecessary MOT failures.

An MOT is a legal roadworthiness inspection designed to confirm that safety-critical systems meet the minimum standards required for use on UK roads. It is not a full mechanical inspection, vehicle service or diagnostic health check, meaning many faults can exist even when a vehicle passes its MOT.

This complete guide explains exactly what MOT testers inspect, which items are not checked, when non-tested components can still cause an MOT failure, common driver misconceptions, advisory risks, repair priorities and links to our complete Does MOT Check? knowledge centre.

Quick answer

What does an MOT check?

An MOT checks whether your vehicle meets minimum UK road safety and environmental standards on the day of the test. The tester inspects safety-critical areas such as brakes, tyres, steering, suspension, lights, seatbelts, mirrors, registration plates, windscreen condition, wipers, washers, vehicle structure, exhaust condition and emissions.

The MOT does not prove that the vehicle is mechanically perfect. A car can pass an MOT while still needing servicing, clutch work, gearbox repairs, wheel alignment, tracking, wheel balancing, air conditioning repair or future maintenance.

The simplest way to understand the MOT is this: it checks legal roadworthiness, not full vehicle health.

Quick rule

If a fault affects tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, lights, visibility, emissions, structure or safety systems, it can matter during an MOT. If it is routine servicing, comfort, future reliability or maintenance history, it is usually outside the MOT scope.

Best answer

What does an MOT not check?

An MOT does not normally check wheel alignment measurements, tracking adjustment, wheel balancing, clutch wear, service history, engine oil quality, gearbox condition, air conditioning performance, fuel economy, future reliability or whether the car has been properly serviced.

However, some non-tested items can still matter indirectly. Bad alignment is not measured, but illegal tyre wear caused by bad alignment can fail. Oil quality is not checked, but a serious oil leak can fail. Clutch wear is not measured, but a fault affecting safe control may be noticed.

Important distinction

A passed MOT means the vehicle met minimum standards at the time of the test. It does not guarantee good service history, good gearbox condition, correct tracking, balanced wheels or future reliability.

Not Directly Checked

What Does An MOT Not Check?

Many drivers assume an MOT is a complete mechanical inspection. In reality, several important maintenance and reliability items are not directly inspected. Some can still affect the MOT indirectly if they create safety defects or cause other components to fail.

Important

An MOT pass confirms that a vehicle met the minimum legal roadworthiness standards on the day of the inspection. It does not confirm the vehicle has been serviced correctly, is mechanically perfect or will remain reliable after the test.

Wheel Alignment

Wheel alignment is one of the most misunderstood parts of the MOT. The tester does not measure alignment angles or adjust the wheels during the inspection. However, poor alignment can cause uneven tyre wear, steering pull and suspension-related symptoms that may affect the MOT if they create safety defects.

Direct vs Indirect

Directly Checked vs Indirectly Affected During An MOT

Some faults are not checked directly, but the damage they cause can still lead to an MOT advisory or failure. This is one of the most important distinctions for UK drivers to understand.

Directly Checked

The MOT checks the safety defect itself

Direct MOT checks are items the tester inspects as part of the legal roadworthiness test. These include tyres, brakes, lights, steering, suspension, visibility, emissions, seatbelts, structure and other safety-related systems.

  • Illegal tyre tread depth
  • Brake performance or brake fluid leaks
  • Suspension or steering play
  • Lighting faults
  • Excessive emissions or smoke
  • Structural corrosion in important areas
Can directly affect MOT result
Indirectly Affected

The MOT does not check the cause, but it may check the result

Some problems are not MOT test items by themselves, but they can create defects that are testable. Poor tracking, bad alignment, old engine oil, clutch wear or gearbox issues may still matter if they affect roadworthiness.

  • Poor tracking causing tyre edge wear
  • Wheel imbalance pointing to tyre or bearing faults
  • Oil leaks affecting safety or emissions
  • Clutch or gearbox faults affecting safe control
  • Air conditioning faults affecting demisting or visibility
  • Poor maintenance causing warning lights or emissions issues
Can matter through related defects
Tracking Explained

Wheel Tracking Compared With The MOT Inspection

Wheel tracking is one of the most misunderstood parts of an MOT. The tester does not measure toe angles or carry out wheel tracking adjustments during the inspection. However, poor tracking can create uneven tyre wear, steering pull and handling problems that may eventually affect the MOT if they lead to safety-related defects.

Mechanic's Rule

The MOT usually tests the roadworthiness result, not the maintenance cause

For example, the MOT does not measure wheel alignment. But if poor alignment has worn the inside edge of a tyre below the legal limit, the tyre can fail. The fail item is the tyre, not the alignment setting.

The same logic applies to engine oil. Oil quality is not tested, but a serious oil leak can fail or receive an advisory. Service history is not checked, but poor servicing can lead to emissions problems, warning lights, brake faults or unsafe tyres.

Ask this before the MOT

Could this problem affect braking, steering, tyres, lights, visibility, emissions, vehicle structure, safety systems or general roadworthiness? If yes, it may still matter even if the exact item is not directly tested.

MOT Myths

Common Myths About What An MOT Checks

Many drivers misunderstand what an MOT actually proves. Passing an MOT confirms that a vehicle met the minimum legal roadworthiness standards on the day of the inspection—it does not guarantee perfect mechanical condition or future reliability.

Myth #1

A Passed MOT Means The Car Is Mechanically Perfect

False. A vehicle can pass its MOT while still needing servicing, tyres in the near future, brake repairs, clutch replacement, gearbox work or other routine maintenance. The MOT is not a full mechanical inspection.

An MOT pass is not a mechanical health certificate.
Myth #2

The MOT Checks Service History

False. MOT testers do not inspect service books, maintenance invoices or stamped service records. These remain important when buying a used vehicle but are outside the MOT inspection.

Myth #3

Tracking And Wheel Alignment Are Tested

False. The MOT does not measure wheel tracking or alignment angles. However, poor alignment can cause illegal tyre wear or steering defects that may result in an MOT failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does MOT Check? Frequently Asked Questions

Clear answers to the questions UK drivers most commonly ask about what is and is not checked during an MOT inspection.

What does an MOT check?

An MOT checks safety and environmental items including brakes, tyres, steering, suspension, lights, seatbelts, mirrors, windscreen, wipers, washers, registration plates, exhaust condition, emissions and vehicle structure.

Does MOT check wheel alignment?

No. The MOT does not measure wheel alignment angles, although poor alignment can lead to tyre wear or steering defects that affect the MOT.

Does MOT check tracking?

No. Tracking is not measured during an MOT, but poor tracking can cause illegal tyre wear that may lead to failure.

Does MOT check wheel balancing?

No. Wheel balancing is not tested with balancing equipment. However, vibration caused by damaged tyres, wheels or suspension should always be investigated.

Does MOT check clutch?

Normal clutch wear is not inspected. The clutch only becomes relevant if a fault prevents safe control of the vehicle during the test.

Does MOT check service history?

No. Service books, maintenance records and invoices are not inspected during the MOT.

Does MOT check air conditioning?

Usually no. Air conditioning performance is not normally part of the MOT unless a related visibility issue exists.

Does MOT check engine oil?

Oil quality is not checked, but excessive oil leaks, smoke or emissions-related faults may affect the MOT.

Does MOT check gearbox?

Internal gearbox wear is not assessed during an MOT, although serious leaks or unsafe operation may become relevant.

Does a passed MOT mean the car is reliable?

No. An MOT confirms only that the vehicle met minimum legal roadworthiness standards on the day of the inspection.

Does MOT check suspension?

Yes. Springs, shock absorbers, bushes, ball joints, suspension arms and mountings are inspected for excessive wear, damage or insecurity.

Does MOT check brakes?

Yes. The MOT includes brake performance testing together with inspection of brake discs, pads, pipes, hoses, fluid leaks and parking brake operation.

Does MOT check warning lights?

Yes, where applicable. Warning lights for systems such as ABS, airbags, electronic stability control, brake systems and emissions can affect the MOT result.

Can poor maintenance fail an MOT?

Not directly. However, poor maintenance often leads to tyre wear, brake defects, suspension wear, oil leaks or warning lights that can result in an MOT advisory or failure.

Should I service my car before an MOT?

A service is not compulsory before an MOT, but it can identify worn brakes, tyres, suspension components, leaks and other faults before the inspection.

What is the most common reason cars fail an MOT?

Lighting faults, tyre defects, suspension wear, brake problems and poor visibility are among the most common reasons vehicles fail the MOT each year.
