Quick answer

Is a Brake Pad Advisory Serious?

A brake pad advisory should be treated as a maintenance warning rather than ignored simply because the vehicle passed its MOT. The tester has identified pad wear or deterioration worth recording, but the condition did not meet the relevant rejection criteria at that time.

How urgent it is depends on the amount of friction material remaining, whether the pads are wearing evenly, the condition of the discs and whether another brake fault is accelerating the wear.

What does the advisory mean?

The pads showed developing wear or deterioration, but the tester did not classify the observed condition as a Major or Dangerous pad defect during that MOT.

When does pad wear fail?

Under the current MOT manual, a brake pad worn down to its wear indicator is a Major defect. A pad worn below 1.5 mm is Dangerous.

What should you do next?

Have the pads inspected properly, compare inner and outer wear, inspect the opposite side of the axle and check the discs, calipers and sliders before deciding how soon replacement is needed.

Brake wear warning light and MOT pad failure are not the same thing

The current DVSA inspection manual specifically states that an illuminated brake wear indicator is not itself a reason for MOT failure. The actual pad condition is what matters. A pad worn down to the wear indicator is a Major defect, while a pad below 1.5 mm is Dangerous.

Mechanic insight

Never assess pad life from the visible outer pad alone. On a floating caliper, the inner pad can sometimes wear substantially faster because of slider or piston problems. A meaningful inspection compares the inner and outer pads as well as the brakes on the opposite side of the axle.

Grinding means the situation has moved beyond a routine advisory

Grinding or heavy scraping can indicate extremely low friction material or metal contact with the disc. Continuing to drive can damage the disc and turn a straightforward pad replacement into a larger brake repair.

Advisory meaning

What Does a Brake Pad Advisory Mean?

A brake pad advisory means the MOT tester identified pad wear or deterioration worth recording, but the condition did not meet the relevant rejection criteria at the time of the test.

The exact meaning depends on what the tester saw. One vehicle may have evenly worn pads with useful friction material remaining, while another may have one inner pad wearing much faster because a caliper slider or piston is not moving correctly.

Developing wear

The pad material is reducing through normal use and has reached a point where future replacement should be planned rather than ignored.

Uneven wear

One pad may be thinner than another because of restricted caliper movement, pad binding, hardware problems or another wheel-end fault.

Condition deterioration

Pads can also suffer cracking, crumbling, overheating, glazing or contamination even when friction material remains.

An advisory is not a measurement

MOT advisory wording does not necessarily tell you exactly how many millimetres of pad material remain. A proper workshop inspection is needed if you want to estimate replacement priority more accurately.

Read the advisory together with the MOT history

A first-time advisory may simply reflect normal wear. The same brake pad warning appearing repeatedly can suggest replacement has been delayed or that the underlying cause of abnormal wear has not been corrected.

Pad thickness

How Brake Pad Thickness Is Assessed

Brake pad condition is judged by the amount and condition of the friction material, not the total thickness of the pad and backing plate together. The steel backing plate is not usable friction material and should never be included when estimating remaining pad life.

Mechanics normally compare the inner and outer pads on each wheel, then compare both sides of the axle. Large differences often reveal a mechanical problem rather than simple normal wear.

Friction material

This is the consumable material bonded to the backing plate. It becomes thinner every time the brakes are applied.

Backing plate

The steel backing plate supports the friction material but is not part of the usable wear thickness.

Wear indicator

Some pads use a mechanical or electronic wear indicator to warn when the friction material has reduced to a defined point.

Inner pad

The inner pad is often harder to see through the wheel and can wear differently from the outer pad if caliper movement is restricted.

Outer pad

The outer pad may appear healthy while the inner pad is much thinner, which is why visual inspection through the wheel can be misleading.

Axle comparison

Pads on the left and right sides should be compared to identify unusual differences in braking load or component operation.

Workshop replacement limits vs MOT thresholds

Workshop decision

Replacement before the legal minimum

A garage may recommend replacing pads before they reach the MOT rejection threshold because there may not be enough useful service life left for the driver's expected mileage or operating conditions.

MOT decision

Statutory rejection criteria

The MOT tester applies the current DVSA defect criteria. Pad wear down to the wear indicator is Major, while a pad worn below 1.5 mm is Dangerous.

Do not estimate pad life from one visible edge

Pad material can wear tapered or unevenly. Measuring or visually confirming only one edge can overestimate how much usable friction material remains elsewhere on the pad.

Wear patterns

Common Brake Pad Wear Patterns and What They Mean

Brake pad wear patterns provide useful diagnostic clues. Normal pads should generally wear progressively and reasonably evenly, while strongly tapered, one-sided or unusually rapid wear often points to a fault elsewhere in the brake assembly.

Even wear

Similar thickness across the pad and between inner and outer pads is usually consistent with normal braking operation.

Inner pad thinner

This can indicate restricted caliper release, piston problems or another condition keeping the inner pad in contact with the disc.

Outer pad thinner

Excess outer-pad wear can indicate slider or caliper movement problems on floating-caliper designs.

Tapered wear

One end of the pad being substantially thinner than the other can suggest restricted movement, misalignment or poor pad support.

Glazed pad surface

A hard, shiny friction surface can develop after overheating or repeated light braking and may contribute to noise or reduced friction quality.

Cracked or crumbling material

Friction material that is breaking apart or separating from the backing plate should not be treated as ordinary wear.

One side wearing faster

A large left-to-right difference can indicate unequal braking, contamination, caliper problems or another localised fault.

Rapid wear after replacement

New pads wearing unusually quickly can point to dragging brakes, incorrect fitting, unsuitable parts or an unresolved caliper fault.

Metal contact marks

If friction material has worn away enough for metal components to contact the disc, the repair priority becomes much higher and the disc may also be damaged.

Wear patterns are diagnostic evidence

The pattern can show that something is wrong, but it should not be used alone to condemn a specific component. Caliper operation, sliders, pad fit, disc condition, hoses and wheel-end temperature should be considered together.

Uneven wear

What Causes Uneven Brake Pad Wear?

Uneven brake pad wear is important because it often reveals a fault that will continue damaging new pads if it is not corrected. Simply replacing the worn friction material without checking the brake mechanism can allow the same pattern to return.

Seized slider pins

A floating caliper must move freely on its sliders. Restricted movement can prevent equal clamping and release across the two pads.

Sticking caliper piston

A piston that does not retract correctly can keep one pad applied after the driver releases the brake pedal.

Pad binding in carrier

Corrosion, damaged hardware or poor fit can stop the pad sliding freely in its carrier and create uneven wear.

Damaged pad hardware

Clips, shims and support hardware help control pad position. Incorrect or damaged parts can affect movement and wear.

Disc surface condition

Heavy corrosion, deep scoring or uneven contact on the disc can make one pad or part of a pad wear differently.

Brake hose restriction

An internally damaged flexible hose can sometimes restrict fluid return and contribute to a brake that does not release properly.

Parking brake mechanism

On some rear brake systems, a sticking mechanical or electronic parking brake mechanism can contribute to dragging and accelerated pad wear.

Incorrect installation

Poorly fitted pads, incorrect hardware or unsuitable replacement parts can prevent normal movement and create uneven wear.

Different braking contribution

Vehicle stability systems and normal brake balance can create some variation, but a large difference still deserves mechanical investigation.

Uneven wear should not be dismissed as normal

Minor differences can occur, but a substantial mismatch between inner and outer pads or between left and right sides should prompt a check for restricted movement, overheating, contamination or another wheel-end fault.

Check the cause before fitting replacement pads

If a caliper or slider caused the original uneven wear, fitting new pads without correcting that problem can shorten the life of the new components and damage the discs.

Pad contamination

Brake Pad Contamination Explained

Brake pads rely on clean, dry friction material. Oil, grease, brake fluid or other contamination can reduce friction, create uneven braking and damage the pad material. The contamination source should always be identified rather than simply wiping the visible surface.

Brake fluid

A leaking caliper, wheel-end hydraulic component or other brake fault can contaminate the friction material and reduce braking effectiveness.

Grease

Lubricant applied incorrectly during servicing can reach the pad or disc friction surfaces and compromise braking.

Oil

Oil contamination can come from nearby mechanical leaks or careless maintenance and should be traced to its source.

Cleaning products

Unsuitable chemicals or residues left on friction surfaces can affect pad performance and should be avoided.

Contaminated disc

If the disc surface is contaminated, new pads can immediately pick up the same substance unless the underlying source is repaired and the brake is cleaned correctly.

Heat and glazing

Heavy overheating can alter the pad surface and produce glazing, which is different from liquid contamination but can still reduce friction quality.

Why contamination matters

Performance risk

Reduced friction

Contaminated pads may not generate the expected braking force, especially if one side of an axle is affected more than the other.

Diagnostic clue

Possible leak or service fault

Contamination often points to another problem such as a hydraulic leak, mechanical fluid leak or incorrect use of lubricant during previous brake work.

Contaminated brake pads are not a clean-and-forget problem

Once friction material has absorbed oil, grease or brake fluid, the safe repair may require replacement rather than surface cleaning. The contamination source must also be corrected before the brake is returned to service.

Current MOT relevance

Under the current MOT inspection criteria, a brake pad or lining contaminated with oil, grease or similar material is classified as a Major defect.

Warning signs

Symptoms That Can Accompany Worn Brake Pads

A brake pad advisory may exist without any noticeable driving symptoms. However, as pad wear progresses or another brake fault develops, the vehicle can begin producing noise, vibration, warning messages or changes in braking behaviour.

Symptoms are especially important because an MOT result only records the vehicle's condition at the time of the test. A previous advisory does not tell you how much the pads have worn since then.

Squealing or squeaking

A persistent high-pitched noise can come from a mechanical wear indicator, glazing, vibration, corrosion or another pad-and-disc contact issue. Noise alone does not confirm how much pad material remains.

Grinding or scraping

Grinding is much more concerning. The friction material may be extremely low, or metal components may already be contacting and damaging the brake disc.

Brake wear warning

Vehicles fitted with electronic pad wear monitoring may display a dashboard warning or service message when the sensor reaches its designed wear point.

Pulling under braking

Pulling to one side can indicate unequal braking force caused by uneven pad wear, contamination, a sticking caliper or another braking fault.

Judder or vibration

Vibration through the pedal, steering wheel or vehicle body can indicate disc thickness variation, uneven friction deposits, corrosion or another brake or wheel-end problem.

Reduced braking response

If the vehicle requires noticeably more pedal effort or stopping distance increases, do not assume the problem is simply normal pad wear. The complete braking system needs assessment.

Hot wheel or burning smell

Excessive heat from one wheel can indicate a dragging pad, sticking caliper, restricted slider or parking brake fault rather than ordinary friction-material wear.

Changed pedal feel

A soft, spongy, unusually long or sinking pedal is not a normal symptom of simple brake pad wear and can indicate a hydraulic problem requiring prompt investigation.

No noticeable symptoms

Brake pads can be significantly worn while the vehicle still feels normal to the driver. That is why physical inspection remains important after an advisory.

Grinding changes the priority

If an advisory has progressed to grinding or harsh metal-on-metal noise, do not continue treating it as routine future maintenance. The pads may be extremely worn and the brake discs may already be suffering damage.

Do not diagnose brake condition from noise alone

Squealing does not automatically mean the pads are dangerously thin, and quiet brakes do not prove that plenty of friction material remains. Pad thickness and brake condition need to be inspected directly.

Workshop inspection

How a Mechanic Checks a Brake Pad Advisory

A proper brake pad inspection should establish more than whether the pads look thin. The technician should determine how much friction material remains, whether wear is even, what condition the discs are in and whether the caliper is applying and releasing correctly.

This is particularly important after an MOT advisory because the visible outer pad may not accurately represent the condition of the inner pad.

1. Read the MOT wording

Start with the exact advisory rather than assuming every brake pad advisory means the same thing. Note the axle, side and any related brake defects or advisories.

2. Inspect pad material

Check usable friction material on the inner and outer pads, including the thinnest visible area rather than one convenient edge.

3. Compare wear

Compare inner with outer pads and then compare the left and right sides of the axle for abnormal differences.

4. Inspect the discs

Check both disc faces for scoring, corrosion, pitting, excessive wear, heat damage and poor pad contact.

5. Check caliper movement

Inspect the piston, slider pins, carrier and pad movement for restriction that could cause dragging or uneven wear.

6. Check for contamination

Look for brake fluid, grease, oil or other material on the pads, discs and surrounding components, then trace the source.

7. Inspect hoses and hardware

Check flexible hoses, pad clips, shims, wear sensors and associated hardware where relevant to the vehicle.

8. Confirm brake operation

Where appropriate, assess pedal feel, brake release, wheel temperature and road-test behaviour to confirm that the braking system operates normally.

What should be recorded?

Remaining pad material

Ask for the measured or observed remaining friction material where access allows a reliable assessment.

Disc condition

Establish whether the existing discs are suitable to remain in service or whether pads and discs should be planned together.

Reason for abnormal wear

If wear is uneven, the inspection should identify the likely cause instead of treating the pads as the only faulty component.

Mechanic inspection rule

Compare the whole axle. A useful brake assessment looks at both pads on one wheel, the opposite wheel, both disc faces and caliper operation. Replacing the visibly thinnest pad without understanding the rest of the axle can miss the actual fault.

Never rely only on what can be seen through an alloy wheel

The outer pad may appear acceptable while the inner pad is considerably thinner. Wheel removal may be necessary for a reliable assessment, depending on brake design and visibility.

Why pads wear

What Causes Brake Pads to Wear Out?

Brake pads are designed to wear because friction material is consumed whenever the brakes slow the vehicle. The rate of wear, however, can change dramatically depending on driving conditions, vehicle weight, brake design and mechanical condition.

Normal wear usually develops progressively across the axle. Very rapid, tapered or one-sided wear deserves further investigation because it may indicate that the braking system is not operating correctly.

Normal friction wear

Every brake application removes a tiny amount of friction material. Eventually the pads reach the point where replacement is required.

Stop-start driving

Urban driving can consume pads faster because the brakes are used repeatedly for junctions, traffic lights, congestion and low-speed traffic.

Heavy vehicle weight

Heavier vehicles require more braking energy to slow down, which can increase friction-material and disc wear.

Frequent heavy braking

Late or aggressive braking creates greater heat and friction, potentially accelerating pad wear and affecting disc condition.

Hills and towing

Repeated braking on steep roads or when carrying additional load increases brake temperature and the amount of work required from the friction material.

Sticking caliper piston

A piston that does not retract correctly can leave a pad dragging against the disc after the pedal is released.

Seized or restricted sliders

Restricted floating-caliper movement can create unequal inner and outer pad wear and may also cause overheating.

Pad binding

Corrosion or damaged hardware around the carrier can prevent the pad moving freely and cause continuous or uneven disc contact.

Poor disc condition

Heavily scored, corroded or irregular disc surfaces can affect pad contact and contribute to abnormal friction-material wear.

Incorrect parts or fitting

Incorrect pad specification, poor-quality components or fitting errors can cause noise, poor movement and premature wear.

Parking brake problems

Rear brake wear can accelerate if a mechanical or electronic parking brake mechanism does not release correctly.

Brake system intervention

Stability and traction-control systems can apply individual wheel brakes during operation, which may contribute to different wear patterns on some vehicles.

Front pads often wear sooner — but not always

Front brakes normally handle a substantial proportion of braking load, so front pads commonly wear faster. Vehicle design, electronic brake control, parking-brake operation and mechanical faults can alter that pattern, so axle position alone should not be used to predict remaining life.

Fast pad wear needs a cause

If replacement pads become low again unusually quickly, investigate caliper release, sliders, pad fit, disc condition and driving conditions before fitting another set.

Diagnostic process

How to Diagnose a Brake Pad Advisory Properly

The purpose of diagnosis is not simply to confirm that a pad is worn. It is to determine whether the wear is normal, how urgent replacement is, whether the discs can remain in service and whether another fault caused the pad condition.

1. Confirm the advisory

Read the exact MOT wording and identify the affected axle or wheel where this is stated.

2. Inspect and compare

Assess inner and outer pad material, disc surfaces and the opposite side of the axle.

3. Find the cause

Decide whether the pattern represents normal wear or a problem such as binding pads, seized sliders, a sticking piston or contamination.

4. Choose the repair

Decide whether the vehicle needs monitoring, pads, pads and discs, or repair of another brake component before new friction parts are installed.

Typical diagnostic conclusions

Monitor

Wear is developing normally

Pad material remains serviceable, wear is reasonably even, discs are acceptable and there are no braking symptoms. Reinspection should still be planned rather than waiting automatically for the next MOT.

Replace

Pads are approaching replacement

Remaining material or operating conditions make continued use poor value or unnecessarily risky. Replace the appropriate axle set and inspect the discs at the same time.

Investigate

Wear pattern suggests another fault

Uneven wear, overheating, contamination or rapid deterioration indicates that the underlying brake fault should be identified and corrected as part of the repair.

Do not diagnose by mileage alone

Two vehicles with identical mileage can have very different pad condition. Remaining friction material, wear pattern, disc condition, caliper operation and actual driving use provide much better evidence than mileage by itself.

The cheapest correct repair is not always pads only

If the discs are unsuitable for reuse or a caliper is causing abnormal wear, fitting pads alone can create repeat labour and another early repair. Diagnose the complete wheel-end brake assembly before deciding which parts to replace.

Driving safety

Can You Drive With a Brake Pad Advisory?

A brake pad advisory does not automatically mean the vehicle must stop being driven. It means the pad condition did not meet the relevant MOT rejection criteria at the time of that test. Whether the vehicle is sensible or safe to continue using depends on its current condition, remaining pad material and any symptoms that have developed since.

Brake pads continue wearing after the MOT. The older the advisory and the more mileage covered since the test, the less useful the original result becomes for judging the brakes today.

Lower immediate risk

Inspection confirms useful material remains

Monitor and plan

Braking feels normal, wear is even, discs are serviceable and a competent inspection confirms the pads remain suitable for use. Plan a sensible recheck or replacement interval.

Increasing risk

Pads are low or wear is uncertain

Arrange repair soon

The advisory is old, mileage has increased significantly, pads are visibly low or their remaining life is uncertain. Arrange inspection rather than assuming they will last until the next MOT.

Urgent

Braking symptoms have developed

Do not delay diagnosis

Grinding, significantly reduced braking, abnormal pedal behaviour, severe pulling, overheating, a suspected hydraulic leak or another serious symptom requires prompt professional assessment.

Get the brakes checked promptly if you notice:

  • ✓ Grinding, harsh scraping or metal-on-metal brake noise.
  • ✓ A significant change in stopping performance.
  • ✓ The vehicle pulling strongly to one side during braking.
  • ✓ A soft, sinking or unusually long brake pedal.
  • ✓ One wheel becoming unusually hot or producing a burning smell.
  • ✓ A brake-system warning accompanied by braking symptoms.
  • ✓ Evidence of brake fluid leakage.
  • ✓ Rapid or strongly uneven brake pad wear.
A valid MOT is not a 12-month brake guarantee

An MOT records the vehicle's condition against the test requirements when it was inspected. Brake pads are wear components and can deteriorate substantially before the next test, particularly with high mileage, frequent town driving, heavy loads or a developing mechanical fault.

There is no universal safe mileage after an advisory

An advisory cannot reliably be converted into a fixed number of remaining miles. Pad thickness, wear pattern, vehicle use, disc condition and caliper operation must be considered together when deciding how soon replacement is required.

Best practical approach

If you do not know how much pad material remains, arrange an inspection rather than trying to make the pads last until a particular date or mileage. That gives you a repair decision based on their current physical condition rather than the age of the MOT advisory.

Current MOT rules

Brake Pad Advisory MOT Rules Explained

A brake pad advisory is not the same as an MOT failure. The tester records an advisory when deterioration is worth bringing to the driver's attention but the observed condition does not meet the relevant rejection criteria at the time of the test.

Once brake pad wear or condition reaches one of the current DVSA defect thresholds, the result changes from maintenance advice to an MOT defect.

Advisory territory

Developing pad wear

Inspect and plan

Pads showing developing wear may be recorded as an advisory when the condition does not meet the relevant Major or Dangerous rejection criteria.

MOT failure

Wear indicator reached

Major defect

A brake lining or pad worn down to its wear indicator is classified as a Major MOT defect.

Serious failure

Pad below 1.5 mm

Dangerous defect

A brake lining or pad worn below 1.5 mm is classified as Dangerous under the current MOT inspection criteria.

Wear sensor warning and MOT rejection are different

The current MOT manual specifically states that an illuminated brake wear indicator is not itself a reason for failure. The tester assesses the physical condition of the brake lining or pad against the applicable defect criteria.

Brake performance can fail separately

A vehicle can also fail because of braking performance, imbalance, binding or another brake-system defect even if the pad thickness itself is not the defect being recorded. Pad condition should therefore be considered as part of the complete braking system.

An advisory is not permission to use the pads until 1.5 mm

The MOT threshold is not a recommended maintenance target. A garage may correctly recommend replacement considerably earlier based on remaining service life, wear pattern, vehicle use and disc condition.

Replacement decision

When Should Brake Pads Be Replaced After an Advisory?

Brake pad replacement should be based on current physical condition rather than the advisory date alone. The useful decision is whether enough friction material remains for the vehicle's expected use and whether the pads, discs and calipers are operating normally.

Waiting until the pads reach the MOT failure threshold is not good maintenance practice. Earlier replacement can reduce the risk of disc damage, unexpected warning noises and an avoidable MOT failure.

Replace

Pad material is low

Replacement due

If inspection shows limited usable friction material remains, replacement should be planned based on current condition rather than trying to reach a particular mileage.

Replace

Wear indicator reached

Do not delay

Pads worn to the mechanical wear indicator have reached the current Major MOT defect threshold and should be replaced.

Replace

Pad below 1.5 mm

Dangerous condition

A pad below 1.5 mm meets the current Dangerous MOT defect criterion and should not be treated as normal future maintenance.

Investigate

Uneven wear

Find the cause

Strongly uneven inner-to-outer or left-to-right wear should prompt inspection of caliper movement, sliders, pad fit and associated brake components.

Replace and repair

Contamination present

Source must be repaired

Oil, grease or brake-fluid contamination can compromise friction material. The pads may require replacement and the contamination source must be corrected first.

Inspect discs

Grinding has started

Disc damage likely

Grinding can indicate severely depleted friction material and possible metal contact. Inspect the discs as well as the pads before ordering parts.

Replace, monitor or investigate?

Monitor

Monitoring may be reasonable when...

  • ✓ Useful pad material remains.
  • ✓ Inner and outer wear is reasonably even.
  • ✓ Both sides of the axle are behaving normally.
  • ✓ Discs remain suitable for service.
  • ✓ There are no significant braking symptoms.
Replace

Replacement is sensible when...

  • ✓ Pad material is approaching the end of useful life.
  • ✓ The wear indicator has been reached.
  • ✓ Pads are damaged, deteriorated or contaminated.
  • ✓ The discs are being renewed.
  • ✓ Expected mileage makes delaying poor value.
Investigate

Further diagnosis is needed when...

  • ✓ One pad is substantially thinner than another.
  • ✓ One wheel becomes unusually hot.
  • ✓ Pads wear out unusually quickly.
  • ✓ The vehicle pulls when braking.
  • ✓ Contamination or brake drag is present.
Do not fit new pads over an unresolved fault

A seized slider, sticking piston, binding pad or hydraulic problem can damage replacement pads quickly. The cause of abnormal wear should be corrected as part of the repair.

Pads and discs

Should Brake Pads and Discs Be Replaced Together?

Not every brake pad replacement requires new discs. If the existing discs remain suitable for service, have an acceptable friction surface and are free from defects requiring replacement, pads may be renewed without replacing the discs.

If the discs are significantly worn, heavily corroded, deeply scored, cracked, damaged or otherwise unsuitable for continued service, replacing pads alone is usually false economy.

Pads only

Discs remain serviceable

Pads may be sufficient

Pads can often be replaced alone when disc thickness and surface condition remain suitable and the brake assembly is operating correctly.

Pads + discs

Disc condition is poor

Replace together

Deep scoring, significant corrosion, excessive wear or other disc damage can justify renewing the discs and pads as one axle repair.

Repair first

Caliper fault present

Correct root cause

If the pads and discs have been damaged by dragging or uneven braking, the caliper or movement fault should be repaired before fitting fresh friction components.

Checks before fitting new pads

  • ✓ Inspect both faces of each brake disc.
  • ✓ Confirm disc condition is suitable for continued service.
  • ✓ Check the caliper piston operates and releases correctly.
  • ✓ Confirm slider pins move freely where fitted.
  • ✓ Clean and inspect pad contact points in the carrier.
  • ✓ Check clips, shims and wear sensors as applicable.
  • ✓ Investigate any contamination before fitting new pads.
  • ✓ Compare the opposite side of the axle.
New pads need a suitable mating surface

Bedding-in works best when the disc surface is suitable and the caliper can apply and release evenly. Correct installation matters as much as simply fitting new friction material.

Replace brake pads as an axle set

Brake pads are normally renewed on both wheels of the same axle so the left and right brakes use comparable friction material and wear condition. Follow the vehicle and component manufacturer's repair instructions.

DIY vs garage

Can You Replace Brake Pads Yourself?

Brake pad replacement can be within the ability of an experienced DIY mechanic, but it is a safety-critical repair. Correct procedures, suitable lifting equipment, torque information and an understanding of the vehicle's brake design are essential.

Experienced DIY

A competent DIY repair requires:

  • ✓ Safe lifting and axle support.
  • ✓ Correct replacement parts.
  • ✓ Vehicle-specific repair information.
  • ✓ Correct caliper piston retraction procedure.
  • ✓ Correct fastener and wheel-bolt torque.
  • ✓ Ability to inspect discs, sliders and calipers.
  • ✓ Understanding of bedding-in procedures.
Garage recommended

Professional repair is preferable when:

  • ✓ Pad wear is strongly uneven.
  • ✓ A caliper appears seized or leaking.
  • ✓ The discs are badly damaged.
  • ✓ Hydraulic work is required.
  • ✓ An electronic parking brake needs service-mode procedures.
  • ✓ A warning remains after replacement.
  • ✓ You cannot confidently verify correct brake operation afterwards.
Brakes are not a trial-and-error repair

If you are unsure about component condition, piston retraction, electronic parking brake procedures, hydraulic integrity or correct assembly, use a competent garage. Incorrect brake work can directly affect stopping performance.

Premium or performance braking system Specialist pads and possibly discs £400–£1,000+ Obtain vehicle-specific quote

What affects the final brake pad quote?

Vehicle make and model

Larger, premium and performance vehicles usually use more expensive brake components.

Front or rear axle

Parts and labour differ between axles and rear systems can involve electronic parking brake procedures.

Pad specification

Friction-material quality, manufacturer specification and aftermarket brand can materially change parts cost.

Disc condition

The bill rises substantially if worn, scored or corroded discs cannot remain in service.

Wear sensors

Some vehicles require a separate electronic pad-wear sensor when the pads are replaced.

Caliper faults

Restricted sliders, damaged pistons or leaking calipers can turn a routine pad job into a larger repair.

Compare like-for-like quotes

Ask whether the quote includes VAT, labour, both wheels on the axle, pad sensors, fitting hardware, disc inspection and any necessary caliper servicing. A cheaper headline price can exclude work included elsewhere.

Do not delay brake repairs simply to reach the next MOT

If inspection shows the pads require replacement now, repair them based on their present condition. Allowing them to wear into the discs can increase both safety risk and repair cost.

Next MOT risk

Will a Brake Pad Advisory Fail the Next MOT?

It can, but there is no automatic rule that a previous brake pad advisory becomes a failure at the next MOT. The result depends on the condition of the pads and braking system when the vehicle is tested again.

Brake pads continue to wear every time the vehicle is driven. If an advisory is left unresolved for many months or significant mileage, the remaining friction material may reduce enough to reach an MOT rejection criterion before the next test.

Lower risk

Advisory repaired

Best position

New pads have been fitted correctly, the discs remain serviceable and no related caliper or braking fault remains.

Increasing risk

Advisory left unresolved

Reinspect before MOT

The pads have continued to wear since the previous test and their present condition is unknown.

High risk

Symptoms have developed

Repair before relying on MOT

Grinding, overheating, uneven wear, contamination or reduced braking performance suggests the condition has progressed beyond a simple historic advisory.

What can turn the advisory into a failure?

Further friction-material wear

Continued use can reduce the pad until the wear indicator is reached or the friction material falls below the Dangerous threshold.

Contamination

Oil, grease or similar contamination can change the MOT outcome even where reasonable pad thickness remains.

Related brake fault

Binding, imbalance, poor braking performance or another component defect can produce a separate MOT failure.

Do not use the next MOT as the replacement deadline

The safest repair date is determined by current brake condition, not the expiry date of the MOT certificate. If the pads need replacing before then, delaying the work simply to reach the test can increase both repair cost and safety risk.

Best pre-MOT approach

If a brake pad advisory remains on the previous MOT history, arrange a brake inspection before the next test. That gives time to deal with worn pads, damaged discs or abnormal caliper wear before the vehicle is presented for inspection.

Used car buying

Should You Buy a Car With a Brake Pad Advisory?

A brake pad advisory on a used car is not automatically a reason to walk away. Brake pads are normal wear items. The important question is whether the advisory represents ordinary maintenance or evidence of wider brake neglect.

Check the current brake condition rather than relying only on the MOT record. Pads may have been replaced since the test, or they may have continued wearing without any repair.

Usually manageable

Normal pad wear only

Maintenance item

If the pads are simply approaching replacement and the discs, calipers and braking performance are otherwise healthy, the repair can usually be budgeted like routine maintenance.

Negotiate

Pads and discs are due

Larger repair bill

If the discs are also worn, corroded or damaged, obtain a repair estimate and include it when deciding what the vehicle is worth.

Investigate carefully

Wear is abnormal

Possible underlying fault

Strongly uneven pad wear, dragging brakes, overheating or repeated brake advisories can indicate caliper or maintenance problems beyond a routine pad replacement.

Used car brake pad checklist

  • ✓ Read the complete MOT history rather than only the latest result.
  • ✓ Check whether the same brake advisory appears repeatedly.
  • ✓ Ask whether the pads have been replaced since the advisory.
  • ✓ Ask for invoices or service records for recent brake repairs.
  • ✓ Check whether the discs were inspected or replaced at the same time.
  • ✓ Listen for squealing, scraping or grinding on the test drive.
  • ✓ Check for pulling, judder or abnormal pedal feel under braking.
  • ✓ Watch for brake-system or pad-wear warnings.
  • ✓ Check whether one wheel smells hot after the test drive.
  • ✓ Obtain a repair estimate before agreeing the purchase price.
Repeated advisories tell a story

One brake pad advisory can represent ordinary wear. The same advisory appearing across several MOT tests may indicate delayed maintenance. Repeated uneven-wear or brake-performance issues deserve closer inspection before buying.

Do not assume a fresh MOT means the brakes need no money

A vehicle can pass its MOT while still having brake components that will require maintenance soon. When buying, budget from the present mechanical condition rather than treating the MOT certificate as a guarantee of future brake life.

Frequently Asked Questions

Brake Pad Advisory FAQs

Straight answers to common UK driver questions about brake pad advisories, MOT limits, replacement timing, driving safety and repair decisions.

What does a brake pad advisory mean?

A brake pad advisory means the MOT tester identified wear or deterioration worth recording, but the observed condition did not meet the relevant MOT rejection criteria at the time of the test.

Is a brake pad advisory serious?

It should be treated as a maintenance warning. The pads may still be serviceable, but their remaining material, wear pattern, disc condition and caliper operation should be checked so the advisory does not develop into a more serious brake fault.

Can I drive with a brake pad advisory?

You may be able to continue driving if the vehicle remains roadworthy and inspection confirms the pads are still serviceable, but there is no universal safe mileage after an advisory. Grinding, reduced braking, severe pulling, overheating or abnormal pedal behaviour requires prompt investigation.

Will a brake pad advisory fail the next MOT?

Not automatically. The next MOT result depends on the condition of the pads and braking system when the vehicle is tested. Continued wear can turn an earlier advisory into a Major or Dangerous defect.

What brake pad thickness fails an MOT?

Under the current MOT inspection manual, a brake lining or pad worn down to its wear indicator is a Major defect. A brake lining or pad worn below 1.5 mm is a Dangerous defect.

Does a brake pad wear warning light fail an MOT?

No. The current MOT inspection manual states that an illuminated brake wear indicator is not itself a reason for failure. The physical condition of the brake pads is assessed separately.

Can contaminated brake pads fail an MOT?

Yes. A brake lining or pad contaminated with oil, grease or similar material is classified as a Major defect under the current MOT inspection criteria.

Is a missing brake pad a Dangerous MOT defect?

Yes. A brake lining or pad that is missing or incorrectly mounted is classified as a Dangerous defect under the current MOT inspection criteria.

How long do brake pads last after an advisory?

There is no fixed time or mileage. Remaining life depends on how much friction material is left, driving conditions, vehicle weight, braking style, disc condition and whether the pads and calipers are operating normally.

Why is one brake pad wearing faster than the other?

Uneven wear can be caused by restricted caliper sliders, a sticking piston, pad binding, damaged hardware, disc problems or another fault preventing the brake from applying and releasing evenly.

Can a sticking caliper cause a brake pad advisory?

Yes. A sticking caliper piston or restricted slider can keep a pad in contact with the disc, causing rapid or uneven wear that may lead to an advisory and eventually more serious brake damage.

Does squealing mean my brake pads are worn out?

Not necessarily. Squealing can come from a wear indicator, glazing, vibration, corrosion or pad-and-disc interaction. Pad thickness should be inspected directly rather than diagnosed from noise alone.

What does grinding from the brakes mean?

Grinding can indicate extremely low friction material or metal contact with the brake disc. It should be investigated promptly because continued driving can damage the discs and increase repair cost.

Should brake pads be replaced on both sides?

Brake pads are normally replaced as an axle set so the brakes on the left and right sides use comparable friction material and wear condition. Vehicle and component manufacturer procedures should always be followed.

Should brake pads and discs be replaced together?

Not always. Pads can often be replaced without new discs when the discs remain suitable for service. If the discs are significantly worn, heavily corroded, deeply scored, damaged or otherwise unsuitable, replacing pads and discs together may be the correct repair.

How much does brake pad replacement cost in the UK?

A broad planning range is around £100 to £250 or more for brake pads on one axle, although vehicle type, labour rates, pad specification, sensors and brake design can move the price higher. Pads and discs together usually cost substantially more.

Should I buy a used car with a brake pad advisory?

It can still be a sensible purchase if the advisory represents normal wear and the price reflects any required maintenance. Repeated advisories, uneven wear, damaged discs or signs of caliper problems deserve closer inspection before buying.

Does a valid MOT mean the brake pads are safe until the next test?

No. An MOT records the vehicle's condition against the test requirements at the time of inspection. Brake pads continue to wear afterwards, so servicing and current mechanical condition remain important throughout the year.
