Quick answer

What does an MOT advisory mean?

An MOT advisory means the tester has noticed wear, deterioration or a developing issue, but the vehicle still meets the minimum standard to pass the MOT at the time of testing. Advisories do not make the vehicle fail, but they should not be ignored.

The most common MOT advisories involve tyres, brakes, suspension components, corrosion, exhaust deterioration, oil leaks and visibility items. The most urgent advisories are normally those affecting tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, brake pipes and structural corrosion.

Repair priority

What should you fix first?

Start with anything that affects safety or could quickly become an MOT failure. Tyres close to the legal limit, worn brakes, corroded brake pipes, steering wear, suspension play and structural corrosion should be treated as high-priority repairs.

Lower-priority advisories, such as minor cosmetic deterioration or light surface corrosion away from safety-critical areas, can often be monitored, but they should still be checked before the next MOT.

Understanding your MOT result

What Is An MOT Advisory?

An MOT advisory is a note added to an MOT certificate when a tester identifies something that is worn, deteriorating, damaged or likely to need attention in the future, but not serious enough to fail the MOT on the day of the test. It is different from a major or dangerous defect because the vehicle still passes, but it is not the same as a clean bill of health.

Many UK drivers look only at the pass or fail result and miss the advisory section. That can be a costly mistake. Advisories often show where money may need to be spent before the next MOT. They can also reveal how a car has been maintained, especially when the same advisory appears year after year.

For example, a tyre advisory may mean the tyre is still legal but wearing close to the minimum tread depth. A brake advisory may mean the pads are still working but becoming thin. A corrosion advisory may mean rust is present but not yet severe enough to weaken a prescribed area. None of these automatically fails the car today, but each one can become a problem later.

If you are maintaining your own vehicle, advisories help you plan repairs. If you are buying a used car, advisories help you spot future costs. If you are selling a car, unresolved advisories can affect buyer confidence and negotiation.

The Most Common MOT Advisories Explained

These are the advisory categories UK drivers most often ask about after receiving an MOT certificate.

Tyres

1. Tyres Worn Close To The Legal Limit

Tyre wear is one of the most common MOT advisory items. The tyre may still meet the legal tread requirement, but the tester has noticed that tread depth is low enough to need attention soon. This is especially important in wet weather because stopping distances increase as tread depth falls.

If a tyre receives this advisory, check all four tyres rather than only the one mentioned. Uneven wear can suggest tracking, wheel alignment, suspension wear or incorrect tyre pressures. Replacing tyres early is usually cheaper than receiving a future MOT failure or risking a fine for illegal tyres.

Brakes

2. Brake Pads Wearing Thin

Brake pad advisories should be taken seriously. The pads may still work well enough to pass, but the friction material is wearing down. If ignored, worn pads can damage brake discs and increase the final repair cost.

You may notice squealing, grinding, vibration, longer stopping distances or a brake warning light, although many cars show no obvious symptoms until the pads are very worn. If brake pads are listed as an advisory, ask a garage to estimate remaining life.

Related guide: Car Fails MOT On Brakes.

Brakes

3. Brake Discs Corroded, Worn Or Pitted

Brake discs often receive advisories for corrosion, pitting or wear. Surface rust can appear when a car is used infrequently, but deeper corrosion or uneven disc surfaces can affect braking performance.

If the advisory mentions brake discs, it is worth checking whether pads and discs should be replaced together. Replacing only one part of a worn braking setup can sometimes lead to poor performance or repeated repairs.

Brake pipes

4. Corroded Brake Pipes

Brake pipe corrosion is one of the advisories that should not be ignored. Brake pipes carry pressurised hydraulic fluid. If corrosion becomes severe, braking performance can be affected and the car may fail a future MOT.

A light corrosion advisory does not always mean immediate replacement is required, but it should be inspected properly. Repeated brake pipe corrosion advisories are a warning sign when buying a used car.

Suspension

5. Suspension Bushes Deteriorated

Suspension bushes are rubber components that reduce vibration and allow controlled movement between suspension parts. Over time they crack, soften or split. A bush advisory usually means deterioration is visible but not yet excessive.

Symptoms may include knocking over bumps, uneven tyre wear, vague steering or a less stable feeling at speed. Suspension advisories often become failures if they are ignored for long enough.

Suspension

6. Ball Joint Or Track Rod End Wear

Ball joints and track rod ends connect steering and suspension parts. Advisories may be issued when slight play is present but not yet excessive. These components are safety-related because they affect steering control.

Common symptoms include knocking noises, steering looseness, uneven tyre wear or vibration. A steering or suspension play advisory should be inspected sooner rather than later.

Shock absorbers

7. Shock Absorber Misting Or Slight Leakage

Shock absorbers may receive an advisory when a light oil mist is visible. This is different from a severe leak, which may fail the MOT. Misting can still indicate that the shock absorber is ageing and may eventually need replacement.

Worn shock absorbers can affect braking, cornering, tyre wear and ride comfort. If one shock absorber is deteriorating, the opposite side should usually be checked as well.

Oil leaks

8. Minor Oil Leak

A minor oil leak advisory means oil is visible but not severe enough to fail the MOT. The leak could come from a rocker cover gasket, sump, oil filter housing, turbo oil feed, crankshaft seal or another engine component.

Even small leaks should be investigated because they can worsen, contaminate other components or lead to low oil levels. Check your oil level regularly if this advisory appears.

Related guide: Can Oil Leak Fail MOT?

Exhaust

9. Exhaust Corrosion Or Deterioration

Exhaust systems are exposed to heat, moisture, vibration and road salt, so corrosion is common. A light corrosion advisory may not affect the car immediately, but rust can quickly develop into holes, leaks or excessive noise.

If the advisory mentions exhaust mounts, silencers or leaks, monitor it carefully. Exhaust problems can eventually affect emissions, noise levels and MOT results.

Related guide: Can Exhaust Leak Fail MOT?

Rust

10. Vehicle Structure Or Underbody Corrosion

Corrosion advisories are common on older vehicles, especially those exposed to winter road salt. Surface rust does not always mean the vehicle is unsafe, but rust near structural areas, suspension mounts, seatbelt mounts or braking components is more serious.

Rust usually gets worse rather than better. Treating corrosion early can prevent expensive welding work later. When buying a used car, repeated corrosion advisories should be investigated before purchase.

Related guide: Car Fails MOT On Rust.

Lights

11. Deteriorated Lights Or Lenses

Light-related advisories may include faded lenses, minor moisture inside a lamp, reduced output or ageing headlamp covers. These may still pass if light output and aim remain acceptable.

Modern headlights can be expensive, so minor deterioration is worth addressing early. Cleaning, polishing or replacing bulbs may help, but damaged lamp units may need replacement.

Number plates

12. Registration Plate Deterioration

Number plate advisories may be issued when plates are damaged, faded, cracked or becoming difficult to read. A minor issue may pass as an advisory, but an unreadable or incorrectly spaced plate can fail.

Number plates are usually inexpensive to replace compared with many mechanical repairs, so this is often a simple advisory to fix before it becomes a problem.

Why repair costs vary so much

Two cars can receive the same advisory but have very different repair costs. A brake pad advisory on a small hatchback may be affordable, while brake work on a larger premium car can cost much more. Corrosion is especially variable because the final cost depends on how far the rust has spread and whether welding is required.

Used car checks

Should You Buy A Car With MOT Advisories?

A car with advisories is not automatically a bad buy. Many used cars have some advisory history. The important question is what the advisories are, whether they repeat year after year and how much they may cost to repair.

A single advisory for a tyre close to the legal limit is usually simple to understand. Repeated advisories for brake pipe corrosion, structural rust or suspension wear require more caution. They may show that previous owners delayed repairs or that the vehicle is developing age-related problems.

Before buying, compare the advisory list with the asking price. If the seller has not repaired known advisory items, factor the cost into your offer.

Red flags

Advisory patterns to watch for

  • Same advisory repeated across several MOTs
  • Corrosion moving from minor advisory to more serious wording
  • Brake pipe corrosion appearing more than once
  • Tyre advisories every year, suggesting poor maintenance
  • Suspension advisories on multiple corners
  • Emissions advisories followed by warning light or DPF issues
  • Advisories disappearing and reappearing without invoices

If you are unsure, use a pre-purchase inspection or walk away from cars with expensive unresolved advisories.

Prevention

How To Avoid MOT Advisories Next Year

The best way to reduce advisories is to inspect and maintain the car before the MOT rather than waiting for the tester to find problems.

Check tyres monthly

Look for low tread, uneven wear, sidewall damage and incorrect pressures. Tyres are one of the easiest advisory categories to prevent.

Inspect brakes early

Listen for grinding, squealing or vibration. Ask a garage to check pad thickness before the MOT if the brakes feel different.

Treat corrosion

Surface rust is cheaper to treat early. Do not wait until corrosion spreads near structural or safety-critical areas.

Fix leaks

Investigate oil, coolant, brake fluid or power steering leaks before they worsen or contaminate other parts.

Check lights and plates

Replace faded number plates, failed bulbs and damaged lenses before the test.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently Asked Questions About MOT Advisories

Common questions UK drivers ask after receiving an MOT pass with advisory items.

What is an MOT advisory?

An MOT advisory is a warning about wear, deterioration or a developing issue that does not currently fail the MOT but may require attention before the next test.

Can a car pass an MOT with advisories?

Yes. Advisories do not prevent a pass. The vehicle has passed the MOT, but the advisory items should be monitored or repaired.

What are the most common MOT advisories?

The most common advisories include tyre wear, brake pad wear, brake disc corrosion, suspension bush deterioration, oil leaks, exhaust corrosion and vehicle rust.

Which MOT advisories are most serious?

Tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, brake pipes and structural corrosion are usually the most serious because they can directly affect safety.

Can MOT advisories become failures?

Yes. Many advisories become failures if they worsen. Tyres, brakes, suspension parts and corrosion are especially likely to deteriorate.

Should I repair MOT advisories immediately?

Safety-related advisories should usually be repaired as soon as practical. Lower-risk advisories can often be monitored but should not be ignored.

Do advisories affect insurance?

An advisory itself does not normally invalidate insurance, but driving a vehicle you know is unsafe can create serious problems. Safety-related advisories should be dealt with promptly.

Do MOT advisories affect used car value?

They can. Multiple advisories suggest future repair costs and may reduce buyer confidence or give buyers room to negotiate.

How long can you drive with an MOT advisory?

There is no fixed time limit, but the longer you leave an advisory, the more chance it has to worsen. Safety-related items should not be delayed.

Should I buy a car with repeated advisories?

Be cautious. Repeated advisories can show that previous owners delayed repairs. Check invoices and consider a professional inspection.

Are tyre advisories urgent?

Usually yes. Tyres near the legal limit can become illegal quickly and affect wet-weather braking and grip.

Are brake advisories urgent?

Brake advisories should be prioritised because braking performance is safety-critical and repairs can become more expensive if delayed.

Final verdict

MOT advisories are early warnings, not paperwork to ignore

The most common MOT advisories often involve normal wear items, but that does not make them unimportant. Tyres, brakes, suspension components, brake pipes, corrosion, oil leaks and exhaust deterioration can all become more expensive if ignored.

A pass with advisories means the car passed the test on the day, not that every component is in excellent condition. Use the advisory list as a maintenance plan. Repair high-priority items first, monitor lower-risk issues and always check advisory history when buying a used car.
