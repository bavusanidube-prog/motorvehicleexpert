Load-Reversal Diagnosis

Car Clunks When Accelerating and Braking: Causes & Fixes UK 2026

A clunk that appears when you accelerate and again when you brake is an important diagnostic clue. Changing from driving force to braking or deceleration reverses load through the suspension, powertrain mountings and drivetrain, which can expose excessive movement that is much harder to identify from the noise alone.

Safety first

Do not assume a load-change clunk is harmless. If the vehicle also pulls under braking, wanders, has abnormal wheel movement, severe vibration, reduced braking control or a heavy worsening knock, avoid normal driving until the cause has been inspected. A current MOT certificate does not guarantee that a vehicle remains roadworthy between tests.

Quick Answer

Why Does My Car Clunk When Accelerating and Braking?

A car that clunks when accelerating and then clunks again when braking or decelerating often has a component that is moving as mechanical load changes direction.

Acceleration applies driving torque through the engine, transmission, drivetrain, wheel assemblies and their mountings. Braking or lifting off the accelerator changes those forces. If a bush, mounting, joint or drivetrain connection has excessive movement, it can shift across its available clearance and produce a distinct knock or clunk.

Important possibilities include lower-arm bushes, engine or gearbox mounts, torque mounts, subframe movement, CV joints, driveshaft connections and excessive drivetrain backlash. Brake or other suspension components can also imitate the same symptom.

The direction change is the clue

Do not diagnose this symptom from the loudness of the clunk alone. Establish whether the noise occurs as drive is applied, as drive is removed, under actual braking, when changing direction, or during all of those conditions. That pattern can narrow the investigation considerably.

Core Diagnostic Principle

Load Reversal Explained

The useful mechanical clue in this symptom is not simply “the car makes a clunk”. It is that the clunk appears when the forces acting through the vehicle change.

Imagine a worn mounting or bush with more movement than it should have. Under acceleration, the component can move towards one side of its available travel. When the accelerator is released or braking force is introduced, the load changes and the component may move back towards the opposite side.

If that movement is large or abrupt enough, the driver may hear or feel a single clunk at each change of load.

Drive Applied

Acceleration

Engine torque is transmitted through the gearbox and drivetrain while powertrain mountings resist engine and transmission movement.

Drive Reduced

Throttle Released

Torque reduces and the powertrain, drivetrain and loaded bushes can move back towards their neutral or opposite loaded positions.

Opposite Load

Braking

Braking introduces strong longitudinal forces through the tyres, hubs, suspension links, bushes, brakes and vehicle structure.

Load reversal does not identify one component

It identifies a diagnostic pattern. The next job is to establish which component is actually moving. Replacing engine mounts, lower arms or CV joints simply because they can cause a clunk is not a professional diagnosis.

Compare the Trigger

Clunk Under Acceleration vs Clunk Under Braking

Separating the two halves of the symptom helps determine which forces reproduce the noise.

Acceleration

Clunk as Drive Is Applied

A clunk exactly as the vehicle begins pulling can place more attention on powertrain mountings, torque mounts, drivetrain clearance, driveshaft connections and suspension bushes that react to driving force.

Note whether it occurs from rest, after every gear change, only under hard acceleration or merely once after the direction of travel has changed.

Braking

Clunk as the Vehicle Slows

A clunk under braking places longitudinal load through the wheel and suspension assembly and can expose lower-arm bush movement, ball-joint play, brake-component movement, subframe movement or other looseness.

If the car clunks under braking but never reacts to throttle changes, use the braking-specific diagnostic route rather than assuming drivetrain backlash.

Clunk in both directions is more informative

If acceleration produces one clunk and braking produces another, ask what component can move when longitudinal or drivetrain load reverses. That is generally more useful than asking which part commonly makes a knocking noise.

Road-Test Pattern

Throttle-On vs Throttle-Off Clunk

A controlled transition between light acceleration and a closed throttle can provide a valuable clue because it changes drivetrain torque without necessarily applying the service brakes.

Road-Test Pattern What Changes Diagnostic Direction
Clunk as throttle is applied Driving torque takes up Check mounts, drivetrain clearance, driveshafts and load-sensitive suspension movement.
Clunk as throttle is released Driving torque reduces or reverses Look closely for excessive movement across a mounting, joint, spline or bush.
Clunk on throttle-on and throttle-off Component repeatedly changes loaded position Stronger evidence of a load-reversal or backlash pattern.
Quiet on throttle changes but clunks under braking Brake and suspension loading dominates Prioritise brake hardware and suspension movement rather than assuming a drivetrain fault.

Any road test must be carried out safely and legally. Aggressive acceleration, harsh braking or repeated direction changes are not required to prove that a fault exists.

Noise Pattern

Single Clunk vs Repeated Clunk

Whether the noise happens once or continues while the vehicle is under load can materially change the diagnostic direction.

One Movement

Single Clunk

One solid clunk as acceleration begins, followed by another when the load reverses, is consistent with a component moving from one end of excessive clearance to the other.

Continuing Movement

Repeated Knocking

Repeated knocking during acceleration may point towards a rotating, oscillating or repeatedly loaded fault and should not automatically be treated as ordinary mounting movement.

A repeated noise that changes with wheel speed, engine speed or steering angle deserves separate attention because those patterns can identify rotating drivetrain or wheel-end faults that a simple load-reversal explanation cannot fully account for.

Initial Drive

Why a Car Can Clunk When Taking Up Drive

“Taking up drive” describes the moment torque begins travelling through the drivetrain strongly enough to move the vehicle. Existing clearances between components are taken up during this transition.

A small amount of engineered clearance exists in many mechanical systems. The concern is excessive or abnormal movement that creates a pronounced impact, especially where the symptom is new, worsening or accompanied by other signs.

Mounting Movement

Engine, gearbox and torque mounts resist movement as torque builds.

Drivetrain Clearance

Joints, splines and final-drive components transfer torque through their working clearances.

Suspension Reaction

Tyre forces are reacted through suspension arms, bushes, joints and the vehicle structure.

Direction Test

Forward vs Reverse Clunk

Changing from forward to reverse is particularly useful diagnostically because the direction of torque through much of the drivetrain changes.

If a worn component has moved towards one side of its available clearance while travelling forwards, reversing can move it towards the other side. The resulting impact can create a clear clunk.

A repeatable direction-change clunk deserves investigation

Check whether the sound occurs only once after changing direction or every time the vehicle moves. One clunk after forward/reverse reversal can suggest clearance being taken up; continuing noise needs a broader investigation.

Transmission Pattern

Manual Clutch Take-Up vs Automatic Drive/Reverse Engagement

Manual and automatic vehicles apply drivetrain load differently, so the exact moment the clunk appears matters.

Manual

Clunk as the Clutch Takes Up

Releasing the clutch progressively connects engine torque to the transmission and driveline. Excessive movement in powertrain mounts, drivetrain connections or suspension components may become noticeable at this moment.

Do not automatically condemn the clutch. Compare the symptom with throttle changes while already moving and with forward versus reverse operation.

Automatic

Clunk Selecting Drive or Reverse

Selecting D or R changes the state and direction of drivetrain loading. Worn mounts or excessive driveline movement can make this transition more noticeable.

A genuinely harsh transmission engagement, delayed engagement or other gearbox symptom needs to be distinguished from a separate mounting or drivetrain clunk.

Changing Load

Clunk During or After Gear Changes

A gear change can temporarily remove and then reapply drivetrain torque. If excessive movement exists somewhere in the load path, that transition can expose it.

Note whether the clunk occurs when the clutch pedal is pressed, when the next gear is selected, as the clutch is released, or only when the accelerator is reapplied. Those are mechanically different events.

Timing matters more than saying “it clunks changing gear”

The exact instant of the noise helps distinguish clutch or transmission behaviour from engine-mount movement, drivetrain take-up and suspension reaction.

Vehicle Load

Uphill vs Downhill Clunk

Gradient changes how much torque or braking effort is required. That can make a marginal fault easier to reproduce.

Worse Uphill

Greater driving torque can exaggerate movement in powertrain mounts, torque mounts, driveshaft joints and other components carrying drivetrain load.

Worse Downhill or Braking

Deceleration and braking can load suspension bushes and wheel assemblies differently, helping reveal fore-and-aft movement or brake-related free play.

Steering Influence

Clunk Driving Straight vs While Steering

If the clunk behaves differently with steering angle, that is another useful clue.

Same With Wheels Straight

A straight-line load-change clunk keeps mounts, longitudinal bush movement, subframe movement and drivetrain clearance high on the list.

Worse With Steering Lock

A strong relationship with steering angle increases the need to investigate CV joints, steering joints, ball joints, top mounts and other wheel-end components.

Noise Location

Front vs Rear vs Central Clunk

Where the driver thinks a clunk comes from can help, but vehicle structures transmit sound extremely well. A noise that seems to come from one corner may originate somewhere else.

Front

Front-End Clunk

Front lower-arm bushes, engine or gearbox mounts, front subframe components, CV joints and brake hardware become particularly relevant on many front-wheel-drive vehicles.

Centre

Central / Underfloor Clunk

On suitable vehicle layouts, inspect propshaft components, centre support bearings, exhaust contact, drivetrain mountings and underbody components.

Rear

Rear-End Clunk

Rear suspension bushes, differential mountings on applicable vehicles, rear drivetrain components and loose underbody or luggage-area items can imitate one another.

Temperature Pattern

Clunk When Cold vs When Warm

Temperature can change the behaviour of rubber bushes, hydraulic mountings, lubricants and clearances, but temperature alone does not identify the failed component.

Record whether the clunk is strongest during the first few minutes, remains unchanged throughout the journey or appears only after the vehicle has been driven for some time.

That information should be combined with the load pattern rather than used as a stand-alone diagnosis.

Mechanic Insight

Diagnose Movement, Not Just Noise

The most useful question is not: “Which part makes a clunk?”

It is: “What changed mechanically at the exact moment the clunk occurred?”

If the noise happens when torque is applied and again when torque or longitudinal load changes direction, the inspection should follow that force through the vehicle. Start at the tyre contact patch and suspension location points, then consider the drivetrain, powertrain mountings, subframes and other components that react against those forces.

A good diagnosis must reproduce the symptom

Finding an old bush, a slightly soft mounting or normal drivetrain clearance does not prove it caused the noise. The strongest diagnosis connects the driver's symptom to abnormal movement found during inspection and then confirms that the symptom has disappeared after repair.

Which Parts Actually Cause an Acceleration-and-Braking Clunk?

The symptom pattern tells us when something moves. The next stage is determining what is moving.

Part 2 follows the mechanical load path through the vehicle and compares lower-arm bushes, engine and gearbox mounts, torque mounts, subframes, CV joints, driveshafts, drivetrain backlash, suspension joints, brake components and the less obvious faults capable of producing the same load-change clunk.

Main Causes

What Causes a Car to Clunk When Accelerating and Braking?

A clunk under both acceleration and braking usually appears because a component is reacting to changing mechanical load.

Acceleration sends engine torque through the transmission and drivetrain to the driven wheels. Those tyre forces then react through the suspension, subframes and body structure. When the accelerator is released or braking begins, those forces reduce, reverse or redistribute.

If a bush, mounting, joint, spline or drivetrain connection has excessive clearance, the component can move abruptly between two loaded positions and produce the characteristic clunk.

Suspension

Bush & Joint Movement

Lower arms, ball joints and other suspension components must control wheel position while driving and braking forces change.

Powertrain

Mount Movement

Engine, gearbox and torque mounts resist the rotational reaction created as torque rises and falls.

Drivetrain

Backlash & Joint Clearance

CV joints, splines, differential components and propshafts contain working clearances that can become excessive with wear.

Structure

Subframe Movement

Subframes and their mountings transfer suspension and drivetrain loads into the vehicle body.

Mechanical Load Path

How Acceleration and Braking Loads Travel Through the Vehicle

Understanding the load path makes this symptom much easier to diagnose. A clunk can originate far away from where the driver hears it because the force travels through several connected components.

1. Engine Produces Torque

Combustion torque turns the engine while the engine and gearbox mountings resist the opposite rotational reaction.

2. Gearbox & Final Drive

Torque is multiplied, reduced or redirected through the transmission and final-drive assembly.

3. Driveshafts Carry Torque

Driveshafts, CV joints, splines or propshafts transfer torque to the driven wheels.

4. Tyres Push Against the Road

The tyre contact patch generates the force that accelerates or slows the vehicle.

5. Suspension Controls the Wheel

Arms, bushes and joints resist fore-and-aft and lateral wheel movement created by tyre forces.

6. Subframe Carries the Load

Many suspension and drivetrain components attach to a subframe before their loads reach the body structure.

7. Driver Releases Throttle

Torque reduces or reverses and any excessive clearance can be unloaded and taken up in the opposite direction.

8. Braking Reverses Wheel Load

Longitudinal braking force is reacted through the wheel, suspension and vehicle structure in the opposite direction.

One noise can involve several load paths

A front-wheel-drive vehicle may transfer acceleration load through the engine mounts, gearbox, driveshafts and front suspension at the same moment. That is why diagnosis must prove the movement rather than choosing a part from the sound alone.

Road Force

The Tyre Contact Patch Is Where Vehicle Load Changes Become Real

The engine creates torque, but the vehicle only accelerates because the driven tyres push against the road. Under braking, the tyre contact patch creates force in the opposite direction.

Those changing tyre forces try to move the wheel relative to the body. Suspension arms and bushes must control that movement while still allowing normal suspension travel.

Acceleration

Driven-wheel force loads suspension and drivetrain components in one longitudinal direction.

Coasting

Driving torque reduces and components can move towards their neutral positions.

Braking

Wheel load acts in the opposite longitudinal direction and can expose bush or joint clearance.

Lower-Arm Bushes

Can Lower-Arm or Wishbone Bushes Cause the Clunk?

Yes. Lower-arm bushes are one of the strongest suspension causes because they help control the fore-and-aft position of the wheel.

If the bush is torn, separated, hydraulically failed or excessively soft, the arm can move farther than intended as acceleration and braking forces change direction.

Acceleration Clunk

Drive force can move the arm towards one side of the worn bush.

Braking Clunk

Braking load can move it back towards the opposite side.

Steering / Geometry Change

Severe movement can alter wheel position and steering response under load.

Cracked rubber does not automatically prove the fault

Rubber bushes are designed to flex. The important finding is excessive or uncontrolled movement that explains the driver's load-change symptom.

Bush Position

Front vs Rear Lower-Arm Bush

Many wishbones use two bushes, and each can control a different mixture of rotation, lateral movement and longitudinal compliance.

Front Bush

Pivot Control

Depending on the suspension design, the front bush can control arm rotation and lateral location while working with the rear bush to position the wheel.

Rear Bush

Longitudinal Compliance

On many layouts, the larger rear bush carries substantial acceleration and braking load and is particularly relevant to fore-and-aft wheel movement.

Hydraulic Bushes

Hydro Bush Failure and Load-Reversal Clunks

Hydraulic suspension bushes use fluid-filled internal chambers to combine vibration isolation with controlled suspension movement.

When the internal structure splits or fluid escapes, the bush can lose much of its designed control even if the outer rubber still appears reasonably intact.

Fluid Loss

Wetness around the bush can support a hydraulic-bush failure.

Excessive Softness

The arm can travel much farther than the opposite side under the same force.

Braking & Acceleration Clunk

Reversing longitudinal load can expose the failed bush very clearly.

Powertrain Mounts

Engine and Gearbox Mounts

Engine and transmission mountings support the powertrain while controlling its movement and isolating vibration from the body.

When torque is applied, the engine and gearbox try to rotate in reaction to drivetrain load. When the driver lifts off or brakes, that reaction reduces or changes direction.

Split Rubber Mount

Torn rubber can allow large movement before the mount reaches its mechanical limit.

Hydraulic Mount Failure

Fluid-filled powertrain mounts can soften or collapse after internal failure.

Loose Mounting Hardware

A sound mount can still clunk if the bracket or fixing is insecure.

Look for secondary contact marks

Excessive powertrain movement can make an exhaust, bracket, intake pipe or other nearby component strike the body and create a clunk that sounds like the mount itself.

Torque Mount

Can a Torque or Dogbone Mount Cause a Clunk?

Yes. The torque mount is specifically intended to control fore-and-aft or rotational movement of the engine and gearbox assembly under torque.

A worn bush or damaged mounting can allow the powertrain to move sharply as torque is applied and released, making this component particularly relevant to a clunk on acceleration and deceleration.

Clunk Taking Up Drive

Powertrain rotation takes up clearance in the mount.

Clunk Lifting Off

Load reverses and the mount moves back towards its opposite position.

Gear-Change Thud

Torque interruption and reapplication can reproduce the same movement.

Subframe

Subframe Bushes and Fixings

A subframe can carry suspension, steering, engine, gearbox or differential components depending on vehicle design. It therefore sits directly in several important load paths.

Excessive movement in subframe bushes, insecure bolts or structural damage around a mounting point can create a heavy clunk when acceleration and braking forces reverse.

Subframe Bush Wear

Deteriorated bushes can allow the frame to move relative to the body.

Loose / Incorrect Fixings

Mounting bolts must remain correctly installed and secure.

Structural Mounting Damage

Corrosion, cracking or deformation around the mounting can be more serious than the bush itself.

Significant subframe movement is not a monitor-only fault

Because the subframe can locate suspension, steering or drivetrain components, excessive movement can affect geometry, steering response and vehicle stability as well as creating noise.

CV Joints

Can CV Joints Cause a Clunk on Load Changes?

Yes. Constant-velocity joints allow driveshafts to transmit torque while the suspension and steering move. Internal wear can increase clearance within the joint.

Inner CV Joint

Load & Plunge Movement

Inner joints commonly accommodate driveshaft length changes as the suspension moves and can contribute to vibration or clunking under torque changes when worn.

Outer CV Joint

Steering Articulation

Outer joints are more strongly associated with clicking during turns, although severe wear can contribute to broader drivetrain noise.

Driveshaft Connections

Driveshaft Splines and Hub / Gearbox Connections

Splined connections allow torque to pass between shafts, hubs and transmission components. Wear, incorrect installation or movement at those interfaces can sometimes create a distinct take-up clunk.

Hub Spline

Movement between the driveshaft and hub can create a wheel-end take-up noise.

Gearbox / Differential Spline

Inner driveshaft engagement points can also develop excessive clearance or movement.

Fastener Preload

Some hub and shaft assemblies rely on correct nut or bolt installation to maintain bearing and spline security.

Drivetrain Backlash

Differential and Final-Drive Backlash

Gears require controlled clearance so they can operate without binding. The problem begins when accumulated wear across several drivetrain components produces enough free rotational movement to create a noticeable impact as torque reverses.

Differential Gears

Internal gear clearance contributes to total drivetrain take-up.

Final-Drive Gears

Crownwheel/pinion or equivalent final-drive components carry changing torque.

CV / Spline Clearance

Small clearances can accumulate through several connected components.

Mounting Compliance

Even normal drivetrain backlash can sound worse when mounts allow excessive assembly movement.

Some backlash is normal

The diagnosis is not simply that rotational clearance exists. The workshop must determine whether the amount is excessive, whether one component contributes disproportionately and whether it actually reproduces the driver's complaint.

RWD / AWD Drivetrain

Propshaft and Centre Support Bearing

Rear-wheel-drive and all-wheel-drive vehicles can add a propshaft, universal or CV joints and sometimes a centre support bearing to the load path.

Wear or excessive movement in these components can create a central or underfloor clunk when drive is taken up or released.

Propshaft Joint

Universal or CV-joint wear can create rotational take-up.

Centre Bearing

A damaged support bush or bearing can allow excessive shaft movement.

Flexible Coupling

Where fitted, deterioration can create movement or vibration during torque changes.

Differential Mounting

Differential Mounts on RWD and AWD Vehicles

A separately mounted differential reacts strongly to torque. Deteriorated bushes or loose mountings can let the differential casing move as drive is applied and removed.

Take-Up Clunk

Differential movement can produce a heavy rear or central thud.

Throttle-Off Clunk

Releasing torque can move the differential back across worn mounting clearance.

Rear-End Movement

Inspect the differential itself, mounts, subframe and surrounding suspension together.

Suspension Joints

Ball Joints

Ball joints allow suspension articulation while controlling wheel position. Excessive play can allow the wheel hub or arm to move when longitudinal force changes.

Braking Clunk

Wheel load can move the worn joint towards one side of its clearance.

Bump Knock

Vertical suspension movement can produce a second symptom.

Steering Change

Severe wear can affect steering precision and wheel geometry.

Steering Linkage

Track Rod Ends and Steering Joints

Steering joints do not normally carry drivetrain torque, but braking and acceleration can change wheel position enough to expose existing steering free play.

Steering Knock

Free play can create a knock as wheel load changes.

Vague Steering

Loose steering response strengthens the case for joint inspection.

Steering-Angle Sensitivity

A clunk that changes with steering angle increases the relevance of wheel-end and steering components.

Top Mounts

Top Mounts and Strut Bearings

Top mounts transfer suspension loads into the body and, on MacPherson-strut systems, may contain the bearing that allows the strut to rotate while steering.

They are not the first component to blame for a pure drivetrain load-reversal clunk, but damaged mounts can contribute when vehicle pitch, steering and suspension movement occur together.

Clunk Over Bumps

Often a stronger top-mount clue than acceleration alone.

Steering Creak / Twang

Bearing roughness can create noise while steering.

Load-Change Knock

Vehicle pitch can change strut load enough to expose mounting movement.

Related specialist guide

If inspection points towards the upper strut assembly rather than drivetrain load reversal, continue into Bad Top Mount Symptoms .

Damper Mountings

Shock Absorber Bushes and Mountings

Worn damper bushes or mounting hardware can clunk as the vehicle pitches during acceleration and braking.

Rubber Bush Wear

Excessive movement at the mounting eye can produce a dull knock.

Loose Fastener

A bolt moving within a bracket can create a sharper clunk.

Damper Condition

Check the mounting separately from damping effectiveness and leakage.

Wheel Bearing

Can a Wheel Bearing Cause a Load-Change Clunk?

Severe wheel-bearing play can allow the hub to move relative to the suspension upright. That can alter brake-disc position and create movement as wheel loads change.

Road-Speed Rumbling

A hum or rumble that changes with road speed is a stronger bearing clue.

Hub Play

Excessive wheel-end movement must be isolated from suspension or steering joints.

Brake Influence

Hub movement can alter disc position and sometimes change when the brake is applied.

Brake Hardware

Brake Pads, Calipers and Carrier Movement

Brake components can create a clunk during direction changes and braking even when the main complaint sounds like a drivetrain or suspension problem.

Pad Movement

Excessive pad clearance can create a click or clunk when braking direction changes.

Anti-Rattle Hardware

Incorrect or missing clips can alter pad location.

Caliper Guides

Excessive guide wear can allow abnormal caliper movement.

Carrier Security

Caliper and carrier mounting points must remain correctly secured under braking torque.

Confirmed loose brake hardware requires urgent correction

A clunk caused by normal pad take-up is very different from an insecure caliper, carrier or mounting. Do not treat brake insecurity as a harmless noise.

Component Security

Loose Suspension Bolts and Mounting Hardware

A newly replaced bush, arm or mount can still clunk if its fixing is incorrect, loose or damaged.

Recent Repair

A noise that begins immediately after suspension or drivetrain work should trigger inspection of the disturbed area.

Incorrect Tightening

Bonded bushes may require final tightening in the specified suspension position.

Damaged Mounting Hole

Repeated movement can enlarge holes, damage threads or deform brackets.

Non-Drivetrain Mimics

Exhaust, Heat Shield, Undertray and Loose Attachments

Not every clunk during acceleration or braking comes from an internal drivetrain or suspension fault.

Powertrain and body movement can make loose exhaust components, heat shields, undertrays or unsecured items strike nearby structures as the vehicle changes load.

Exhaust Hanger

A broken or stretched hanger can allow the exhaust to strike the body or subframe.

Heat Shield

Loose shields can tap or knock as the powertrain moves.

Undertray

Missing fixings can allow panels to shift under acceleration or braking.

Loose Battery / Spare Wheel

Unsecured heavy items can create surprisingly convincing load-change thuds.

Professional Diagnosis

How a Mechanic Diagnoses a Clunk When Accelerating and Braking

A proper diagnosis starts by reproducing the exact load change that creates the clunk, then separating suspension movement from powertrain movement, drivetrain backlash and brake-related noise.

The strongest evidence is not that a component looks old or has some movement. The strongest evidence is that abnormal movement in that component matches the direction, timing and location of the driver's symptom.

1. Reproduce

Confirm exactly when the clunk occurs during acceleration, deceleration, braking, gear changes or direction changes.

2. Separate the Load

Compare throttle, brake, steering and suspension inputs so only one major force changes at a time.

3. Inspect Under the Right Conditions

Load bushes, mounts, joints or drivetrain components in the direction that reproduces the road-test complaint.

4. Confirm After Repair

Repeat the original road-test sequence and prove that the load-change clunk has disappeared.

Mechanic principle

The road test identifies the force that creates the clunk. The workshop inspection identifies the component that moves abnormally under that force.

Driver Interview

Start With the Exact Load-Change Pattern

“It clunks when I accelerate” is not enough information for a strong diagnosis. The technician should establish the precise sequence that produces the noise.

When Did It Start?

Sudden onset after suspension, clutch, gearbox, driveshaft, subframe or engine-mount work deserves close attention.

One Clunk or Repeated?

One take-up clunk suggests a different movement from repeated knocking that follows wheel or engine speed.

Acceleration and Braking?

Confirm whether the noise appears in both directions of load or only under one condition.

Forward and Reverse?

Direction changes can expose backlash or movement across opposite sides of a bush or mounting.

Manual or Automatic?

Clutch take-up and automatic D/R engagement apply drivetrain load in different ways.

Any Second Symptom?

Vibration, steering pull, bump noise, clicking on turns or harsh gear engagement can narrow the fault significantly.

Controlled Road Test

Reproduce the Clunk Without Introducing Extra Variables

The road test should use gentle, repeatable inputs. The aim is not to create the loudest possible clunk; it is to identify which change in load reliably triggers it.

Light Acceleration

Apply enough torque to take up drivetrain load without harsh wheelspin or aggressive acceleration.

Throttle Release

Lift off and note whether the same component appears to move back in the opposite direction.

Gentle Braking

Compare actual braking load with simple engine-overrun deceleration.

Direction Change

Forward/reverse comparison can reveal take-up through drivetrain, mount or suspension clearance.

Do not perform aggressive DIY load tests

If the car pulls sharply, has unstable steering, severe vibration, abnormal wheel movement or uncertain braking performance, inspection should take priority over repeated road testing.

Torque Test

Throttle-On / Throttle-Off Test

This test is valuable because it changes drivetrain torque without immediately adding strong service-brake force.

Clunk on Throttle-On

Mounts, drivetrain joints, splines and torque-sensitive suspension movement become more relevant.

Clunk on Lift-Off

The same component may be moving back as drive torque reduces or reverses.

No Clunk Until Brake Applied

Brake hardware and suspension components loaded specifically by braking deserve more attention.

Direction Reversal

Forward and Reverse Load-Reversal Test

Changing from forward to reverse can move worn drivetrain, suspension and mounting components across opposite sides of their available clearance.

1. Forward Take-Up

Note the first clunk as drive is applied.

2. Stop

Bring the vehicle to rest safely without introducing harsh braking.

3. Reverse Take-Up

Apply drive gently in the opposite direction.

4. Compare the Noise

Determine whether each direction change reproduces one matching load-take-up clunk.

A direction-sensitive clunk is a valuable clue

It does not prove one specific component, but it strengthens the case for excessive movement or backlash somewhere in the driveline, mountings or longitudinal suspension load path.

Manual Transmission

Manual Clutch Take-Up Test

On a manual vehicle, the technician should identify whether the clunk occurs as the clutch begins transmitting torque or only after the accelerator is reapplied.

Clunk at Bite Point

Powertrain mounts, torque mount, driveshafts and drivetrain take-up become important.

Clunk After Clutch Fully Engaged

The fault may depend more strongly on engine torque than clutch engagement itself.

Clutch Judder as Well

Separate vibration during clutch engagement from a single mechanical take-up clunk.

Do not condemn the clutch from the clunk alone

A healthy clutch can transmit torque into worn mounts, suspension bushes or drivetrain joints and expose movement elsewhere.

Automatic Transmission

Automatic Drive / Reverse Engagement Test

Selecting Drive or Reverse applies drivetrain load before the vehicle necessarily begins moving. That can help separate powertrain and driveline movement from suspension load created by road forces.

Clunk Selecting D

Powertrain mount or drivetrain take-up may occur as forward load is applied.

Clunk Selecting R

Reverse engagement loads the same system in the opposite direction.

Harsh / Delayed Engagement

A transmission-control or internal gearbox problem must be considered separately from an external mounting clunk.

Gear-Change Diagnosis

Identify the Exact Moment the Clunk Happens During a Gear Change

“Clunks when changing gear” can describe several mechanically different events.

Exact Timing What Changes Diagnostic Direction
Clunk when clutch pedal is pressed Drive torque is removed Mount or drivetrain load-release movement
Clunk selecting the next gear Transmission components are repositioned Gear linkage / transmission / mount consideration
Clunk as clutch re-engages Torque returns to drivetrain Mounts, torque mount, CV/driveshaft take-up
Clunk only when throttle is reapplied Engine torque rises Powertrain and drivetrain load reversal
Brake Comparison

Compare Engine Overrun With Actual Braking

The distinction between lifting off the accelerator and pressing the brake pedal can help separate drivetrain torque reversal from wheel-end and suspension braking load.

Lift-Off Only

Clunk Without Brake Pedal

Mountings, drivetrain backlash and torque-related movement become stronger candidates.

Brake Applied

Clunk Requires Braking Force

Lower-arm bushes, ball joints, brake hardware and other wheel-location components deserve closer inspection.

Workshop Safety

Safe Lifting and Correct Vehicle Support

Many load-reversal faults cannot be confirmed safely from the ground, but lifting the vehicle changes suspension geometry and load distribution.

The technician must therefore decide which checks need the suspension loaded, partially loaded or hanging freely.

Never work under a vehicle supported only by a jack

Correct lifting points and suitable workshop support equipment are essential before under-vehicle inspection, levering or drivetrain checks.

Suspension Loading

Loaded vs Unloaded Suspension Inspection

Loaded

Better for reproducing normal road relationships in many bushes and suspension arms.

Unloaded

Useful for checking certain ball joints, wheel bearings, springs and unrestricted suspension movement.

Vehicle-Specific Procedure

Some suspension layouts require a particular support condition to expose genuine free play accurately.

Lower-Arm Confirmation

Lower-Arm Bush Levering and Fore-Aft Movement

The strongest bush confirmation comes from applying controlled force in the same direction that acceleration and braking load the suspension arm.

1. Observe First

Check cracking, separation, sleeve position and hydraulic-bush leakage before levering.

2. Load Fore-and-Aft

Apply controlled force in the longitudinal direction relevant to the complaint.

3. Watch the Relative Movement

Identify whether movement occurs within the bush, at the fixing or elsewhere in the arm.

4. Compare

Compare the opposite side where suspension design and condition make that comparison useful.

Normal compliance must not be mistaken for failure

Rubber bushes are designed to move. The concern is excessive, separated or uncontrolled movement that can reproduce the driver's load-change symptom.

Powertrain Confirmation

Engine and Gearbox Mount Movement

Static appearance alone can miss a mount that only allows excessive movement when torque is applied.

Rubber Separation

Look for torn, split, displaced or collapsed rubber.

Hydraulic Leakage

Fluid-filled engine mounts may show evidence of internal failure or collapse.

Excessive Powertrain Travel

Compare movement as torque is applied and removed under controlled conditions.

Movement alone is not the diagnosis

Engines and gearboxes are designed to move on their mountings. The issue is excessive travel, impact against a movement stop, mount separation or contact with neighbouring components.

Torque Mount Confirmation

Confirming a Torque / Dogbone Mount Fault

Because the torque mount is intended to resist powertrain rotation, it should be inspected specifically under torque-reversal conditions.

Bush Separation

Look for tearing around the bonded rubber or centre sleeve.

Metal-to-Metal Limit Contact

Witness marks can show where excessive movement reaches the mount's mechanical limit.

Matching Road-Test Pattern

A take-up clunk followed by an opposite lift-off clunk strongly supports further torque-mount investigation.

Subframe Confirmation

Confirming Subframe Bush or Mounting Movement

The inspection needs to determine whether the movement exists within a compliant bush, at a mounting bolt or in the surrounding structural attachment.

Bush Movement

Compare expected compliance with excessive relative movement.

Bolt / Fixing Security

Inspect mounting hardware and evidence of previous movement.

Structure

Check corrosion, cracking, distortion or damaged mounting areas.

CV Joint Confirmation

Inner vs Outer CV Joint Confirmation

Inner CV

Torque & Plunge Behaviour

Inspect excessive rotational movement, plunge behaviour, vibration under acceleration and abnormal joint looseness.

Outer CV

Steering-Related Behaviour

Compare load-change clunking with clicking, knocking or roughness that changes with steering lock.

Driveshaft Backlash

Driveshaft and Spline Free-Play Checks

The technician should identify where rotational movement begins and ends rather than treating all driveline clearance as one fault.

Hub-End Spline

Check for relative movement between driveshaft and hub where the design allows.

Inner Spline / Gearbox Connection

Inspect engagement points for abnormal rotational or lateral movement.

Shaft / Joint Comparison

Establish whether free play comes from the spline, CV joint or farther into the transmission.

Final-Drive Diagnosis

Differential and Final-Drive Backlash

Some rotational free play is inherent in geared drivetrains. The workshop needs to determine whether the total clearance is excessive, concentrated in one component or accompanied by other signs of wear.

Rotational Take-Up

Compare shaft rotation with wheel or differential movement.

Noise Location

Establish whether the clunk is strongest at the differential, gearbox, shaft or mounting.

Secondary Symptoms

Whine, rumble, vibration or oil leakage can support a wider final-drive problem.

Propshaft Diagnosis

Propshaft and Centre-Bearing Checks

On rear-wheel-drive and all-wheel-drive vehicles, a central load-change clunk can come from propshaft joints, flexible couplings or the centre support bearing.

Joint Play

Check universal or CV joints for excessive rotational or articulated movement.

Centre Support

Inspect the bearing and surrounding rubber support for separation or excessive movement.

Contact Evidence

Look for fresh marks showing the shaft or coupling has moved enough to contact surrounding structure.

Differential Mounts

Confirming Differential Mount Movement

A separately mounted differential should be observed while torque direction changes, with attention paid to bush movement and any impact against mounting limits.

Bush Integrity

Check tearing, separation or collapsed rubber.

Casing Movement

Compare differential movement under opposite torque directions.

Subframe Relationship

Confirm that the noise is not actually coming from the supporting subframe or its bushes.

Wheel-End Isolation

Ball-Joint and Steering-Joint Isolation

Wheel play must be traced to its exact source because ball joints, track rods and wheel bearings can all create movement that feels similar during a basic wheel-rocking test.

Observe the Ball Joint

Watch for movement between the joint pin and housing.

Observe the Track Rod

Confirm whether steering movement originates at the outer or inner steering joint.

Isolate the Bearing

Determine whether the hub itself moves relative to the suspension upright.

Brake Isolation

Separate Brake-Hardware Movement From Drivetrain Load Reversal

Brake pads and caliper components can move when the direction of braking force changes, particularly after reversing. That can produce a load-change clunk that sounds very similar to suspension or drivetrain movement.

Pad Fit

Check excessive movement in the carrier.

Abutment / Anti-Rattle Hardware

Confirm all specified clips and springs are present and fitted correctly.

Caliper Guides

Inspect abnormal clearance or seizure.

Carrier Security

Verify the brake mounting itself is secure.

Confirmed brake-component insecurity is safety critical

Do not treat abnormal caliper or carrier movement as an ordinary load-reversal noise.

Misdiagnosis

Why Load-Reversal Clunks Get Misdiagnosed

Several systems can react at exactly the same instant, and the body shell can transmit a heavy clunk far away from its source.

Noise Travels

A clunk heard at the front can originate from a central mount or subframe.

Several Components Have Clearance

Total driveline backlash may be distributed across multiple joints and gears.

Old Parts Look Suspicious

Age or surface cracking alone does not prove a component caused the symptom.

Static Inspection Misses Torque

Some mounts only move excessively when the powertrain is loaded.

Brake Noise Mimics Driveline Noise

Pad take-up after reversing can sound like spline or CV movement.

Suspension Wear Mimics Mount Wear

Lower-arm movement can clunk under both acceleration and braking without an engine-mount fault.

Do not replace the most common suspect first

Lower-arm bushes, engine mounts and CV joints are all plausible, but none should be replaced until abnormal movement has been linked to the actual load-change complaint.

Post-Repair Verification

Confirm the Repair Using the Original Load Pattern

1. Reinspect

Confirm correct installation and fastening.

2. Repeat Throttle-On / Off

Recreate the original torque-reversal condition.

3. Repeat Forward / Reverse

Confirm that direction changes no longer reproduce the clunk.

4. Repeat Braking Comparison

Verify that the braking half of the original complaint is also resolved.

Where suspension geometry has been disturbed, wheel alignment or geometry measurement should be considered after the mechanical repair is complete.

Safe-To-Drive Decision

Is It Safe to Drive With a Clunk When Accelerating and Braking?

A clunk during acceleration and braking should not be judged by noise volume alone. The important question is what is moving and whether that movement affects vehicle control, braking, steering, suspension or drivetrain security.

A light, unchanged take-up clunk with normal steering, braking and handling may allow the vehicle to be booked for diagnosis rather than immediately recovered. A heavy or rapidly worsening clunk, however, can indicate substantial movement that should not be ignored.

Lower Concern

Light & Stable Clunk

The noise is mild, repeatable and unchanged. Steering, braking and straight-line stability remain normal, with no obvious wheel movement or severe vibration.

Book Diagnosis
Increased Concern

Clunk Is Getting Worse

The noise is becoming heavier or is now accompanied by vibration, steering changes, bump knocking, harsh take-up or noticeable movement.

Diagnose Promptly
High Concern

Control or Security Is Affected

Strong pulling, unstable steering, abnormal braking, visible wheel movement, severe vibration or confirmed loose brake, suspension, subframe or drivetrain hardware requires urgent attention.

Stop Normal Driving
Do not continue normal driving if vehicle control has changed

Stop using the vehicle normally if the clunk is accompanied by steering instability, severe pulling, abnormal braking, obvious wheel movement, major vibration, grinding, a component contacting the body, or known loose suspension or brake hardware. Arrange professional inspection or recovery as appropriate.

If You Ignore It

What Happens If You Ignore a Load-Reversal Clunk?

The outcome depends entirely on the underlying fault. Some minor noises can remain stable for a considerable period, while genuine free play or mounting failure can worsen because the component is repeatedly loaded in opposite directions.

Once controlled movement becomes impact movement, every acceleration, lift-off, braking event and direction change can place additional shock load through the surrounding components.

Clearance Increases

A worn bush, joint or mount can progressively allow more movement.

Impact Loading Increases

Components can begin striking their movement limits instead of transferring force progressively.

Secondary Parts Are Loaded

Extra movement can transfer stress into brackets, joints, shafts, exhaust components and neighbouring mountings.

Progressive Wear

Worsening Bush and Mount Movement

A compliant mounting is designed to control movement within a specific range. Once rubber separates, a hydraulic bush loses fluid or a mount collapses, the component may travel farther before the remaining material or mechanical stop catches it.

Early Stage

Slightly increased movement may produce an occasional light clunk.

Developing Stage

Movement becomes easier to reproduce under acceleration, braking or direction changes.

Advanced Stage

Large movement can create heavy impact, geometry changes or contact between components.

Geometry & Tyres

Can the Fault Cause Wheel-Alignment Changes and Tyre Wear?

Yes, if the clunk comes from a suspension component responsible for locating the wheel. A worn lower-arm bush, ball joint, subframe mounting or similar component can allow geometry to change dynamically while the vehicle is being driven.

A static alignment measurement cannot permanently correct geometry that changes because a component has excessive free play.

Toe Change

Wheel direction can alter as suspension components move under braking or acceleration load.

Uneven Tyre Wear

Persistent geometry movement can contribute to abnormal inner, outer or irregular tread wear.

Steering Instability

The vehicle may feel different under acceleration compared with braking because wheel position is changing.

Repair free play before relying on alignment

If suspension or subframe movement is responsible for changing geometry, the mechanical fault should be repaired before final wheel alignment is used to set the vehicle correctly.

Drivetrain Consequences

Excessive Backlash Can Increase Drivetrain Shock Loading

Normal drivetrain clearance allows components to operate without binding. Excessive accumulated clearance, however, can allow one component to gain momentum before contacting the next loaded surface.

The result can be a sharper torque impact whenever the driver accelerates, lifts off, changes gear or changes direction.

Harder Take-Up

Torque reaches the next loaded surface more abruptly.

Mount Loading

Engine, gearbox and differential mountings can experience stronger reaction forces.

Joint Loading

CV joints, splines and other driveline connections can receive repeated impact loading.

CV & Spline Wear

Can CV Joint or Spline Wear Get Worse?

Yes. Where excessive clearance already exists, repeated torque reversal can continue loading worn contact surfaces.

Increasing Clunk

Rotational take-up can become more noticeable as clearance develops.

Acceleration Vibration

Some inner CV or driveshaft faults can progress from noise into vibration under load.

Additional Driveline Stress

Excessive movement can increase impact through neighbouring drivetrain components.

Mounting Damage

Subframe and Mounting Problems Should Not Be Left to Worsen

A subframe carries major vehicle loads. If a bush, bolt or structural mounting is allowing abnormal movement, repeated load reversal can increase wear around the affected attachment.

Structural security matters more than the noise itself

Significant subframe movement, damaged mounting points, loose fixings or severe corrosion require proper assessment. These are fundamentally different from a harmless trim rattle.

Secondary Contact

Excessive Movement Can Damage Exhausts, Shields and Nearby Parts

A failed engine or gearbox mount can allow the powertrain to move farther than intended. The resulting clunk may actually be the exhaust, downpipe, heat shield, intake system or another attached component contacting the body or subframe.

Exhaust Contact

Repeated impact can damage hangers, joints or exhaust components.

Hose / Wiring Strain

Excessive powertrain movement can place additional strain on connected hoses, wiring and pipes.

Witness Marks

Fresh polished or impact marks can help identify the actual contact point.

Repair Strategy

Repair the Confirmed Fault, Not Every Component With Some Wear

Older vehicles often contain several bushes, mounts and joints showing age-related deterioration. That does not mean every one needs replacing to cure one clunk.

One Proven Fault

Repair the component whose abnormal movement reproduces the complaint.

Several Related Faults

Consider combined repair where multiple components are genuinely defective and labour overlaps.

Wear but No Excessive Movement

Record and monitor rather than automatically replacing a part that has not been shown to cause the problem.

UK MOT

Can a Car Fail an MOT for Clunking When Accelerating and Braking?

The noise itself is not the deciding MOT defect. What matters is whether the underlying component has a testable defect such as excessive wear, insecurity, serious deterioration or another condition covered by the MOT inspection.

A car can therefore make a clunk yet have no corresponding MOT failure if the source does not meet a rejection threshold. Conversely, a relatively quiet suspension, steering or mounting defect can still fail if inspection shows excessive movement or insecurity.

Suspension

Bushes & Joints

Excessive wear or insecurity in relevant suspension components can affect the MOT result.

Steering

Steering Free Play

Steering joints and associated components are assessed on condition, security and excessive play.

Brakes

Brake Security

A genuine insecure brake component is substantially more serious than an unexplained noise.

MOT pass does not prove the clunk is harmless

The MOT is a minimum roadworthiness inspection of specified items at the time of the test. It is not a full diagnostic investigation into every noise or drivetrain complaint.

MOT History

Repeated MOT Advisories Can Help Explain the Clunk

MOT history is especially useful when buying a car or investigating a long-standing suspension-related clunk. One advisory does not automatically prove the current noise, but repeated entries can reveal a pattern.

Same Advisory Repeated

Repeated suspension deterioration over several tests deserves closer inspection.

Advisory Then Failure

This can show that deterioration progressed rather than being repaired.

Advisory Disappears

It may have been repaired, may not have been recorded again, or may have been interpreted differently at the later test. Confirm physically rather than assuming.

Before the Garage

Pre-Diagnostic Checklist for a Load-Reversal Clunk

You do not need to dismantle the vehicle to give a mechanic useful information. Recording the exact symptom can shorten diagnosis considerably.

Acceleration

Does it clunk when throttle is first applied or only under heavier acceleration?

Lift-Off

Does releasing the accelerator produce an opposite clunk?

Braking

Does the noise require the brake pedal, or does ordinary deceleration reproduce it?

Forward vs Reverse

Note whether changing direction produces one clear take-up clunk.

Gear Changes

Record whether the noise happens when torque is removed, during selection or when drive returns.

Clutch Take-Up

On a manual, note whether the clunk occurs exactly as the clutch begins to bite.

Drive / Reverse Selection

On an automatic, note whether D or R engagement produces the noise before the car moves.

Steering Position

Check whether straight ahead and steering lock produce different symptoms.

Secondary Symptoms

Record vibration, pulling, bump knocking, clicking, tyre wear or harsh engagement.

Give the garage a sequence, not just a noise description

For example: “One clunk when I accelerate from low speed, then another when I lift off, and it also happens when changing from reverse to forward” is far more diagnostically useful than “there is a clunk underneath”.

Prevention

How to Reduce the Risk of Load-Reversal Clunks Developing

Normal ageing cannot be prevented completely, but good repair practice and early investigation can prevent small movement from becoming a larger problem.

Investigate New Noises Early

A newly developing clunk is easier to assess before multiple components become worn.

Use Correct Installation Procedures

Suspension bushes, shafts and mountings should be installed and tightened according to the vehicle procedure.

Recheck After Major Repairs

A new noise after clutch, gearbox, driveshaft or suspension work deserves prompt inspection.

Watch Tyre Wear

New or rapidly developing uneven wear can reveal geometry movement before handling becomes obviously poor.

Do Not Ignore MOT Advisories

Repeated suspension or steering advisories can provide useful warning of developing wear.

Avoid Parts Swapping

Correct diagnosis prevents unnecessary repairs while leaving the actual moving component untouched.

Guide Summary

Car Clunks When Accelerating and Braking: Key Takeaways

Think Load Reversal

A clunk under acceleration and braking often appears because mechanical load is moving a component between two positions.

Do Not Guess From the Noise

Lower-arm bushes, powertrain mounts, CV joints, splines, subframes, drivetrain backlash and brake hardware can create similar symptoms.

Throttle-On / Off Is Valuable

A clunk without using the brake pedal points more strongly towards torque-related movement.

Braking Changes the Diagnosis

If the noise requires braking force, suspension wheel-location components and brake hardware become more important.

Forward / Reverse Helps

Direction reversal can expose excessive backlash or movement across opposite sides of a bush or mounting.

Mounts Must Be Tested Under Load

A powertrain mount can look acceptable when stationary yet allow excessive movement under torque.

Suspension Free Play Matters

Lower-arm, ball-joint or subframe movement can affect geometry, tyre wear and steering behaviour.

MOT Tests the Defect

The clunk itself is not the deciding MOT issue; the condition, wear and security of the relevant tested component matter.

Diagnosis Comes Before Parts

Reproduce the load → isolate the system → prove abnormal movement → repair the confirmed fault → repeat the original test.

The core mechanic rule

Do not ask only “what part can make this noise?” Ask “what component can move in both directions under the exact acceleration and braking loads that reproduce this clunk?” That question turns a broad symptom into a structured diagnosis.

Frequently Asked Questions

Car Clunks When Accelerating and Braking: FAQs

Quick answers to the most important load-reversal, suspension, powertrain, drivetrain, safety and MOT questions covered in this guide.

Why does my car clunk when accelerating and braking?

A clunk during both acceleration and braking often means that a component is moving as load changes direction through the vehicle. Common possibilities include worn lower-arm bushes, engine or gearbox mounts, subframe movement, CV or driveshaft wear, drivetrain backlash and loose suspension or brake components. The noise should be diagnosed from the conditions that trigger it rather than from sound alone.

What does load reversal mean in a car?

Load reversal is the change in force through the drivetrain, mountings and suspension when the vehicle changes between driving, coasting and braking. A worn bush, mount or joint can move across available clearance when the direction of force changes, sometimes producing one distinct clunk.

Can lower-arm bushes cause a clunk when accelerating and braking?

Yes. Worn lower-arm or wishbone bushes can allow excessive fore-and-aft movement of the suspension arm and wheel assembly. Acceleration and braking apply forces in different directions, so excessive bush movement can produce a clunk as the arm moves under changing load.

Can engine mounts cause a clunk when accelerating and braking?

Yes. A severely worn, split or loose engine mounting can allow excessive powertrain movement as engine torque is applied and removed. The resulting movement may produce a knock or clunk during acceleration, deceleration, braking, gear changes or clutch take-up.

Can a gearbox mount cause a clunk when accelerating?

Yes. Gearbox mountings help control powertrain movement and can produce a clunk if deterioration or looseness allows excessive movement. The symptom may be particularly noticeable during throttle changes, clutch engagement, gear changes or transitions between acceleration and deceleration.

Can a torque mount or dogbone mount cause a clunk?

Yes. A torque mount, sometimes called a dogbone mount, is designed to control powertrain rotation under torque. Worn bushes or damaged mountings can allow the engine and gearbox assembly to move sharply when drive is applied or removed, creating a noticeable clunk.

Can a subframe cause a clunk when accelerating and braking?

Yes. Excessive movement caused by deteriorated subframe bushes, damaged mountings or insecure fixings can produce a heavy clunk as acceleration and braking loads change direction. Because the subframe supports important suspension or drivetrain components on many vehicles, suspected movement needs careful inspection.

Can a CV joint cause a clunk when accelerating and braking?

Yes. Wear or excessive clearance in a CV joint or related driveshaft connection can sometimes create a clunk when torque is applied and removed. Outer CV joints are commonly associated with clicking on turns, while load-change clunks may require inspection of inner joints, driveshaft connections and the wider drivetrain.

Can drivetrain backlash cause a clunk?

Yes. Drivetrain components require some operating clearance, but excessive accumulated play can produce a noticeable clunk when torque changes direction. The source can vary with vehicle layout and may involve driveshaft joints, splines, differential components, propshaft components or mountings.

Why does my car clunk when I press and release the accelerator?

Pressing and releasing the accelerator changes torque through the engine, gearbox, driveshafts, mountings and suspension. If a component has excessive clearance or movement, it may shift as the load changes and produce a clunk. Powertrain mounts, suspension bushes and drivetrain play are important areas to investigate.

Why does my car clunk when taking up drive?

A clunk as drive is taken up can occur when clearance in a mounting, bush, joint or drivetrain component is suddenly loaded. The diagnostic significance depends on whether the same noise occurs during braking, reversing, clutch engagement, gear selection or throttle changes.

Why does my car clunk when changing from reverse to forward?

Changing direction reverses torque through the drivetrain and can move worn components across their available clearance. A clunk during forward-to-reverse or reverse-to-forward transitions can therefore point towards mountings, suspension bushes, driveshaft joints or excessive drivetrain play, although the exact cause must be inspected.

Why does my manual car clunk when the clutch takes up?

Clutch engagement applies torque to the gearbox, driveshafts and vehicle mountings. A clunk during take-up can therefore come from excessive movement in engine or gearbox mounts, suspension bushes, driveshaft connections or other drivetrain components rather than necessarily from the clutch itself.

Why does an automatic car clunk when selecting Drive or Reverse?

Selecting Drive or Reverse changes the direction and application of drivetrain load. Excessive movement in powertrain mounts, drivetrain joints or related components can make this transition more noticeable. A harsh engagement can also have transmission-related causes, so diagnosis should not be based on the clunk alone.

Is a clunk when accelerating and braking dangerous?

It can be. Some causes produce noise before they create an immediate safety problem, while others involve safety-critical suspension, steering, drivetrain or mounting components. Strong steering movement, unstable braking, abnormal wheel movement, severe vibration or a rapidly worsening clunk should be inspected urgently.

Can a car fail an MOT for clunking when accelerating and braking?

A clunking noise by itself is not normally the defect classification. The MOT result depends on the underlying component and its condition. Excessive wear, deterioration, damage or insecurity affecting testable suspension, transmission, mounting, steering or braking components can result in an MOT defect.

How does a mechanic diagnose a clunk during load changes?

A mechanic should first reproduce the symptom and establish exactly when the load changes. Diagnosis can then compare acceleration, deceleration, braking, forward and reverse operation before inspecting suspension bushes, powertrain mountings, subframes, driveshaft joints and other components for abnormal movement or clearance.

Should I buy a used car that clunks when accelerating and braking?

A used car with a repeatable load-change clunk should be inspected before purchase. The cause may be relatively straightforward, but it can also involve suspension, mountings, subframes or drivetrain components. Check the MOT history, obtain a diagnosis and price the confirmed repair before deciding whether the vehicle represents good value.
