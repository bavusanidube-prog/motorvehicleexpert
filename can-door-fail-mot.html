Quick answer

Can a Car Door Fail an MOT?

Yes. Under the current MOT inspection rules, a door can fail if it will not open using the required control, will not close properly, is likely to open inadvertently or has a missing or insecure hinge, catch or pillar.

The important point is that the MOT is concerned with door operation and security, not simply appearance. A dent or cosmetic scratch is very different from a latch that will not hold the door closed or a handle that prevents a required door from opening.

Won't open

Access requirement

Driver and passenger doors must open from the outside using the relevant control. Driver and front passenger doors must also open from the inside.

Won't close

Major defect

A door that will not close properly can be classed as a Major defect.

May open while moving

Dangerous possible

A turning door likely to open inadvertently can be classed as Dangerous rather than merely Major.

Cosmetic damage

Not automatic failure

A dent or cosmetic defect does not automatically fail the MOT unless it affects security, operation, structure or creates dangerous bodywork.

No conventional handle does not automatically mean MOT failure

Some vehicles use buttons, electronic releases or other controls instead of conventional handles. The MOT manual allows this where the design or specialist modification still lets the door open using the relevant control and latch securely when closed.

Current UK MOT rules

What Does the MOT Tester Check on Car Doors?

The MOT door inspection is more specific than simply opening and closing every door. The tester looks at whether the relevant doors can be operated using their required controls, whether they close correctly and whether the hinges, catches and surrounding structure allow the door to work as intended.

Outside opening

Driver & passenger doors

Driver and passenger doors must open from the outside using the relevant control.

Inside opening

Driver & front passenger

The driver and front passenger doors must also open from the inside using the relevant control.

Closing

Door must close properly

A door that cannot close properly is a Major defect.

Security

Door must remain closed

A door likely to open inadvertently is an MOT defect, with the category depending on the door type and severity.

Hinges & catches

Condition must support normal operation

Excessive deterioration is assessed according to whether the door still works as intended, while a missing or insecure hinge, catch or pillar is a more serious defect.

Body condition

Sharp or insecure damage matters

Damaged or corroded bodywork can separately fail if it is likely to cause injury when contacted or if a body component is insecure.

Do not confuse deterioration with automatic failure

The DVSA specifically says hinges, catches and pillars should only be rejected for deterioration where the deterioration causes the door not to work as intended. That is different from a missing or insecure hinge, catch or pillar, which has its own defect category.

Corrosion severity depends on location and function

A small amount of corrosion can be important if it affects a load-bearing or safety-related area, while more extensive corrosion on a non-structural section may have little effect on safety. Door, sill and pillar corrosion therefore needs to be judged by what the affected structure actually supports.

Door applicability

Which Car Doors Must Open for an MOT?

This is where door MOT advice is often oversimplified. The requirements are not identical for every opening control on every door.

For the standard car MOT inspection, driver and passenger doors must be capable of being opened from outside using the relevant control. The driver and front passenger doors must also open from inside using the relevant control.

Driver's door

Outside and inside operation matter

The driver's door needs to open using the relevant outside control and the relevant inside control.

Front passenger door

Outside and inside operation matter

The front passenger door is also subject to the outside-opening and inside-opening requirements.

Rear passenger door

Outside opening still matters

Rear passenger doors must satisfy the applicable outside-opening and closing requirements, but the standard inside-opening requirement is different from the front doors.

Sliding door

It still needs to operate securely

Sliding doors need to operate using their relevant controls and remain securely retained rather than being judged by the rules for a conventional hinged door alone.

Electronic release

A conventional handle is not essential

A button, switch, fob or other manufacturer-designed control can satisfy the opening requirement where that is how the vehicle is intended to operate.

Boot / tailgate

Do not treat it as a passenger door

Boot lids and tailgates have their own security and body-attachment considerations rather than simply being treated as another passenger door.

Why rear child locks cause confusion

A rear child lock is specifically designed to prevent the rear door being opened from inside. That is different from a failed front-door interior handle or a rear-door mechanism that remains defective when the child lock is switched off.

Do not diagnose the MOT result from “one handle is broken”

First identify which door, whether the failed control is inside or outside, whether another manufacturer-designed control is provided and whether the door still opens, closes and remains securely latched as intended.

Door access fault

Will a Door That Does Not Open Fail an MOT?

Yes, where the door is required to open using that control. A door that cannot be opened as required can be classed as a Major defect.

The useful diagnostic question is therefore not simply “is the door stuck?” It is which operating path has failed: outside handle, inside handle, latch release, lock mechanism, central-locking actuator, cable, linkage or electronic release.

Outside handle moves freely

Cable or linkage may be detached

A handle that suddenly feels very light can indicate that its cable, rod or linkage is no longer operating the latch.

Handle feels normal

Latch may remain locked

Normal handle resistance with no release can point towards the latch, lock mechanism or actuator rather than the handle itself.

Works inside only

Outside release path needs inspection

If the door opens from inside but not outside, focus on the exterior handle, cable, linkage and the relevant part of the latch mechanism.

Works outside only

Check the interior control

On a front door this can directly affect the inside-opening MOT requirement. On a rear door, first make sure a child lock is not deliberately preventing internal opening.

Neither control works

Lock or latch may be jammed

A seized latch, failed actuator, deadlocking fault or mechanical damage can leave both release paths ineffective.

Intermittent opening

Do not wait for complete failure

A handle that only works after several pulls or a latch that releases intermittently is already showing deterioration and can leave the door unusable later.

A jammed door is not best fixed by repeatedly forcing the handle

Excessive force can break the handle, stretch or detach a cable and make access to the latch more difficult. If both release controls have stopped working, the trim may need to be removed carefully so the mechanism can be inspected.

Door security

Will a Car Door That Does Not Close Properly Fail an MOT?

Yes. A door that will not close properly is a Major defect. If a turning door is likely to open inadvertently, the condition can reach the Dangerous category.

A door that only shuts after being slammed hard, bounces back open, sits partly latched or moves against the catch while driving should therefore be investigated rather than treated as normal ageing.

Bounces open

Latch may not be engaging

The latch jaw may be stuck in the wrong position, internally damaged or unable to engage correctly with the striker.

Must be slammed

Alignment deserves attention

Hinge wear, door drop, striker movement, accident distortion or latch wear can make a door difficult to secure.

Half catches

Do not assume it is securely shut

A door sitting on an initial catch position may not have achieved the secure closing condition intended by the mechanism.

Rattles when driving

Check latch and striker engagement

Movement between the latch and striker can reflect wear, looseness or poor alignment even if the door appears closed.

Door has dropped

Hinge condition may be causing misalignment

A sagging door can force the latch upward or downward against the striker and eventually prevent correct closure.

After accident repair

Structure may be misaligned

Distorted pillars, poor replacement-panel alignment or incorrectly positioned hinges and strikers can all affect door closure.

Do not drive with a door that cannot remain securely closed

A temporary strap, repeated slamming or manually holding a defective door shut does not repair the latch, hinge or structural fault. If the door can open inadvertently, repair the cause before normal road use.

Exterior release control

Can a Broken Outside Door Handle Fail an MOT?

It can. The relevant question is whether the driver or passenger door can still be opened from outside using the control the vehicle is designed to use.

A physically broken conventional handle is therefore significant where it prevents the required outside opening. But the MOT should not automatically reject a vehicle simply because there is no conventional handle when a button, switch, fob or other approved control performs that function by design.

Handle snapped

Door cannot be released

If the outside control can no longer open the relevant passenger door, the access requirement is affected.

Handle pulls but does nothing

Inspect cable or linkage

The visible handle may still be intact while its internal cable, rod or lever has become detached.

Handle sticks

Release may remain partly applied

A sticking handle can prevent the latch resetting correctly and may contribute to a door that does not remain securely closed.

Flush electronic handle

Design matters

Modern vehicles may use powered or flush handles. The issue is whether the intended outside control can operate the door as required.

Frozen or temporary fault

Tester assesses condition at test

A temporary condition that prevents the required door opening can still stop the tester confirming satisfactory operation.

Replacement part

Check operation after repair

After fitting a handle or cable, check release, latch reset, closing and locking rather than confirming only that the handle moves.

Missing handle and unusable control are not always the same thing

Vehicle design is important. A specialist or manufacturer-designed system may legitimately use a different control from a traditional pull handle. What matters is whether the door can still be operated correctly using that intended control.

Interior release control

Can a Broken Inside Door Handle Fail an MOT?

Yes, on the driver or front passenger door where the relevant inside control no longer opens the door. Those two front doors are required to open from inside during the standard passenger-car MOT inspection.

Rear-door interior operation needs more careful interpretation because a child lock can intentionally disable the interior release while leaving the door's normal outside operation intact.

Driver's inside handle

Required operating path

If the driver's door cannot be opened from inside using its relevant control, the MOT opening requirement is affected.

Front passenger handle

Also required from inside

The front passenger door is subject to the same inside-opening requirement.

Rear inside handle

Check child-lock position first

A rear door deliberately prevented from opening internally by its child lock is different from a genuinely broken release mechanism.

Handle loose

Cable or mounting may be failing

Excessive free movement can indicate a stretched cable, broken mounting or partially detached linkage.

Electronic button

Intended control still counts

Some vehicles use an electronic interior release rather than a purely mechanical lever. It still needs to provide the intended opening function.

Emergency mechanical release

Do not assume it replaces normal operation

Vehicle-specific emergency releases can differ from the normal control. The intended operating arrangement should be assessed correctly rather than relying on an improvised workaround.

Before replacing a rear-door inside handle

Check the child-lock position first. If the door opens normally from the inside once the child lock is disengaged, the mechanism may be operating exactly as designed.

Latch, catch & locking mechanism

Can a Faulty Door Latch or Lock Fail an MOT?

Yes, where the fault prevents the door operating as required or leaves it insecure. The latch and catch are mechanically more important to the MOT than whether the remote central locking happens to work from the key fob.

The latch must release when the relevant door control is operated and must also secure the door correctly after closing. A lock or actuator fault becomes important when it stops one of those functions.

Latch will not release

Door remains stuck shut

The problem may lie inside the latch, locking motor, linkage or the release control rather than in the visible handle.

Latch will not engage

Door cannot remain closed

A stuck-open or damaged latch can cause the door to rebound rather than remain securely shut.

Striker misalignment

Check why alignment changed

Simply moving the striker may hide worn hinges, door drop or structural distortion. Identify the reason the alignment changed before adjustment.

Central-locking actuator

Convenience fault or access fault?

If remote locking fails but the door still opens, closes and secures correctly using the relevant controls, that is different from an actuator leaving the door permanently locked or unable to latch.

Deadlock fault

Can block both release paths

A mechanical or electronic deadlocking fault can leave a door difficult to release from either side and may require deeper latch diagnosis.

Worn catch

Security can deteriorate gradually

Wear can begin as rattling or the need to slam the door before progressing to unreliable engagement.

Do not confuse a lock fault with a latch-security fault

A car can have a faulty remote-locking function while the door remains mechanically secure and operable. Conversely, a door can appear to lock electronically yet still have a worn latch or catch that does not retain it correctly.

Mechanic inspection sequence

Check the outside release, inside release where applicable, locking function, latch reset, striker engagement and security after closing. That sequence distinguishes a handle problem from a latch, actuator, alignment or hinge fault.

Door hinge condition

Can Worn or Loose Door Hinges Fail an MOT?

Yes, depending on severity and how the deterioration affects the door. Door-hinge faults range from slight wear to loose mounting, damaged hinge pins, distorted brackets and corrosion around the hinge pillar.

The important distinction is whether the hinge deterioration actually affects secure attachment or normal door operation. Slight deterioration is not treated the same as a missing or insecure hinge, catch or pillar.

Hinge-pin wear

Door moves vertically

Worn hinge pins or bushes can allow the door to drop when opened and can gradually move the latch out of alignment with the striker.

Loose mounting

Hinge moves at the body or door

Loose fasteners or damaged mounting points can make the door feel insecure and should be distinguished from normal hinge-pin movement.

Distorted hinge

Accident or overload damage

A bent hinge can alter panel alignment and make the latch difficult to engage even when the latch itself is healthy.

Pillar damage

The mounting structure matters

A good hinge bolted to weakened or distorted pillar metal may still leave the door insecure or misaligned.

Corrosion

Check around hinge mounting points

Corrosion around the hinge or pillar can be more important than surface rust on the outer door skin because the hinge depends on that structure for support.

Cracking / movement

Inspect the surrounding metal

Cracking, flexing or visible movement around the mounting area can point to fatigue, corrosion or previous repair damage rather than simple hinge wear.

Do not correct a dropping door only by moving the striker

If the root cause is hinge wear, loose mounting or pillar distortion, moving the striker may only hide the misalignment temporarily. Diagnose the hinge and supporting structure first.

Sagging door diagnosis

Why Does a Car Door Drop or Sag When Opened?

A door that visibly drops as it opens is useful diagnostic evidence. The problem is often more than a worn latch because the latch does not support the weight of the open door.

Typical causes include hinge-pin wear, worn bushes, loose hinge mountings, distorted hinges, pillar damage or previous accident repair.

Drops when opened

Hinge wear is likely

Lift the open door carefully and look for vertical movement at the hinge before assuming the latch or striker is responsible.

Scrapes the sill

Alignment has shifted

A sagging door can contact the sill, weather seal or adjacent panel as it closes.

Must be lifted to shut

Strong sign of hinge or structural misalignment

If the door only closes when physically lifted, inspect hinge play and pillar condition before adjusting the latch.

Striker heavily worn

Long-term misalignment clue

Polished or deeply worn striker surfaces can show that the latch has been dragging across the striker for some time.

Uneven panel gap

Compare top and bottom gaps

A narrowing or widening gap between the door and adjacent body panels can help show whether the door has dropped or the structure has moved.

Recent accident repair

Check more than the hinge

A replacement door or repaired pillar can be poorly aligned even when the hinge itself has little wear.

Door drop can explain several apparently separate symptoms

One worn hinge can create poor panel gaps, striker wear, difficulty closing, rattling and a handle that feels harder to operate because the latch is loaded against the striker.

Rear-door child locks

Can a Child Lock Cause an MOT Failure?

A correctly operating child lock is not the same as a broken door mechanism. Its purpose is to prevent a rear door being opened from the inside while still allowing normal operation from outside.

The important check is whether the rear door behaves normally when the child lock is disengaged and whether it still meets the applicable outside-opening and closing requirements.

Child lock on

Rear inside handle disabled by design

This is expected operation and should not automatically be mistaken for a failed interior handle.

Child lock off

Inside handle should behave normally

If the rear interior handle still does not release the door, there may be a separate cable, linkage or latch fault.

Outside handle works

Normal child-lock pattern

A rear door that opens from outside but not inside with the child lock engaged is behaving as designed.

Outside also fails

Not explained by child lock alone

If neither outside nor inside control opens the door, investigate the latch, actuator or linkage instead of blaming the child lock.

Electric child lock

Modern systems can be electronic

Some vehicles use an electronic child-lock function rather than a mechanical switch on the door edge.

Test deliberately

Know which mode is active

Before diagnosing the handle, confirm whether the vehicle's child-lock system is switched on.

Child lock and deadlock are different systems

A child lock intentionally blocks rear-door interior release. A deadlocking fault can prevent normal release more broadly and may leave the door difficult to open from either side.

Central-locking faults

Does Central Locking Have to Work for an MOT?

Central locking itself is not the key MOT question. The important issue is whether the relevant doors can be opened using their required controls and secured correctly when closed.

That means a failed remote-locking function can be very different from an actuator that leaves a door permanently locked shut or unable to latch.

Remote fob fails

Door still works manually

If the door opens and closes correctly using its normal controls, the remote convenience fault is different from a door-operation defect.

One actuator fails

Check whether the door remains usable

An actuator that fails electrically but leaves manual operation intact presents a different situation from one that traps the latch in a locked position.

Door permanently locked

Access requirement may be affected

A central-locking fault becomes more significant if the relevant door cannot be opened using its required control.

Door will not stay locked

Security and latch are separate checks

A security-locking fault should be distinguished from the mechanical latch's ability to keep the door safely closed.

Wiring in door loom

Repeated flexing can cause electrical faults

Wiring where the door loom passes between body and door can fatigue, affecting locks, windows, mirrors and other electrical functions.

Multiple doors affected

Look for a shared electrical cause

If several locks fail together, consider fuses, body-control systems, power supply or communication faults before condemning several actuators.

Diagnose central locking by function, not by the key-fob symptom alone

Confirm manual opening, inside opening where applicable, latch security and actuator behaviour. That separates a convenience-system fault from an MOT door-operation problem.

Corrosion & structural condition

Can Rust Around a Car Door Fail an MOT?

Yes, but the important issue is where the corrosion is and what that area does. Surface rust on an outer door skin is not mechanically equivalent to corrosion weakening a hinge pillar, sill, latch mounting or another safety-related structural area.

Outer door skin

Often cosmetic first

Light surface corrosion on non-structural outer metal does not automatically mean the door will fail the MOT.

Hinge pillar

More important structurally

Corrosion around the hinge-supporting structure can affect the security and alignment of the door.

Sill area

Function and load path matter

Sill corrosion needs to be assessed according to the structure's safety and load-bearing role rather than appearance alone.

Catch / striker mounting

Security depends on sound metal

A healthy latch cannot secure the door properly if the metal supporting the striker is seriously weakened.

Perforated door edge

Check for sharp or insecure metal

Corrosion can become a bodywork concern if it leaves sharp metal, insecure sections or affects operation.

Previous welding

Inspect the repair quality

A repaired area should restore secure structure and should not leave cracking, weak attachment or dangerous edges.

Rust quantity alone does not determine severity

A small amount of corrosion in a critical mounting or load-bearing area can be more important than a large patch of superficial corrosion on a non-structural panel.

Dangerous bodywork

Can Sharp Door Damage Fail an MOT?

Yes. Damaged or corroded bodywork can fail where it is likely to cause injury if someone grazes or contacts it. The concern is the physical hazard, not whether the damage looks untidy.

Torn metal

Exposed sharp edge

Accident-damaged metal that projects outward can create a direct injury risk.

Rust hole

Jagged corroded edges

Perforated corrosion can leave sharp metal even when the affected panel is not carrying a major structural load.

Broken trim

Check whether hard edges are exposed

Missing or damaged mouldings can reveal sharp fixings or damaged metal beneath them.

Protruding component

Attachment matters too

A loose body component may be dangerous if it projects outward or is likely to detach.

Cosmetic crease

Not automatically a failure

A smooth dent with no sharp projection and no effect on door operation is different from torn or insecure bodywork.

Temporary covering

Repair the underlying hazard properly

Tape or improvised covering should not be relied on where damaged metal remains insecure or the underlying body defect still creates a hazard.

MOT bodywork decisions are about risk, not appearance

Scratches, faded paint and smooth dents are fundamentally different from jagged corrosion, torn metal or a component likely to detach.

Previous collision damage

Can Accident Damage Around a Door Cause an MOT Failure?

Yes. Previous accident damage can alter door alignment, hinge position, latch engagement, pillar strength and body-panel security. The MOT result depends on the condition that remains after the repair, not simply on whether the car has been involved in an accident.

Uneven panel gaps

Possible alignment clue

Compare the front, rear, upper and lower door gaps rather than judging a single point.

Striker moved

Why was adjustment needed?

Heavy striker adjustment can sometimes indicate previous hinge or body misalignment.

Overspray

Evidence of previous refinishing

Paint on seals, trim or hidden edges can point towards previous bodywork, although good repairs may leave little visible evidence.

Pillar repair

Inspect welds and geometry

Poorly repaired hinge or latch pillars can affect both structure and door alignment.

Replacement door

Check fit and operation

A replacement door can be perfectly acceptable if it is aligned, securely mounted and operates correctly.

Hard closing

Do not assume the latch is worn

A door that needs slamming after accident repair may be showing body, hinge or striker misalignment rather than an isolated latch fault.

Used-car clue: inspect the complete door opening

Do not look only at the door skin. Check the hinge pillar, latch pillar, sill, weather seals, panel gaps, striker position and how much force the door needs to close.

Pre-MOT inspection

What Should You Check on Car Doors Before an MOT?

A useful pre-MOT door check should test operation, security, alignment and surrounding structure rather than simply confirming that the doors appear closed.

Work through every relevant door in the same order so a broken handle, sticking latch, sagging hinge or structural problem is not missed.

Step 1

Open from outside

Use the normal outside control for each relevant driver and passenger door and confirm that the latch releases cleanly.

Step 2

Open front doors from inside

Check the driver and front passenger interior controls separately.

Step 3

Close each door normally

The door should latch securely without bouncing back, remaining partly open or needing excessive force.

Step 4

Check for door drop

Open the door and look for excessive vertical movement, sagging or a change in the panel gaps.

Step 5

Inspect latch and striker

Look for looseness, heavy wear marks, poor engagement and evidence that the latch is dragging across the striker.

Step 6

Check hinge security

Look for loose mountings, excessive hinge play, distorted brackets or movement in the supporting pillar.

Step 7

Test locking separately

Confirm manual and central-locking operation, but remember that remote locking convenience is not the same thing as mechanical latch security.

Step 8

Inspect rust and body damage

Check hinge pillars, latch pillars, sills, door edges and damaged body panels for corrosion, cracking or sharp metal.

Step 9

Watch how the door behaves

Rattling, bouncing, dragging, lifting to close or needing repeated slamming all deserve investigation before test day.

Test the complete operating sequence

A handle can move normally while the latch is failing, and central locking can appear to operate while the door is poorly retained. Always test opening, closing and security as separate functions.

Mechanic-style diagnosis

How to Diagnose a Car Door Fault Properly

Door faults are easier to diagnose when you follow the mechanical path from the driver's input to the final latch engagement rather than replacing the most obvious component first.

Step 1

Identify the exact symptom

Does the door fail to open, fail to close, drop, rattle, stick, remain locked or only fail on one operating control?

Step 2

Compare inside and outside operation

A difference between the two release paths can immediately narrow the fault towards a handle, cable or linkage rather than the latch itself.

Step 3

Check lock state

Confirm whether the latch is mechanically locked, electronically locked, deadlocked or simply not releasing.

Step 4

Inspect hinge movement

Check whether door weight causes vertical play or whether the hinge mounting itself moves against the pillar.

Step 5

Inspect latch-to-striker alignment

See whether the latch enters the striker cleanly or is being forced up, down or sideways because the door has moved.

Step 6

Check surrounding structure

Pillar corrosion, previous accident repair and distorted mounting points can create repeated door faults even when the latch and hinge are new.

A latch adjustment should not be used to hide a structural problem

If a door has dropped because the hinge, pillar or body structure has moved, repositioning the striker may restore closing temporarily without correcting the root cause.

MOT defect categories

When Is a Door Fault Minor, Major or Dangerous?

Door defects are not all graded at the same level. The MOT decision depends on the exact defect and whether it affects normal operation, secure attachment or the likelihood of the door opening inadvertently.

Minor

Excessive deterioration

Excessive deterioration of a door hinge, catch or pillar can be classed as Minor where the condition has not reached the more serious insecurity criteria.

Major

Required opening fails

A relevant door that cannot be opened using the required control can be classed as Major.

Major

Door will not close properly

Failure to close properly is a Major defect.

Major

Hinge, catch or pillar insecure

A missing or insecure hinge, catch or pillar is treated more seriously than deterioration that does not yet prevent normal operation.

Dangerous

Turning door likely to open

Where a turning door is likely to open inadvertently, the defect can be categorised as Dangerous.

Major / Dangerous bodywork

Injury or detachment risk

Sharp damaged bodywork can be Major, while a body component likely to become detached can reach the Dangerous category.

Dangerous means do not keep using the vehicle as though it merely needs a retest

A door that may open while the vehicle is moving creates an immediate safety issue. Repair the security fault rather than treating the MOT classification as an administrative problem.

Repair decision

Which Car Door Repairs Are DIY-Friendly?

Some door faults are suitable for a careful owner, while hinge, structural, electronic and accident-related repairs are better handled by a garage, auto electrician, locksmith or bodyshop.

Reasonable DIY checks

Low-risk inspection and maintenance

  • Check child-lock position.
  • Clean and inspect latch movement.
  • Lubricate an appropriate sticking mechanism.
  • Check obvious loose trim.
  • Inspect panel gaps and hinge movement.
  • Replace simple accessible handles if competent.
Garage / specialist

Higher-risk repair work

  • Door stuck completely shut.
  • Door will not remain latched.
  • Significant hinge or pillar movement.
  • Structural corrosion or welding.
  • Complex central-locking or wiring faults.
  • Accident-related alignment or body repair.
Be careful around side airbags and wiring inside doors

Modern door assemblies can contain side-impact sensors, airbag wiring, window mechanisms, electronic modules and sharp internal metal. Do not remove trim or disturb restraint-system wiring unless you understand the correct procedure for the vehicle.

Used-car inspection

What Can Car Doors Reveal When Buying a Used Car?

Doors can reveal much more than cosmetic condition. Poor alignment, difficult closing, striker adjustment and corrosion can provide clues about wear, previous accident repair and structural condition.

Cold inspection

Open every door yourself

Do not let the seller demonstrate only the easiest door. Operate each handle, lock and latch independently.

Panel gaps

Compare both sides of the car

Uneven gaps can indicate poor alignment, replacement panels or previous structural work.

Hinge movement

Check for vertical play

Excessive movement or a door that drops as it opens can indicate worn hinges or damaged mounting points.

Striker position

Look for heavy adjustment

Scratched paint around striker bolts or extreme striker movement can be evidence that alignment has been corrected after wear or body repair.

Paint evidence

Check hidden edges

Overspray, masking lines, different paint texture and paint on seals can suggest previous refinishing.

Corrosion

Inspect sill and pillars

Bubbling paint or previous welding around the sill, hinge pillar or latch pillar deserves more attention than a cosmetic mark on the outer skin.

A freshly adjusted door can hide an underlying issue

If one door closes differently from the others, has unusual panel gaps or shows signs that the striker has recently been moved, investigate why the adjustment was necessary.

Frequently asked questions

Car Door MOT FAQs

Can a car door fail an MOT?

Yes. A door can fail an MOT if it will not open using the relevant control, will not close properly, is likely to open inadvertently or has a missing or insecure hinge, catch or pillar. The exact result depends on the defect and how the door is intended to operate.

Can a door that will not open fail an MOT?

Yes. Driver and passenger doors must open from the outside using the relevant control. Driver and front passenger doors must also open from the inside using the relevant control. A door that will not open as required can be a Major defect.

Can a rear door that will not open from inside fail an MOT?

Not necessarily. The MOT requirement for opening from the inside specifically applies to the driver and front passenger doors. A rear passenger door still needs to meet the applicable outside-opening and closing requirements, and a genuine mechanical fault should not be confused with a correctly operating child lock.

Can a broken outside door handle fail an MOT?

It can. Driver and passenger doors must be capable of opening from the outside using the relevant control. The control does not have to be a conventional handle if the vehicle was designed with another operating method, such as a button or fob.

Can a broken inside door handle fail an MOT?

It can on the driver or front passenger door if the door cannot be opened from the inside using the relevant control. Rear passenger doors do not have the same inside-opening requirement under the standard car MOT door inspection.

Can a car door that will not close properly fail an MOT?

Yes. A door that will not close properly is a Major defect. If a turning door is likely to open inadvertently, the defect can be classed as Dangerous.

Can a faulty door latch fail an MOT?

Yes. A faulty latch or catch can fail the MOT if the door does not close properly, is likely to open inadvertently or the catch is missing or insecure. Slight deterioration alone is treated differently unless it affects the door's intended operation.

Can loose or worn door hinges fail an MOT?

Yes, depending on severity. An excessively deteriorated hinge, catch or pillar is a Minor defect, while a missing or insecure hinge, catch or pillar is a Major defect. Deterioration should only be rejected where it affects the door working as intended.

Can a sagging car door fail an MOT?

A sagging door can affect the MOT if hinge, catch or pillar deterioration prevents the door opening or closing properly, leaves the door insecure or creates another defined defect. Door drop by itself should be assessed by how it affects actual operation and security.

Does central locking have to work for an MOT?

Central locking itself is not the main MOT requirement. What matters is whether the relevant doors can be opened using the required controls and can be closed and secured correctly. A central-locking fault can therefore become an MOT issue if it prevents proper door operation.

Can a child lock cause an MOT failure?

A correctly operating child lock on a rear door is not the same as a broken inside handle or latch. The rear door still needs to satisfy the applicable MOT opening and closing requirements. If the mechanism remains faulty when the child lock is disengaged, further inspection is needed.

Can rust around a car door fail an MOT?

Yes, if corrosion affects door operation, security, a hinge, catch or relevant supporting structure, or creates another defined structural or body defect. Light cosmetic rust on a non-critical outer skin is not treated the same as corrosion compromising a load-bearing or safety-related area.

Can sharp damage on a car door fail an MOT?

Yes. A damaged or corroded body panel or component that is likely to cause injury when grazed or contacted is a Major defect. A body component likely to become detached can be classed as Dangerous.

Can a dented car door fail an MOT?

A cosmetic dent does not automatically cause an MOT failure. It becomes relevant if the damage prevents proper opening or closing, affects door security, creates dangerous sharp edges, weakens an important structure or leaves a body component insecure.

Can accident damage around a door fail an MOT?

Yes. Accident damage can affect hinges, catches, pillars, panel security, sharp edges and the ability of the door to open or close correctly. The MOT result depends on the actual condition rather than the fact that the vehicle has previously been repaired.

Can you pass an MOT with a missing outside door handle?

A missing conventional handle is not automatically a defect if that is part of the vehicle's original design or a specialist modification and the door can still be opened using the relevant control and latched securely in the closed position.

Should you repair a faulty door before the MOT?

Yes if the door does not open as required, does not close or latch securely, is loose on its hinges, has dangerous sharp damage or has corrosion affecting its operation or security. Repairing an obvious functional or safety defect before the test can avoid a failure or Dangerous result.

What should you check on car doors before an MOT?

Check that each relevant door opens using the required controls, closes and latches securely, does not drop excessively, remains properly attached and has no dangerous sharp damage or serious corrosion affecting hinges, catches, pillars or surrounding structure.

How Motor Vehicle Expert Writes MOT Door Guides

Motor Vehicle Expert creates UK MOT guidance by separating cosmetic condition, mechanical operation, access, security and structural condition. A dented panel, failed central-locking actuator, broken handle, worn hinge and unsafe latch can all involve the same door but have very different mechanical and MOT implications.

Our door guides follow the fault through the complete mechanism rather than treating the visible symptom as the diagnosis. We look at the outside and inside release controls, cables and linkages, latch and striker engagement, central locking, hinge movement, supporting pillars, corrosion and previous accident repair before deciding what the actual problem is likely to be.

MOT information is written around the applicable UK inspection criteria, including whether the required door opens using its relevant control, whether it closes properly, whether hinges, catches and pillars remain secure and whether body damage creates an injury or detachment risk. This avoids turning every cosmetic door fault into an MOT failure while still treating genuine safety defects seriously.

Motor Vehicle Expert also connects the MOT decision with practical repair planning and used-car inspection. Door drop, excessive striker adjustment, unusual panel gaps, corrosion around mounting points and poor closing can all reveal deeper hinge, structural or previous accident-repair problems that deserve investigation before money is spent on superficial repairs.
