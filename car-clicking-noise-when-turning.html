Quick Answer

What Causes a Clicking Noise When Turning?

The most common cause of repeated clicking on tight turns or full lock is a worn outer CV joint. The joint must transfer engine torque while operating at a large steering angle, so internal wear becomes easier to hear during slow, tight manoeuvres.

A split CV boot often comes first. Once grease escapes and contamination enters the joint, wear accelerates. Clicking can also come from driveshaft components, strut top mounts, ball joints, steering linkage, suspension joints, wheel-related faults or loose brake hardware.

Mechanic Diagnostic Method

How Mechanics Diagnose a Clicking Noise When Turning

A clicking noise when turning should be diagnosed by reproducing the exact condition that makes the sound appear. Steering angle, vehicle speed, acceleration, reversing, braking and road surface all help separate CV-joint wear from steering, suspension, wheel or brake-related faults.

The strongest clue is usually whether the clicking is rhythmic and repeated as the wheel rotates on a tight turn. That pattern is highly characteristic of outer CV-joint wear, especially when power is being applied.

1. Reproduce the sound

Confirm whether the clicking appears on full lock, gentle turns, reversing, acceleration or uneven road surfaces.

2. Listen for repetition

A repeated click-click-click that follows wheel rotation is a stronger CV-joint clue than one isolated pop or knock.

3. Add drive torque

Gentle acceleration through a tight turn increases load through the outer CV joint and can make wear easier to reproduce.

4. Compare reversing

Reversing on steering lock can expose a worn CV joint that is quieter during normal forward driving.

5. Check for grease

Grease around the inner wheel, arch liner or driveshaft is a strong clue that a CV boot has split.

6. Compare bumps and steering

If bumps create heavier clicks, pops or knocks, inspect suspension joints, bushes, links and top mounts.

7. Check wheel security

A new click after tyre, wheel or brake work should prompt inspection of fittings, hardware and anything recently disturbed.

8. Confirm physically

The suspected component should show wear, damage, looseness or another physical condition that matches the road-test symptom.

Repeated clicking is more diagnostic than a single click

Outer CV-joint wear usually creates a rhythm that repeats as the wheel rotates. A single pop, knock or twang is more likely to involve suspension movement, steering joints or a top mount.

Why Full Lock Matters

Why Full Lock Makes a Worn CV Joint Click

A CV joint allows the driveshaft to transfer engine torque while the wheel changes steering angle and suspension position. The outer joint works hardest when the steering is near full lock because it is operating at a large articulation angle.

If internal tracks or bearings are worn, the joint can move through that clearance repeatedly as it rotates. The result is the familiar rhythmic clicking or clacking heard during parking manoeuvres and tight turns.

Steering Angle

Joint Angle Increases

The outer CV joint operates through a larger angle as the steering approaches full lock.

Drive Torque

Load Rises Under Acceleration

Applying power while turning increases stress through the worn joint and often makes the clicking louder.

Rotation

The Noise Repeats

Internal wear creates a repeating sound as the joint rotates through its loaded position.

CV Boot Failure

Split CV Boot and Clicking When Turning

The CV boot seals grease inside the joint while keeping dirt, grit and water out. Once the rubber splits, grease can escape and road contamination can enter the joint.

If the boot is repaired early enough, the joint may still be serviceable. If clicking has already started, internal wear may have progressed far enough that the CV joint or complete driveshaft requires replacement.

Split or Torn Rubber

Cracks and tears usually appear around the boot folds or clamp areas.

Grease Around Wheel Area

Rotational force throws grease onto the arch liner, suspension and surrounding components.

Dirt Inside the Joint

Once contamination enters, internal tracks and bearings can wear quickly.

CV boot damage is cheaper to catch before clicking starts

If the boot is split but the joint is still smooth and quiet, repairing the boot early may prevent the cost of replacing the entire joint or driveshaft.

Driveshaft Faults

Can a Driveshaft Cause Clicking When Turning?

Yes. The driveshaft assembly includes the CV joints that allow power to reach the wheels while steering and suspension movement takes place. Wear in the joints or shaft assembly can produce clicking, knocking or vibration under load.

Outer Joint Wear

Usually produces repeated clicking during tight turns.

Inner Joint Wear

More often linked to vibration or shudder under acceleration than classic full-lock clicking.

Complete Shaft Replacement

Some vehicles are more economical to repair with a complete driveshaft rather than a separate CV joint.

Repeated clicking on full steering lock is the classic pattern associated with outer CV-joint wear. The diagnosis becomes stronger when the clicking repeats with wheel rotation and becomes louder while gentle power is applied.

Clicking When Reversing and Turning

Direction Clues

Clicking When Turning Left vs Turning Right

The direction of the turn is useful diagnostic information, but it does not prove which side has failed. Steering angle, torque and lateral load affect both sides differently, and sound can travel through the body and subframe.

Clicking Mainly Turning Left

Record whether the noise appears under power, on full lock or during ordinary cornering and inspect both front driveshafts.

Clicking Mainly Turning Right

The opposite steering direction can load the CV joints and suspension differently. Do not replace a joint based on direction alone.

Clicking Both Directions

Possible causes include more than one worn joint, suspension/steering wear, tyre scrub or another wheel-area fault.

Professional Diagnosis

Professional Diagnostic Workflow for Clicking When Turning

A professional diagnosis should establish exactly what makes the clicking appear before any CV joint, driveshaft or suspension component is replaced. Several front-end faults can create similar noises, so the technician needs to connect the road-test symptom with physical evidence.

The strongest initial distinction is whether the sound is a rhythmic click that repeats with wheel rotation or a single click, pop or knock caused by suspension or steering movement. Steering angle, drive torque, reversing, road bumps and braking then narrow the diagnosis further.

1. Confirm the complaint

Establish whether the sound is a click, clack, pop or knock and whether it comes from the front left, front right or another area.

2. Record when it happens

Full lock, ordinary cornering, acceleration, reversing, braking and uneven roads provide different diagnostic clues.

3. Inspect obvious faults first

Check tyre condition, wheel security, CV boots and visible grease before carrying out more involved testing.

4. Reproduce the noise

Use controlled low-speed manoeuvres to establish whether steering angle and wheel rotation control the clicking.

5. Add drive torque

Gentle acceleration during a tight turn places additional load through the CV joint and driveshaft.

6. Compare both directions

Turn left and right under similar conditions and record which direction changes the sound.

7. Inspect on the ramp

Check CV boots, joints, driveshafts, wheel bearings, steering joints, suspension components and brake hardware.

8. Confirm before repair

The failed component should explain both the physical wear found and the exact circumstances that produced the clicking.

Do not diagnose a CV joint from the word “clicking” alone

The strongest CV-joint pattern is repeated clicking that follows wheel rotation, becomes obvious at high steering angle and often gets louder when drive torque is applied.

Check wheel security before an extended road test

If the clicking began immediately after tyre or wheel work, or the wheel feels unstable, confirm wheel fitting and seating before reproducing the fault on the road.

Road-Test Procedure

How a Mechanic Road-Tests Clicking When Turning

A controlled road test changes one operating condition at a time. This allows the technician to distinguish a rotational CV-joint click from suspension movement, wheel-bearing noise, tyre scrub or loose wheel-area components.

Test 1

Straight-Line Baseline

Confirm whether the car is quiet while travelling straight ahead and whether vibration, humming or pulling is also present.

Test 2

Gentle Left and Right Turns

Compare ordinary cornering before moving towards full-lock testing.

Test 3

Full-Lock Manoeuvres

Tight low-speed turns place the outer CV joints through their greatest steering angles.

Test 4

Gentle Acceleration on Lock

Drive torque can make worn CV-joint tracks and bearings click more clearly.

Test 5

Reverse on Steering Lock

Reversing can make a developing CV-joint fault more obvious than forward driving.

Test 6

Uneven Road Surface

If bumps create the noise, suspension joints, links, bushes and top mounts move higher on the diagnostic list.

Workshop Inspection

What a Garage Checks for Clicking When Turning

After reproducing the clicking, a garage should inspect the systems that match the road-test behaviour. The inspection should begin with simple security and visible-condition checks before moving into joint play and more detailed drivetrain assessment.

CV Boots

Check for splits, cracking, missing clamps, grease leakage and contamination.

Outer CV Joints

Check for wear consistent with repeated full-lock clicking.

Driveshafts

Inspect shaft condition, inner and outer joints and abnormal movement.

Wheel Fittings

Confirm wheel security, correct seating and fitting condition.

Wheel Bearings

Check for roughness, abnormal noise and excessive movement.

Ball Joints

Inspect for excessive free play and damaged protective boots.

Track Rod Ends

Check steering-joint play, boot condition and security.

Strut Top Mounts

Look for rough rotation, binding, spring wind-up or excessive movement.

Suspension Bushes

Inspect for splitting, separation and excessive movement.

Anti-Roll Bar Links

Check for joint wear where clicking becomes a knock over broken surfaces.

Brake Hardware

Confirm caliper, pad hardware and shields are secure and correctly positioned.

Tyre & Wheel Damage

Check for pothole damage, bulges, distortion and abnormal clearance.

The road test should determine the inspection order

Full-lock rotational clicking should put the CV joint near the top of the list. Clicking over bumps should move the suspension higher. Road-speed humming should change the focus towards the wheel bearing and tyre.

A clicking noise is not automatically an emergency, but its severity increases when the sound becomes louder, changes into knocking or vibration, or appears alongside steering, suspension or wheel-security symptoms.

Driving Safety

Is It Safe to Drive With a Clicking Noise When Turning?

Whether it is safe to continue driving depends on what is causing the clicking and whether the vehicle still steers, brakes and tracks normally. A developing outer CV-joint fault may allow cautious driving to an inspection, while clicking linked to wheel insecurity, steering play or severe suspension wear needs much faster attention.

The noise becomes more concerning if it is getting louder, changes into a heavy knock, appears with vibration, or begins immediately after a pothole, tyre replacement, wheel removal or suspension work.

  • ✓ The vehicle does not pull strongly.
  • ✓ No severe vibration is present.
  • ✓ The clicking is mild and stable.

  • ! The steering feels loose or unpredictable.

Do not keep driving just because the car still moves normally

Clicking caused by a worn CV joint can worsen gradually, while wheel or steering-related clicking can be more urgent. If the symptoms change, reassess the risk rather than relying on how the car behaved earlier.

Frequently Asked Questions

Car Clicking Noise When Turning FAQs

These questions cover the most common CV-joint, steering, suspension, wheel, safety, MOT and repair-cost concerns associated with clicking while turning.

Why does my car click when turning?

A car clicking when turning is commonly caused by a worn outer CV joint, split CV boot, driveshaft wear, steering linkage fault, suspension joint wear, wheel-related issue or loose component.

Why does my car click on full lock?

Repeated clicking on full lock, especially during slow tight turns while accelerating, commonly points towards a worn outer CV joint.

Can a worn CV joint cause clicking when turning?

Yes. A worn outer CV joint commonly produces a rhythmic clicking or clacking noise when the driveshaft is transferring power at a high steering angle.

Can a split CV boot cause clicking?

Yes. A split CV boot allows grease to escape and dirt or water to enter the joint. If the joint runs contaminated or without enough grease, internal wear can develop and clicking may begin during turns.

Why is there grease around my wheel with clicking when turning?

Grease around the inner wheel, suspension or wheel arch can indicate a split CV boot. Rotational force throws grease from the damaged boot as the driveshaft turns.

Why does my car click when turning left?

Turning left changes the angle and load through the CV joints, wheel bearings, steering and suspension. The noisy side should be confirmed by road testing and physical inspection because sound can travel through the body.

Why does my car click when turning right?

Turning right changes load through the driveshafts, steering, suspension and wheel bearings. The direction is a useful clue, but it does not prove which side has failed without inspection.

Why does my car click when reversing and turning?

A worn outer CV joint can become very noticeable when reversing on steering lock because the joint is operating at a large angle while drive torque is being applied.

Can suspension parts cause clicking when turning?

Yes. Ball joints, suspension bushes, anti-roll bar links, strut top mounts and other worn components can click, knock or pop as steering and suspension loads change.

Can a strut top mount click when turning?

Yes. A worn or binding strut top mount or bearing can click, pop, creak or twang as the strut and spring assembly rotate with the steering.

Can a wheel bearing cause clicking when turning?

Wheel bearings more commonly hum, drone or rumble, but severe wear or movement can create other noises. Turning can change the sound because lateral bearing load changes.

Why did my car start clicking after hitting a pothole?

A pothole can damage a tyre, wheel, steering joint, suspension component or wheel bearing and may also expose wear that was already developing.

Why did clicking start after tyre or wheel work?

A new clicking noise after tyre or wheel work should be checked promptly. Wheel fittings, wheel seating, trims, centre caps, tyre clearance and anything disturbed during the work should be inspected.

Is it safe to drive with a clicking noise when turning?

It depends on the cause. Mild clicking from a developing CV joint may allow cautious driving to inspection, but avoid driving if the noise is worsening or accompanied by loose steering, vibration, strong pulling, wheel movement or concern about wheel security.

When should I stop driving with clicking when turning?

Stop driving if clicking is accompanied by unstable steering, severe vibration, a heavy knock, obvious wheel movement, tyre damage, grinding or suspicion that a wheel, steering or suspension component is insecure.

Can clicking when turning fail an MOT?

The clicking noise itself is only a symptom, but the underlying fault can affect the MOT if it involves a defective CV boot, excessive steering or suspension play, wheel-bearing wear, insecure components or unsafe wheel fittings.

How much does it cost to fix clicking when turning in the UK?

Repair cost depends on the cause. A CV boot may be relatively inexpensive if caught early, while CV joints, complete driveshafts, suspension components, wheel bearings or steering repairs can cost several hundred pounds.

What is the best first check for clicking when turning?

First identify whether the clicking happens on full lock, during gentle turns, while accelerating or when reversing. Then inspect for a split CV boot, escaped grease, tyre or wheel damage and anything loose before arranging a front-end inspection.
