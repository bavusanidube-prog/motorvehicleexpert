UK MOT Advisory Guide

Exhaust Corrosion Advisory Explained

An exhaust corrosion advisory means the MOT tester has identified rust or deterioration on part of the exhaust system, but the condition had not reached the applicable rejection threshold when the vehicle was tested. The important question is not simply whether the exhaust looks rusty, but whether corrosion is beginning to weaken the pipework, silencers, joints or mountings and develop into leakage, insecurity, excessive noise, fume ingress or an emissions problem.

Quick Answer

Is an Exhaust Corrosion Advisory Serious?

It depends on what the corrosion has done to the exhaust. Light surface oxidation on an otherwise solid and secure exhaust may only need monitoring. Corrosion becomes more important when it thins the metal, attacks seams or welded joints, weakens mounting points or creates holes and leaks.

The key MOT distinction is that corrosion itself is not automatically a failure. The tester assesses the condition that corrosion has produced. A major exhaust leak or an insecure exhaust is a Major defect. Exhaust fumes entering the cabin are also Major, while fumes creating a danger to occupants' health are Dangerous.

Excessive exhaust noise and some exhaust leaks that can affect emissions can also create MOT failures. That is why an exhaust corrosion advisory should be treated as an early warning rather than simply ignored until the next test.

The most important first checks

Is it actually leaking?

Listen for blowing or hissing and inspect the suspected area for soot staining, perforation or split seams.

Is the exhaust secure?

Check whether pipes, silencers and brackets remain correctly supported without excessive movement or risk of detachment.

Where is the corrosion?

Corrosion around a simple rear silencer is a different repair proposition from deterioration around a catalytic converter, DPF, flexi section or integrated emissions component.

Mechanic interpretation

The correct diagnosis is not simply “the exhaust is rusty”. Determine whether the corrosion is superficial, whether the metal is losing strength, whether gases can escape and whether the exhaust remains securely supported.

Fumes inside the vehicle need urgent attention

If you smell exhaust gases inside the passenger compartment, do not treat the problem as routine corrosion maintenance. Exhaust fumes entering the cabin are specifically covered by MOT rejection criteria and can also present a serious health risk.

Do not wait for the exhaust to fall apart

Corrosion often progresses gradually through thinning metal, weakened seams and failing joints before an obvious hole develops. An advisory gives you an opportunity to inspect and budget before a small deterioration becomes a noisy leak or a more expensive roadside failure.

Advisory Meaning

What Does an Exhaust Corrosion Advisory Mean?

An exhaust corrosion advisory means the MOT tester has seen deterioration somewhere on the exhaust system but, at the time of inspection, the condition had not reached the applicable MOT rejection threshold.

The wording does not automatically mean that the exhaust needs to be replaced immediately. It means the tester has identified a condition worth bringing to the driver's attention because corrosion can continue to weaken exhaust pipework, silencers, joints, seams, brackets or mountings after the test.

The important mechanical question is: what effect has the corrosion had on the exhaust? A rusty-looking but solid exhaust can be very different from a heavily scaled system that is beginning to perforate, leak or lose mounting strength.

Repair priority

Why an advisory should not be ignored

Exhaust corrosion is usually progressive. Moisture, salt, heat cycling and vibration continue acting on already weakened metal, so a section that was only advisory-level at one MOT can become perforated, insecure or excessively noisy before the next test.

An advisory describes condition, not a guaranteed remaining life

An MOT tester cannot tell you that a corroded exhaust will last another twelve months. The test records the vehicle's condition on the day. Remaining service life depends on corrosion depth, material, location, vehicle usage and how quickly deterioration continues.

Ask which exact part was advisory

“Exhaust corroded” is not enough information for a repair decision. Find out whether the affected area is a rear silencer, centre pipe, flexi section, joint, bracket, catalytic converter casing, DPF section or another component. Repair cost and urgency can differ considerably.

MOT Wording

How to Interpret Exhaust Corrosion MOT Wording

Drivers often assume that words such as corroded, rusty, deteriorated, leaking and insecure all mean the same thing. Mechanically, they describe different conditions.

A good interpretation separates the condition into metal deterioration, gas sealing, component security and system function.

1. Corrosion

Rust or material deterioration is present, but the remaining metal may still be structurally sound.

2. Leakage

Exhaust gases are escaping through a hole, split, failed seam, flexi section or leaking joint.

3. Insecurity

A pipe, silencer, bracket or mounting is no longer held securely in its intended position.

4. Functional effect

Noise, fumes, emissions or component movement may now be affected by the deterioration.

Advisory wording does not equal a failure category

A recorded advisory means the tester considered the deterioration worth reporting but did not judge it to meet the relevant rejection standard on that test day. If the exhaust later develops a major leak, loses security or produces excessive noise, the MOT outcome can be different.

Do not judge severity from the word “corroded” alone

Two vehicles can receive similar advisory wording while having very different remaining exhaust life. One may have minor surface rust; another may have deep scaling around a seam that is close to perforating.

The location matters

Corrosion around a removable clamp or rear silencer can be easier and cheaper to address than deterioration around integrated emissions equipment, difficult flanges or heavily seized joints.

Key Difference

Surface Rust vs Serious Exhaust Corrosion

Exhaust systems live underneath the vehicle where they are exposed to water, road contamination, salt and repeated heating and cooling. Some surface oxidation is therefore common, particularly on older mild-steel exhaust components.

The concern increases when corrosion stops being superficial and begins reducing wall thickness, weakening seams, attacking welded joints or affecting mounting points.

Five clues corrosion is more than cosmetic

Heavy flakes

Thick layers of rust breaking away can indicate considerable loss of material beneath the surface.

Deep pitting

Localised deep corrosion can weaken thin-wall exhaust tubing even when a complete hole is not yet visible.

Rust around seams

Silencer seams and welded joints are common weak points because corrosion can concentrate at edges and heat-affected areas.

Soot staining

Black soot around a corroded seam or joint can indicate that exhaust gases are already escaping.

Distorted casing

A silencer that looks swollen, fractured or structurally weak may be approaching failure.

Movement at mounts

Corrosion around brackets or welded hangers can reduce support and allow the exhaust to move excessively.

Stainless steel does not mean corrosion-proof

Stainless exhaust systems generally resist corrosion better than conventional mild-steel systems, but joints, clamps, welds, brackets and mixed-metal connections can still deteriorate.

A small visible hole can hide wider metal thinning

Before fitting a local patch or sleeve, inspect the surrounding metal. Repairing one perforation is poor value if the adjacent section is already thin enough to fail shortly afterwards.

Common Areas

Where Exhaust Corrosion Commonly Develops

Exhaust corrosion does not develop evenly across the system. Condensation, road spray, temperature, vibration, welds, joints and component design create localised areas where deterioration may start or accelerate.

Rear Silencer

Back box casing and seams

Rear silencers are common corrosion points. Moisture can collect internally while road spray and salt attack the outer casing. Seams and welded hangers deserve close inspection.

Centre Section

Centre pipe and silencer

Long centre sections are exposed to road contamination and can deteriorate around pipe bends, silencer seams, joints and mounting points.

Joints

Clamps, sleeves and flanges

Connections trap moisture and can seize together. Corrosion here may cause leaks or make what should be a simple section replacement much harder.

Mountings

Brackets and welded hangers

A silencer body may still be gas-tight while corrosion around its hanger or mounting bracket weakens component support.

Flexi Section

Flexible exhaust joint

Flexi sections accommodate engine movement and vibration. Their braid, inner liner and welded ends can deteriorate, split or leak.

Front Pipe

Engine-side pipework

Front pipes experience high temperatures and repeated engine movement. Flanges and welded joints can suffer from both heat and corrosion.

Catalyst Area

Catalytic converter casing

Corrosion around the casing, connecting flanges or welded pipes can be more expensive than ordinary rear exhaust deterioration because the catalyst assembly may form part of the replacement.

Diesel Exhaust

DPF and sensor connections

Diesel systems can incorporate a DPF, pressure pipes, temperature sensors and other emissions hardware. Corrosion around these areas can increase repair complexity.

Tailpipe

Tailpipe and rear outlet

The tailpipe is exposed to external moisture while condensation can also be present internally. Corrosion may develop around the outlet and its connection to the rear silencer.

Rear-box corrosion does not automatically mean the whole exhaust needs replacing

Many exhaust systems are divided into replaceable sections. Determine which parts are genuinely deteriorated before approving a complete system replacement.

Emissions components can change the economics of the repair

Corrosion near a catalytic converter, DPF, sensor boss or integrated exhaust manifold may turn what looks like a simple rust problem into a considerably more involved repair.

Why It Happens

What Causes Exhaust Systems to Corrode?

Exhaust corrosion develops because the system is attacked from both outside and inside. Road water and salt reach the external surfaces, while combustion produces water vapour that can condense inside colder sections of the exhaust.

The combination of moisture, heat, oxygen, contaminants and repeated expansion and contraction gradually degrades susceptible metals.

External Moisture

Rain and road spray

Water repeatedly wets the underside of the exhaust and encourages oxidation where protective coatings are damaged or absent.

Winter Exposure

Road salt

Salt contamination can accelerate corrosion of exhaust pipes, brackets, clamps, seams and fasteners.

Internal Moisture

Condensation

Water is produced during combustion. When exhaust gases cool, moisture can condense inside silencers and pipework.

Driving Pattern

Repeated short journeys

Short journeys may not keep the exhaust hot long enough to evaporate accumulated condensation from the rear sections.

Temperature

Heat cycling

Repeated heating and cooling causes expansion and contraction, placing stress on seams, welds, brackets and joints.

Mechanical Load

Engine and road vibration

Constant movement can fatigue already weakened seams, flexi sections and brackets once corrosion has reduced material strength.

Age

Long-term material deterioration

Older exhaust systems have experienced more moisture, salt, temperature cycles and mechanical vibration.

Surface Damage

Lost protective coating

Scraping, impact damage and manufacturing seams can expose areas where corrosion establishes more quickly.

Mixed Metals

Clamps and replacement sections

Replacement components, clamps and fasteners made from different materials can corrode at different rates and create local weak points.

Why short journeys can be hard on an exhaust

When a vehicle completes a long enough journey, exhaust temperature rises and can help evaporate moisture from much of the system. Repeated short journeys can leave the rear silencer and tail section cooler, allowing internal condensation to remain for longer.

Corrosion often attacks from the inside as well as the outside

A rear silencer can sometimes look acceptable externally while internal deterioration is already progressing. Changes in sound, loose internal baffles or corrosion around drain points and seams can provide useful clues.

Warning Signs

Signs an Exhaust Corrosion Advisory Is Getting Worse

Exhaust corrosion does not always produce symptoms while it is superficial. Once metal begins perforating, seams split or mountings weaken, the vehicle may provide several warning signs.

Sound

Louder exhaust note

A sudden or progressive increase in exhaust volume can indicate a developing hole, failed joint or split silencer seam.

Leak

Blowing or hissing

A rhythmic blowing, ticking or hissing noise can indicate gases escaping from a pipe, flange, flexi section or corroded casing.

Movement

Rattling or knocking

Worn rubber mounts, broken brackets or weakened hangers can allow the exhaust to contact the body, axle or heat shields.

Position

Exhaust hanging low

A change in exhaust position can indicate a failed mounting, fractured hanger or weakened pipe connection.

Visual

Holes or heavy rust flakes

Perforation, deep scaling and falling metal flakes suggest that deterioration is progressing beyond light surface corrosion.

Leakage Clue

Black soot marks

Dark deposits around joints, seams or holes can indicate escaping exhaust gas.

Cabin Safety

Exhaust smell inside the car

Exhaust odour entering the passenger compartment requires prompt investigation because a leak may be allowing fumes to reach the cabin.

Urgent inspection
Performance

Engine or emissions symptoms

Some upstream leaks can disturb exhaust sensor readings or affect emissions-system operation, depending on leak location and vehicle design.

History

Repeated MOT advisory

The same corrosion advisory appearing across several MOTs can indicate persistent deterioration that deserves closer inspection before the metal finally fails.

A rattle is not automatically exhaust corrosion

Loose heat shields, damaged rubber hangers, internal silencer baffles and nearby underbody components can all create similar noises. The source should be confirmed before replacement parts are ordered.

Mechanic Inspection

How a Mechanic Checks Exhaust Corrosion

A proper exhaust corrosion inspection does more than confirm that rust is visible. The aim is to determine exactly which component is affected, how much sound metal remains, whether the exhaust is still gas-tight and whether every section remains securely supported.

A mechanic will normally assess the system from the engine-side connections towards the rear of the vehicle, checking pipework, flexible sections, silencers, seams, joints, clamps, flanges, emissions components and mountings as one connected system.

Step 1

Identify the exact area

Establish whether the advisory relates to a rear silencer, centre section, front pipe, flexi joint, clamp, mounting, catalytic converter, DPF or another exhaust component.

Step 2

Assess corrosion depth

Look beyond colour alone. Check for pitting, scaling, flaking, thinning, swelling, cracking and perforation around the affected metal.

Step 3

Check seams and welds

Silencer seams, welded hangers and pipe connections often weaken before the larger flat areas of the exhaust casing.

Step 4

Check system security

Confirm that pipes and silencers are correctly supported and that brackets, clamps and rubber mounts are not allowing excessive movement.

Step 5

Inspect the joints

Flanges, sleeves and clamps are checked for corrosion, looseness, soot staining and signs that gases are escaping.

Step 6

Check for exhaust leakage

With the vehicle safely positioned and the exhaust operating, listen for abnormal blowing or ticking and inspect accessible areas for evidence of leakage.

Step 7

Check component clearance

A weakened mounting can allow the exhaust to strike the body, suspension, heat shields or road surface.

Step 8

Decide repair scope

Determine whether the fault can reasonably be repaired locally or whether the affected exhaust section is too deteriorated to retain.

What the mechanic is really trying to establish

Is the metal still strong?

Surface corrosion can look worse than it is. Conversely, heavy scaling can leave very little usable metal beneath the visible rust.

Is the system still gas-tight?

Perforation, failed seams, loose joints and damaged flexible sections can allow exhaust gases to escape.

Is the system securely mounted?

A gas-tight exhaust can still be unsafe if corrosion has weakened a bracket, hanger or attachment point.

Never work underneath a vehicle supported only by a jack

Exhaust inspection often requires access underneath the vehicle. Use proper lifting equipment and approved support points. A vehicle jack is designed to raise the car, not to provide safe working support underneath it.

Remember that exhaust components become extremely hot

Do not touch exhaust pipework, silencers, catalytic converters or DPF components immediately after driving. Allow sufficient cooling time before carrying out a close physical inspection.

Inspect beyond the exact advisory point

If one section is badly corroded, inspect neighbouring sections before ordering parts. Replacing one silencer only to discover that the adjoining pipe or flange is too weak to reuse can increase both labour time and repair cost.

Leak Diagnosis

How Exhaust Leaks Are Diagnosed

Corrosion becomes much more important once it compromises the gas-tightness of the exhaust system. A leak can occur through a perforated pipe, failed silencer seam, fractured flexi section, damaged flange, loose clamp or another connection.

The diagnosis should confirm the source rather than assuming that the loudest area is automatically where the metal has failed. Exhaust noise can travel along pipework and reflect from the underside of the vehicle.

Check 1

Cold visual inspection

Before the exhaust becomes hot, inspect accessible seams, joints, flexi sections and corroded areas for holes, cracks and soot staining.

Check 2

Listen after start-up

A leaking exhaust may produce a blowing, puffing, ticking or hissing noise that changes with engine speed.

Check 3

Locate the sound

Determine whether the noise comes from the engine-side pipework, flexi section, centre exhaust, rear silencer or a connection between sections.

Check 4

Confirm with workshop testing

Where a small leak is difficult to locate, a garage may use smoke testing or other controlled diagnostic methods to identify the leaking point.

Useful leak clues

Visual

Black soot deposit

Dry black staining around a seam, clamp, flange or small hole can indicate repeated gas escape.

Sound

Rhythmic puffing

A small leak may produce a regular puffing or ticking sound in time with the engine's exhaust pulses.

Noise

Deep blowing sound

Larger perforations or split silencers can produce a much deeper and more obvious exhaust note.

Flexi

Noise changes with engine movement

A damaged flexi section may become louder as the engine moves under acceleration or load.

Cabin

Fume smell

Exhaust odour around or inside the vehicle can indicate a leak, but the source must be located rather than guessed.

Electronics

Sensor or emissions symptoms

Depending on its location, a leak may disturb exhaust sensor readings and contribute to warning lights or emissions problems.

Exhaust leak location matters

Do not deliberately inhale exhaust fumes while looking for a leak

Exhaust gases can be harmful. Diagnosis should be carried out in a properly ventilated environment, and suspected cabin fume ingress should be treated seriously.

Avoid unsafe tailpipe-blocking methods

Do not place your hand over a hot tailpipe or use improvised objects to obstruct the exhaust while working underneath the vehicle. Controlled workshop leak-testing methods are safer when a leak cannot be found visually or acoustically.

Do not condemn a complete exhaust from noise alone

A failed gasket, clamp, sleeve or flexi section can sometimes create a surprisingly loud noise while the rest of the exhaust remains serviceable. Locate the defect before deciding the repair scope.

Differential Diagnosis

Other Faults That Can Look Like Exhaust Corrosion

A rusty exhaust may be visible during inspection, but that does not prove corrosion is responsible for every noise, vibration, warning light or emissions problem. Several other faults can produce similar symptoms.

Mounting Fault

Failed rubber exhaust hanger

A stretched or split rubber mount can allow an otherwise sound exhaust to hang low or knock against the body.

Heat Shield

Loose heat shield

Thin metal heat shields can loosen around their fixings and create a metallic rattle that sounds like internal exhaust damage.

Joint Fault

Failed gasket

A flange gasket can leak even when the adjoining exhaust pipes are not seriously corroded.

Clamp

Loose exhaust clamp

A loose or incorrectly positioned clamp can produce leakage, movement or rattling without requiring complete exhaust replacement.

Internal Failure

Loose silencer baffle

Internal silencer components can break loose and rattle even if the outer casing has not yet perforated.

Flexi

Flexi fatigue

Repeated engine movement can damage a flexi section independently of widespread exhaust corrosion.

Engine Movement

Worn engine or gearbox mount

Excessive powertrain movement can pull or twist the front exhaust and cause knocking, flexi stress or joint leakage.

Catalyst

Catalytic converter failure

Internal catalyst deterioration can cause rattling, poor performance or emissions faults even when external corrosion is not the main problem.

Diesel System

DPF-related fault

DPF restriction, sensor faults or regeneration problems can cause warning lights and performance symptoms unrelated to ordinary exhaust surface rust.

Diagnose the symptom and the corrosion separately

A vehicle can have an exhaust corrosion advisory and an unrelated exhaust noise at the same time. Confirm cause and effect before authorising replacement parts.

This matters particularly when buying a used car

Do not accept “it only needs an exhaust” as a diagnosis for every rattle, warning light or loss of power. Expensive catalyst, DPF, sensor or engine faults can sometimes be hidden behind a vague exhaust explanation.

Driving Safety

Can You Drive With an Exhaust Corrosion Advisory?

An advisory does not automatically mean the vehicle must be taken off the road. If the exhaust remains structurally sound, securely mounted and gas-tight and the vehicle remains roadworthy, continued driving may be reasonable while the deterioration is monitored or a repair is arranged.

The decision changes once corrosion creates a leak, weakens a mounting, allows the exhaust to hang down, causes significant noise or results in exhaust gases entering the passenger compartment.

What if the car still has a valid MOT?

A valid MOT does not guarantee that a deteriorating exhaust will remain safe until the certificate expires. The vehicle must remain roadworthy between tests, and exhaust condition can change significantly after the MOT inspection.

If a previously advisory-level exhaust starts blowing, moving, dragging or allowing fumes into the vehicle, judge it by its current condition, not by the fact that it passed an MOT several months earlier.

Stop and reassess if the exhaust becomes loose or starts dragging

A partially detached exhaust can strike the road, damage other components or separate from the vehicle. Do not attempt to complete a normal journey simply because the original MOT entry was only an advisory.

Fumes inside the cabin require urgent investigation

Do not rely on opening a window as a repair. The source of the leak and the route by which gases are entering the vehicle need to be identified and corrected.

Repair timing should follow condition, not the MOT calendar

If deterioration is already advanced, repairing the exhaust while the neighbouring joints and fixings are still serviceable can be easier than waiting for a complete roadside failure or a heavily seized system at the next MOT.

Current MOT Rules

Can Exhaust Corrosion Fail an MOT?

Exhaust corrosion does not automatically fail an MOT simply because rust is visible. The important question is whether that deterioration has created a defect covered by the MOT inspection criteria.

Under the current DVSA MOT inspection manual, the tester assesses the overall security of the exhaust system and checks for significant leakage and fume entry. Exhaust noise and emissions-related defects are assessed separately under the nuisance and emissions sections of the manual.

The key distinction

Rust is the deterioration mechanism. The MOT decision is based on the condition it has produced. Surface corrosion may remain advisory-level, while the same corrosion can eventually create a major leak, insecurity, excessive noise or another rejectable defect.

Current MOT exhaust defect categories

MOT condition Defect category What it means
Exhaust system has a major leak Major The exhaust is no longer sufficiently gas-tight and the leakage meets the MOT rejection threshold.
Exhaust system is insecure Major The system does not have adequate overall security.
Exhaust fumes entering cabin Major Exhaust gases are entering the passenger compartment.
Exhaust fumes causing danger to occupants' health Dangerous Fume exposure has reached the more serious DVSA danger threshold.

What does not automatically fail?

Appearance

Surface rust alone

Visible oxidation is not automatically a reason for rejection if the exhaust remains secure, adequately gas-tight and otherwise compliant with the test requirements.

Mountings

One defective mounting

DVSA guidance specifically states that one or more missing or defective exhaust mountings does not necessarily make the complete exhaust system insecure.

Advisory

Previous corrosion advisory

An advisory recorded last year is not itself a failure this year. The tester assesses the condition actually present on the current test date.

Do not confuse a corroded exhaust with structural body corrosion

Exhaust pipework and silencers are assessed under the exhaust-system criteria. Corrosion of vehicle structure, prescribed suspension or braking areas is assessed under different MOT sections and can have different rejection rules.

The MOT tester assesses the whole condition

A rusty hanger does not automatically mean failure, just as a rusty silencer does not automatically pass. What matters is the resulting security, leakage, noise, fumes and other testable effects.

Repair Decisions

Should a Corroded Exhaust Be Repaired or Replaced?

The best repair depends on how localised the deterioration is and how much usable metal remains around it. A clamp, gasket, mounting or small local defect can sometimes be repaired economically, while a deeply corroded silencer or pipe section is usually better replaced.

The objective is not simply to stop today's noise. A good repair should restore gas sealing and security without attaching new components to metal that is already close to failing.

Local Repair

Clamp, sleeve or joint repair

Suitable where the surrounding pipework remains structurally sound and the defect is limited to a connection or serviceable joint.

Often economical
Component Repair

Hanger or mounting repair

A failed rubber hanger or replaceable bracket may be repairable without replacing otherwise healthy exhaust sections.

Inspect surrounding metal
Replacement

Corroded pipe or silencer

Replacement is usually more sensible where the casing is deeply pitted, extensively scaled, perforated or too thin to support a durable repair.

Replace affected section

When a local repair may make sense

Sound surrounding metal

The repair area should have sufficient solid material around it to support a durable connection or repair.

Isolated joint problem

A leaking clamp, sleeve, flange or gasket may not justify replacing a complete exhaust section if the components remain usable.

Replaceable mounting

Rubber mounts and some brackets can often be replaced separately when the exhaust itself is still sound.

When replacement is usually the better decision

Thin Metal

Extensive scaling

There is little value repairing one hole if neighbouring metal is already close to perforation.

Multiple Faults

Several weak seams

Multiple leak points or deteriorated seams suggest the complete section is reaching the end of its useful life.

Mounting Failure

Hanger tearing from silencer

If corrosion has weakened the silencer casing around a welded hanger, simply fitting a new rubber mount will not restore the damaged metal.

Perforation

Repeated patch failures

Repeated temporary repairs often indicate that the surrounding exhaust section is too deteriorated for another economical patch.

Seized Joints

Connections cannot be separated cleanly

Severe corrosion can seize flanges, clamps and bolts together, sometimes increasing the number of components needed during replacement.

Integrated Part

Catalyst or DPF assembly affected

If corroded pipework forms part of an integrated emissions component, replacement choices may be more limited and vehicle specific.

Sealants can be useful in appropriate joints and some minor repair situations, but they cannot restore the strength of severely thinned or perforated exhaust metal.

DIY vs Garage

Can You Repair Exhaust Corrosion Yourself?

Some exhaust jobs are mechanically simple in principle, but access, seized fasteners, hot components, vehicle lifting and heavily corroded joints can make even a basic replacement difficult in practice.

The right DIY decision depends on whether you can support the vehicle safely, identify the actual fault and remove the affected section without damaging adjoining components.

DIY Possible

Simple rubber mounting

Some exhaust rubber hangers can be replaced with basic hand tools once the vehicle and exhaust are safely supported.

Suitable for competent DIY
Moderate DIY

Clamp-on rear section

A separate rear silencer may be straightforward where joints are accessible and not heavily seized.

Access dependent
Garage Recommended

Heavily seized system

Corroded bolts, flanges and sleeves may need heat, cutting tools or workshop equipment and can damage adjoining parts during removal.

Professional repair

Jobs usually better suited to a garage

Welding

Exhaust welding requires suitable equipment, competent technique and enough sound material for a reliable repair.

Flexi replacement

Some flexi sections are welded into larger pipe assemblies rather than simply bolted or clamped into position.

Catalyst / DPF work

Emissions components can involve sensors, seized fixings, expensive parts and diagnostic checks after replacement.

Manifold-side repairs

Restricted access and the risk of broken studs can make front-end exhaust work considerably more involved.

Difficult leak diagnosis

Smoke testing and workshop lifting equipment can make small or hidden leaks much easier to locate accurately.

Extensive corrosion

When several sections are deteriorated, a garage can assess what will separate cleanly and what should be replaced together.

DIY safety checklist

  • ✓ Allow the exhaust to cool fully before touching components.
  • ✓ Never work beneath a vehicle supported only by a jack.
  • ✓ Support heavy exhaust sections before removing clamps or hangers.
  • ✓ Wear eye protection when disturbing rust and scale underneath the vehicle.
  • ✓ Expect heavily corroded bolts and clamps to break rather than undo cleanly.
  • ✓ Check that replacement parts match the exact vehicle and emissions specification.
  • ✓ Recheck exhaust clearance and security after installation.
Never enter an enclosed space with the engine running to diagnose an exhaust leak

Exhaust gases can accumulate rapidly. Running-engine diagnosis requires appropriate ventilation and safe working practices.

Be careful with emissions-system parts

Catalytic converters, DPF assemblies, oxygen sensors and temperature sensors can be expensive. Confirm that corrosion is actually the root problem before replacing them.

Next MOT Risk

Will an Exhaust Corrosion Advisory Fail the Next MOT?

Not automatically. An exhaust corrosion advisory recorded on a previous MOT does not become a failure simply because another year has passed. The exhaust will be judged on its actual condition when the next test is carried out.

The concern is that corrosion normally progresses rather than repairs itself. Metal can become thinner, silencer seams can perforate, brackets can weaken and joints can begin leaking between one MOT and the next.

The likelihood of a future MOT failure therefore depends on how advanced the deterioration already is, where it is located and whether it begins affecting exhaust security, gas sealing, noise, cabin fumes or emissions.

Lower Risk

Light surface corrosion

Minor oxidation on sound metal may remain serviceable for some time if the exhaust stays secure and gas-tight.

Inspect periodically
Rising Risk

Heavy scaling or pitting

Deep deterioration suggests less sound metal remains and increases the chance of perforation before the next test.

Plan repair
High Risk

Noise, leakage or movement

Once corrosion starts producing blowing, insecurity or significant movement, the likelihood of a future MOT rejection is much higher.

Repair promptly

Signs the next MOT risk is increasing

Advisory appears repeatedly

Several MOT records mentioning the same exhaust area suggest persistent deterioration rather than an isolated observation.

Exhaust is becoming louder

More rust is flaking away

Soot appears around joints

Mountings are deteriorating

Repair has already been patched

Before the next MOT

  • ✓ Compare the current exhaust condition with the previous MOT advisory.
  • ✓ Listen for new blowing, ticking, hissing or excessive exhaust noise.
  • ✓ Check that the exhaust remains securely mounted.
  • ✓ Inspect heavily corroded seams, joints and silencer casings.
  • ✓ Investigate any exhaust smell inside the passenger compartment.
  • ✓ Repair known deterioration before it becomes a test-day failure.
Do not use the next MOT as the repair deadline

If the exhaust begins leaking, moving excessively or allowing fumes into the vehicle, deal with its current condition rather than waiting for the MOT expiry date.

Early repair can protect adjoining sections

Waiting until joints and fixings are completely seized can turn an otherwise straightforward section replacement into a larger repair.

Used Car Buying

An exhaust corrosion advisory does not automatically make a used car a bad purchase. Many older vehicles develop some exhaust oxidation, and a separate rear silencer or centre section may be relatively straightforward to replace.

Frequently Asked Questions

Exhaust Corrosion Advisory FAQs

These answers cover the most common questions UK drivers ask after seeing exhaust corrosion recorded on an MOT certificate.

What does an exhaust corrosion advisory mean?

An exhaust corrosion advisory means the tester has identified rust or deterioration on part of the exhaust system, but the condition had not reached the applicable MOT rejection threshold at the time of the test.

Is an exhaust corrosion advisory serious?

It can be. Light surface corrosion may only need monitoring, while corrosion that weakens joints, silencers, pipework or mountings can progress into leakage, insecurity, excessive noise or another MOT failure condition.

Does exhaust corrosion automatically fail an MOT?

No. Corrosion itself does not automatically fail the MOT. The tester assesses whether deterioration has resulted in a rejectable condition such as a major exhaust leak, insecurity, excessive noise, dangerous fumes or an emissions-related defect.

What is the difference between surface rust and serious exhaust corrosion?

Surface rust affects the outer metal without necessarily reducing its strength significantly, while serious corrosion can thin, perforate, split or weaken the exhaust around pipes, seams, welds, silencers, joints or mounting points.

Can a corroded exhaust leak?

Yes. Corrosion can thin the metal until holes develop or seams and welded joints split, allowing exhaust gases to escape.

Can an exhaust leak fail an MOT?

Yes. A major exhaust leak is a Major MOT defect, and an exhaust leak that could affect emissions levels can also be classified as a Major defect under the emissions inspection.

Can an insecure exhaust fail an MOT?

Yes. An exhaust system that is insecure is a Major MOT defect. A noise suppression component that is likely to become detached is classified as Dangerous.

Can exhaust fumes entering the cabin fail an MOT?

Yes. Exhaust fumes entering the cabin are a Major MOT defect, and fumes causing a danger to the health of people on board are classified as Dangerous.

Can a noisy exhaust fail an MOT?

Yes. Exhaust noise that is unreasonably above the level expected from a similar vehicle with a standard silencer in average condition can result in a Major MOT defect.

Can I drive with an exhaust corrosion advisory?

Continued driving may be reasonable if the exhaust remains secure, there is no major leak, no dangerous fume ingress and the vehicle remains roadworthy, but corrosion should be investigated because deterioration can progress.

What are the signs that exhaust corrosion is getting worse?

Warning signs include increasing exhaust noise, blowing or hissing sounds, visible holes, heavy flaking rust, rattling, movement at joints or mountings, an exhaust hanging lower than normal, fumes and repeated MOT advisories.

What causes exhaust systems to corrode?

Exhaust corrosion is encouraged by moisture, condensation, road salt, repeated heat cycles, short journeys, age, damaged protective coatings, vibration and exposure underneath the vehicle.

Can exhaust corrosion affect emissions?

Yes. Corrosion that creates a leak can disturb exhaust gas flow or allow air into the system, and an exhaust leak capable of affecting measured emissions can result in an MOT failure.

Can an exhaust be repaired instead of replaced?

Sometimes. Sound pipework may allow a clamp, joint, hanger or local repair, but badly thinned, perforated or extensively corroded sections usually need replacement rather than repeated patching.

How much does exhaust corrosion repair cost in the UK?

Cost depends on the affected section, vehicle design, labour, parts quality and whether the repair involves a simple clamp or hanger, pipe section, silencer, catalytic converter, diesel particulate filter or a larger part of the exhaust system.

Will an exhaust corrosion advisory fail the next MOT?

Not automatically. The exhaust will be assessed in its condition on the next test day, but corrosion can progress into a major leak, insecurity, excessive noise, fume ingress or an emissions-related defect.

Should I buy a used car with an exhaust corrosion advisory?

It may still be a reasonable purchase if the corrosion is properly assessed and the repair cost is reflected in the price. Repeated advisories, substantial leakage, insecure sections, excessive noise or corrosion around expensive emissions components justify greater caution.

Does a valid MOT mean the exhaust is good for another year?

No. An MOT records the vehicle's condition against the test requirements at the time of inspection. Exhaust corrosion can continue afterwards, so the vehicle still has to remain roadworthy throughout the year.
