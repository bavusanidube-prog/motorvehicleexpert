Quick answer

What Causes a Car Exhaust Blowing Noise?

A blowing exhaust usually means exhaust gases are escaping before they reach the tailpipe. Common causes include a rust hole, split flexi pipe, loose clamp, leaking flange, failed gasket, cracked downpipe, damaged silencer or manifold leak.

The location and behaviour of the noise provide important diagnostic clues. A sharp tick near the engine may indicate a manifold or gasket leak, a rasp under acceleration often points towards a flexi pipe or front section, while a deep roar from the rear commonly involves the silencer or rear pipework.

What is most common?

Corroded pipework, damaged flexi sections, loose joints, failed gaskets and worn silencers are common sources of blowing exhaust noise.

What is the best clue?

Identify whether the noise comes from the front, centre or rear and whether it becomes louder when cold or under acceleration.

What is urgent?

Cabin fumes, hanging pipework, severe front-end leaks or an exhaust that may detach require prompt inspection.

Mechanic insight:

Do not replace an exhaust section just because the car is louder. A small flange gasket, clamp or flexi leak can sound dramatic. Confirm the exact leak location on a ramp before deciding whether the repair needs a seal, welding, a flexi section or complete exhaust replacement.

Exhaust Operation

How the Exhaust System Works

The exhaust system carries hot combustion gases away from the engine, reduces noise, supports emissions control and releases gases safely behind the vehicle. It is made from several connected sections rather than one continuous pipe.

Depending on the vehicle, the system may include an exhaust manifold, turbocharger outlet, downpipe, flexi section, catalytic converter, diesel particulate filter, oxygen sensors, centre pipe, silencers, hangers and tailpipe. Every joint, gasket, weld and flexible section is a possible leak point as the system ages.

1. Exhaust Gases Leave the Engine

Combustion gases exit the cylinders through the exhaust valves and enter the exhaust manifold.

2. Gases Pass Through Front Exhaust

The manifold, turbo outlet where fitted, downpipe and flexi section carry hot gases away from the engine.

3. Emissions Are Treated

Catalytic converters, DPF systems and oxygen sensors help control and monitor exhaust emissions.

4. Noise Is Reduced

Centre and rear silencers reduce exhaust pressure pulses before gases leave through the tailpipe.

Why exhaust leaks get louder under load

Acceleration increases exhaust gas flow and pressure. A small leak that is barely audible at idle can become much louder when the engine is pulling hard.

Mechanic Diagnostic Method

How Mechanics Diagnose an Exhaust Blowing Noise

A good exhaust diagnosis starts by locating the noise and identifying when it becomes most obvious. Front leaks can sound completely different from rear silencer faults, and a small gasket leak can imitate injector or valvetrain ticking.

The technician should compare cold start, warm idle, acceleration, engine movement and visible exhaust condition before deciding which section needs repair.

1. Locate the Noise

Decide whether the blowing comes from the front, centre or rear of the vehicle.

2. Compare Cold and Warm

Manifold and gasket leaks can be easier to hear before metal components expand with heat.

3. Add Engine Load

Listen for increased blowing, rasping or hissing during acceleration.

4. Check for Fumes

Any exhaust smell inside the cabin increases the safety priority.

5. Inspect on a Ramp

Check joints, clamps, flexi sections, silencers, welds, flanges and hangers from underneath.

6. Look for Soot Marks

Dark soot around a joint or crack can provide strong evidence of gas escape.

7. Check Exhaust Movement

Broken hangers or excessive engine movement can strain joints and flexi sections.

8. Confirm Before Replacing Parts

The failed component should explain the sound, leak location and physical evidence found during inspection.

A ramp inspection is usually more useful than guessing from outside

Exhaust noise travels along pipework and underneath the body. The loudest point heard beside the car is not always the actual leak location.

Common Causes

Common Causes of an Exhaust Blowing Noise

Exhaust systems fail through corrosion, heat cycling, vibration, damaged joints, failed flexible sections and broken mountings. The repair can range from a small gasket or clamp to replacement of a complete exhaust section.

Rust Hole

Corrosion can perforate pipes and silencers, creating blowing, chuffing or roaring.

Split Flexi Pipe

The flexible exhaust section can crack internally or split around the woven outer layer.

Loose Clamp

A clamp that no longer seals the pipe joint can allow exhaust gases to escape.

Failed Gasket

Exhaust gaskets can burn, flatten or lose sealing pressure between mating surfaces.

Corroded Flange

Rust can weaken flange faces and bolts until the joint can no longer seal correctly.

Cracked Downpipe

Front exhaust cracks can create loud blowing and may affect sensor readings or cabin-fume risk.

Damaged Silencer

Internal or external silencer failure can make the exhaust significantly louder.

Broken Hanger

Unsupported pipework can move excessively, strain joints and eventually create leaks.

Previous Poor Repair

Weak welding, exhaust paste or badly aligned replacement parts can fail again.

Front Exhaust Fault

Exhaust Manifold and Manifold Gasket Leaks

The exhaust manifold collects gases directly from the engine cylinders, so leaks here are exposed to very high temperature and pressure pulses.

Manifold leaks often sound sharper than leaks farther down the exhaust. Drivers commonly describe them as ticking, puffing or rapid blowing near the engine.

Cold-Start Ticking

A failed gasket or small manifold crack can be easiest to hear immediately after starting.

Soot Around Flange

Black carbon marks around the manifold-to-head or manifold-to-downpipe joint can reveal gas escape.

Broken Studs or Bolts

Heat cycling can contribute to fastener failure and reduce gasket clamping pressure.

Cracked Manifold

Cast or fabricated manifolds can crack after repeated heat cycles or mechanical stress.

Sensor Effects

Upstream leaks can disturb oxygen-sensor readings on some systems.

Cabin-Fume Risk

Leaks near the engine bay deserve faster attention because gases are closer to ventilation and body openings.

Common Front Leak

Flexi Pipe Blowing and Failure Symptoms

The exhaust flexi section allows controlled movement between the engine and the exhaust system. Without this flexibility, normal engine movement would place more stress on rigid pipes, joints and welds.

As the flexi ages, its internal bellows or outer braid can deteriorate, producing rasping, blowing or vibration-related exhaust noise.

Rasp Under Acceleration

Increased gas flow and engine movement often make flexi leaks much louder under load.

Frayed Outer Braid

Damage to the woven outer layer can provide a visible clue, although internal failure may occur first.

Engine Movement Changes Noise

The sound can vary as the engine rocks under acceleration and deceleration.

Front-Floor Noise

Drivers may hear the leak underneath the front seats or footwell area.

Possible Fume Smell

Because flexi sections are usually near the front of the vehicle, fumes should be taken seriously.

Repair Options Vary

Some flexi sections can be replaced or welded separately; others are supplied as part of a larger exhaust assembly.

Front Pipework

Downpipe and Front Exhaust Leaks

The downpipe carries hot exhaust gases from the manifold or turbocharger towards the emissions-control components. Because this section experiences high temperature and vibration, leaks can become particularly noticeable under engine load.

Cracked Pipe

Cracks near welds, bends or stressed areas can create loud front-end blowing.

Flange Leak

Corroded or distorted flanges may no longer compress the gasket correctly.

Failed Gasket

Gaskets can burn through or lose sealing force after heat cycling.

Turbo Outlet Leak

Turbocharged engines can leak at outlet joints, clamps or adjacent pipework.

Sensor-Related Effects

A leak near oxygen sensors can sometimes disturb measured exhaust conditions.

Heat Exposure

Escaping gases close to wiring, shields or underbody parts should be investigated promptly.

Emissions Components

Catalytic Converter and DPF Joint Leaks

Catalytic converters and diesel particulate filters are joined into the exhaust using flanges, clamps, sleeves or integrated pipe sections. The emissions component itself may be sound while the surrounding joint or pipework leaks.

Corroded Flange

Rust can weaken the sealing faces around expensive emissions components.

Clamp or Sleeve Leak

A loose or corroded joining sleeve can produce blowing without failure of the catalyst or DPF itself.

Sensor Port Leak

Threaded sensor fittings or surrounding pipework can leak where corrosion or previous work has caused damage.

Do not automatically condemn the catalytic converter or DPF

A leak close to an emissions component may come from a flange, gasket or adjoining pipe rather than the expensive emissions unit itself.

Rear Exhaust Faults

Silencer, Back Box and Tailpipe Corrosion

Rear silencers live in a harsh environment of moisture, condensation, road salt and temperature changes. Corrosion can attack seams, pipe entries and the bottom of the box until gas escapes.

Deep Roaring

A large silencer hole often makes the exhaust substantially louder than normal.

Corroded Seams

Silencer seams and welded pipe entries are common corrosion points.

Tailpipe Corrosion

Rear tailpipes can rust through or detach from the silencer body.

Internal Silencer Failure

Broken internal baffles can create rattling even when the outer casing has no obvious hole.

Broken Rear Hanger

Failed rubber mounts can allow the system to hang low or strike the body.

Several Corroded Areas

Replacing the complete rear section can be more sensible than repeatedly patching weak metal.

Temperature Clues

Exhaust Blowing When Cold vs When Hot

Exhaust systems expand significantly as they heat up. Temperature changes can therefore alter the size of a leak and the amount of noise it produces.

Louder When Cold

Small manifold cracks and gasket leaks can be louder before the metal expands and temporarily closes part of the gap.

  • ✓ Sharp ticking after cold start
  • ✓ Noise reduces as engine warms
  • ✓ Manifold and gasket area deserves inspection

Louder When Hot

Some cracks, loose joints or weakened pipework can open further as the exhaust expands and moves.

  • ✓ Noise develops after driving
  • ✓ Heat changes alignment at joints
  • ✓ Flexi sections and corroded pipes remain possible
Noise Comparison

What Different Exhaust Noises Can Mean

Ticking

Sharp Tick Near Engine

Commonly associated with manifold, manifold gasket or front exhaust leaks.

Hissing

Fine Gas Leak

A small gasket, joint or pipe leak can produce a sharper hissing noise.

Puffing

Repeating Exhaust Pulse

Exhaust pressure pulses escaping through a joint or small hole can produce rhythmic puffing.

Rasping

Rough Noise Under Load

Flexi pipes and front exhaust cracks commonly produce a rasping sound when accelerating.

Roaring

Large Leak or Silencer Fault

A large hole, failed rear box or missing exhaust section can create a deep roar.

Rattling

Mount or Heat Shield

Metallic rattling may involve a hanger, clamp, heat shield or broken internal silencer baffle rather than gas leakage.

Knocking

Exhaust Contact

Broken mounts can allow the exhaust to strike the body, subframe or suspension.

Whistling

Small High-Velocity Leak

Certain narrow cracks or gasket leaks can create a higher-pitched whistle under load.

Cabin Safety

Exhaust Smell or Fumes Inside the Car

Exhaust fumes inside the cabin should be treated more seriously than noise alone. A leak near the engine bay, front exhaust, floor area or other body openings can allow gases to enter the passenger compartment.

Common Warning Patterns

  • ! Exhaust smell becomes stronger while stationary.
  • ! Fumes increase in slow traffic.
  • ! Smell enters when the heater or fan is operating.
  • ! Noise comes from underneath the front floor.
  • ! The front exhaust is visibly damaged or loose.

What Requires Immediate Attention

  • ! Strong or persistent fumes inside the cabin.
  • ! Passengers develop headache, nausea or irritation.
  • ! Exhaust gases appear to escape near the cabin floor.
  • ! Pipework is hanging or dragging underneath.
  • ! The exhaust may detach while driving.
Do not continue normal driving with persistent exhaust fumes inside the cabin

The exhaust system should be inspected and the route of fume entry identified before the vehicle returns to normal use.

Professional Diagnosis

Professional Diagnostic Workflow for an Exhaust Blowing Noise

A professional exhaust diagnosis should identify the exact point where gases are escaping before any pipe, silencer, flexi section or emissions component is replaced. Exhaust noise can travel through the pipework and underbody, which means the loudest place heard from outside the car is not always the actual leak.

The technician should combine cold-start behaviour, engine load, exhaust location, visible corrosion, soot staining, system movement and a ramp inspection. Where the leak is too small to locate confidently, controlled leak-detection methods can provide further confirmation.

1. Confirm the Complaint

Establish whether the driver is hearing ticking, blowing, hissing, rasping, roaring, rattling or knocking.

2. Locate the Area

Decide whether the noise is strongest around the engine, front floor, centre section or rear exhaust.

3. Compare Cold and Hot

Determine whether temperature changes the sound as manifolds, pipes, joints and gaskets expand.

4. Add Engine Load

Check whether acceleration or increased engine speed makes the leak substantially louder.

5. Check Safety First

Look for hanging pipework, failed mounts, excessive heat and any evidence of exhaust fumes entering the cabin.

6. Inspect on a Ramp

Examine flanges, clamps, flexi sections, welds, silencers, hangers and corrosion along the complete system.

7. Confirm the Leak

Use soot marks, audible gas escape or an appropriate controlled leak test to prove the failed area.

8. Choose the Correct Repair

Decide whether the system needs a gasket, clamp, weld, flexi section, silencer or complete replacement section.

Diagnose the leak before pricing the exhaust

A £50–£100 sealing repair and a complete exhaust-section replacement can produce very similar noise. The repair decision should follow the physical leak location and the condition of the surrounding metal.

Road-Test Procedure

How a Mechanic Road-Tests a Blowing Exhaust

Not every exhaust leak needs a long road test, but comparing the symptom under several controlled conditions can help separate an exhaust-pressure leak from a rattle, engine tick, wheel-end noise or drivetrain sound.

Test 1

Cold Start

Listen for sharp ticking or puffing around the manifold and front exhaust before thermal expansion changes the gap.

Test 2

Warm Idle

Compare whether the noise becomes quieter, remains unchanged or becomes stronger after the system heats up.

Test 3

Gentle Acceleration

Increased gas flow often makes flexi, flange and pipe leaks easier to identify.

Test 4

Higher Engine Load

A leak may become more obvious when climbing or accelerating because exhaust mass flow rises.

Test 5

Lift-Off Comparison

An exhaust blowing sound often reduces as engine load and gas flow fall, unlike many road-speed wheel noises.

Test 6

Cabin-Fume Check

Any exhaust smell inside the passenger compartment should end normal diagnostic driving and move the vehicle towards physical inspection.

Ramp Inspection

What a Garage Checks Underneath a Blowing Exhaust

A ramp inspection allows the complete exhaust system to be assessed for leakage, corrosion, security and previous repairs. It also shows whether replacing one small area would leave badly weakened metal immediately beside the repair.

Exhaust Manifold Area

Inspect visible manifold joints, gaskets, studs and nearby heat shielding where accessible.

Flexi Section

Check braid condition, internal failure, cracks and signs of gas escape.

Flanges & Gaskets

Look for corrosion, failed fasteners, soot and poor sealing between exhaust sections.

Welds & Pipe Seams

Cracks commonly develop around stressed or previously repaired areas.

Catalyst / DPF Connections

Confirm whether the expensive emissions unit is damaged or only the adjoining joint or pipework is leaking.

Centre & Rear Silencers

Inspect seams, pipe entries, corrosion and internal rattling.

Exhaust Hangers

Check rubber mounts and brackets for failure, stretching or excessive system movement.

Heat Shields

Loose shields can imitate exhaust failure without allowing gases to escape.

Tailpipe

Check corrosion, security and whether the pipe exits in its intended position.

Exhaust security matters as much as the leak

A relatively small gas leak can coexist with badly corroded mountings or pipework. An exhaust that may detach needs attention even if the noise itself is not severe.

Leak Confirmation

How Exhaust Leaks Are Confirmed

Large exhaust leaks are often obvious from sound, soot or visible corrosion. Smaller leaks can require more deliberate confirmation, especially around manifolds, flanges, flexi sections or complicated emissions assemblies.

Audible Gas Escape

A technician may locate a distinctive puff, hiss or blow while the engine is running under safe workshop conditions.

Soot Evidence

Black carbon staining around a joint, crack or flange is a strong sign that exhaust gas has been escaping.

Controlled Smoke Testing

Where appropriate, workshop leak-testing equipment can introduce controlled low-pressure smoke to help reveal small leak paths.

Visual Corrosion Check

Holes, split seams and heavily rusted joints may make further testing unnecessary.

Joint Movement

Movement around flanges, clamps or broken hangers can show why a seal repeatedly opens.

Sensor Data Where Relevant

Diagnostic data can support investigation when a front leak may be affecting oxygen-sensor behaviour or emissions control.

Leak testing should be controlled workshop work

Exhaust systems become very hot and contain harmful gases. Physical leak confirmation should use appropriate workshop procedures rather than improvised attempts around a hot, running engine.

Physical Evidence

Soot Marks, Heat Damage and Other Exhaust Leak Evidence

A professional diagnosis should look for evidence that supports the sound. Soot, heat staining, corrosion and surrounding damage can often reveal both where the leak is and how long it may have been present.

Black Soot Marks

Carbon staining around a flange, crack or pipe seam is one of the most useful visual clues.

Burnt or Discoloured Area

Escaping hot gases can discolour nearby metal or damage heat-sensitive material.

Rust Perforation

A small visible hole may sit within a much larger area of weakened metal.

Failed Exhaust Paste

Old sealing compound around a joint can indicate a previous leak or temporary repair.

Broken Hanger Marks

Contact marks on the body or subframe can reveal excessive exhaust movement.

Heat Shield Damage

Loose or distorted shielding can create noise and may also indicate previous exhaust movement or impact.

Engine-Bay Comparison

Exhaust Manifold Leak vs Injector Ticking

Some engines naturally produce noticeable injector ticking. A small exhaust-manifold leak can sound similar, particularly from above the engine bay.

More Typical of Exhaust Leakage

  • ✓ Noise may be stronger when cold.
  • ✓ Puffing or blowing accompanies the tick.
  • ✓ Soot may be visible around a gasket or flange.
  • ✓ Cabin-fume smell may be present.

More Typical of Injector Operation

  • ✓ Consistent mechanical ticking at injector locations.
  • ✓ No soot or exhaust-gas evidence nearby.
  • ✓ No exhaust smell associated with the sound.
  • ✓ Sound may be normal for the injection system.
Sound alone is not enough

A leak should have supporting evidence such as gas escape, soot, damaged sealing surfaces or a failed exhaust component.

Repair Decision

How a Mechanic Decides Whether to Seal, Weld or Replace the Exhaust

Finding the leak is only the first stage. The surrounding metal must then be assessed to determine whether a local repair is likely to last.

Seal / Gasket

Healthy Pipework, Failed Joint

Suitable where the exhaust sections are structurally sound and only the gasket, clamp or seal has failed.

Usually Lower Cost
Weld / Flexi Repair

Localised Damage

May be appropriate where the damaged area can be removed or repaired and the surrounding metal remains strong.

Condition Dependent
Replace Section

Widespread Corrosion

More reliable where several seams, joints or nearby areas are badly weakened.

Higher Cost, Better Durability
Avoid repairing strong metal onto weak metal

A neat patch beside heavily corroded pipework can simply move the next failure a few centimetres along the exhaust. Condition of the complete section matters.

Driving Safety

Is It Safe to Drive With a Blowing Exhaust?

Whether it is safe to continue driving depends on the location of the leak, the condition of the exhaust system and whether fumes, heat or loose pipework create an additional risk.

A small rear leak in otherwise secure pipework may allow a short careful journey to a garage, but a front leak, cabin fumes, hanging exhaust or pipework that may detach should be treated much more seriously.

Lower Concern

Small Rear Leak

A minor rear exhaust leak with no cabin fumes and secure pipework may allow cautious driving while repair is arranged.

Needs Repair

  • ✓ The exhaust is securely mounted.
  • ✓ No exhaust fumes enter the cabin.
  • ✓ No pipework is hanging or dragging.
  • ✓ No severe emissions or engine-running symptoms are present.

Stop Normal Driving When...

  • ! Exhaust fumes enter the cabin.
  • ! The exhaust is hanging loose.
  • ! Pipework is dragging on the road.
  • ! The system looks likely to detach.
  • ! Hot gases are escaping near vulnerable components.
  • ! The leak is worsening rapidly.
Cabin fumes change the safety decision

A car that merely sounds louder is different from one allowing exhaust gases into the passenger compartment. If fumes are entering the cabin, stop treating the issue as a noise complaint and arrange prompt physical inspection.

Prevention

How to Reduce Exhaust Leak and Corrosion Problems

Exhaust systems eventually deteriorate, but early attention to corrosion, failed hangers and small leaks can prevent minor faults becoming larger repairs.

Repair Small Leaks Early

A failed gasket or clamp can damage adjoining surfaces if ignored for too long.

Replace Broken Hangers

Supporting the system correctly reduces strain on flexi sections, welds and joints.

Investigate New Rattles

A loose shield or mounting issue is usually cheaper to repair before it causes further damage.

Check Corrosion During Servicing

Exhaust condition can be assessed while the vehicle is already raised for routine maintenance.

Do Not Ignore Cabin Fumes

Fume entry deserves immediate investigation even if the exhaust noise itself is mild.

Review MOT Advisories

Repeated exhaust corrosion or emissions advisories can provide early warning of future repair costs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Car Exhaust Blowing Noise FAQs

These questions cover the most common exhaust-leak, flexi-pipe, manifold, cabin-fume, driving-safety, MOT and UK repair-cost concerns.

Why is my exhaust making a blowing noise?

An exhaust blowing noise usually means exhaust gases are escaping through a hole, failed joint, damaged flexi pipe, loose clamp, cracked pipe, damaged silencer, corroded flange or manifold gasket leak.

Why is my exhaust louder when accelerating?

Exhaust gas flow and engine movement increase during acceleration, so leaks in flexi pipes, joints, manifolds, gaskets, downpipes and silencers often become easier to hear under load.

Can a flexi pipe cause an exhaust blowing noise?

Yes. A damaged flexi pipe is a common source of front exhaust blowing, rasping or hissing and often becomes louder during acceleration because exhaust flow and engine movement increase.

What does an exhaust manifold leak sound like?

An exhaust manifold or manifold gasket leak can produce sharp ticking, puffing or blowing near the engine, particularly when the engine is cold or under acceleration.

Why does my exhaust blow more when the engine is cold?

Some exhaust leaks are more noticeable when cold because metal joints, manifolds and gaskets have not expanded with heat. As the exhaust warms, thermal expansion may temporarily reduce the size of a small leak.

Can an exhaust leak cause fumes inside the car?

Yes. A leak near the engine bay, front exhaust, floor area or other body openings can allow exhaust fumes to enter the cabin, particularly when stationary, in traffic or when the heater or ventilation system is operating.

Is a blowing exhaust dangerous?

It can be dangerous if exhaust fumes enter the cabin, the exhaust becomes insecure, pipework hangs low, the leak is near the engine bay or hot gases are escaping close to other vehicle components.

Can I drive with a blowing exhaust?

A very small leak may allow a short careful journey for inspection if there are no cabin fumes and the exhaust is secure, but avoid driving if fumes enter the car, the exhaust hangs loose, the noise is severe or pipework could detach.

When should I stop driving with an exhaust leak?

Stop driving if exhaust fumes enter the cabin, the exhaust is hanging or insecure, pipework is dragging on the road, hot gases are escaping near vulnerable components or the system appears likely to detach.

Will a blowing exhaust fail an MOT?

A blowing exhaust can affect the MOT if the system leaks, is insecure, excessively noisy, likely to fall off, missing required emissions equipment or causes emissions to exceed the permitted limits.

Can an exhaust leak cause an emissions test failure?

Yes. Exhaust leaks can interfere with gas flow or sensor readings and may contribute to an emissions failure depending on the location and severity of the leak.

Can an exhaust leak cause an engine management light?

Yes. A leak near oxygen sensors or upstream exhaust components can affect sensor readings and may contribute to engine management faults or diagnostic trouble codes.

What is the difference between an exhaust leak and a loose heat shield?

An exhaust leak usually produces blowing, hissing, puffing, rasping or roaring as gases escape, while a loose heat shield more commonly causes a metallic rattle or buzzing that changes with engine speed or vibration.

How does a mechanic find an exhaust leak?

A mechanic listens for the leak, inspects the exhaust on a ramp, checks joints, flexi sections, manifolds, silencers and hangers, looks for soot marks and corrosion and may use controlled smoke or other leak-detection methods where appropriate.

How much does it cost to fix a blowing exhaust in the UK?

A small clamp or gasket repair may cost around £40 to £150, a flexi pipe repair may cost around £100 to £300 or more, and larger exhaust sections can cost £200 to £900 or more depending on the vehicle and parts required.

Is it better to weld an exhaust or replace the section?

Welding can be suitable where the surrounding metal is still strong, but replacing the section is usually more reliable when corrosion is widespread, joints are badly deteriorated or several nearby areas are close to failure.

Should I buy a used car with a blowing exhaust?

Only after the cause and repair cost have been confirmed. A loose clamp may be inexpensive, while a manifold leak, failed flexi pipe, catalytic converter problem or heavily corroded exhaust system can cost substantially more.

What is the best first check for an exhaust blowing noise?

First identify whether the noise comes from the front, middle or rear of the car and whether it changes when cold, warm or accelerating. Then check for visible damage, loose sections, broken hangers, soot marks and cabin fumes before arranging a ramp inspection.
