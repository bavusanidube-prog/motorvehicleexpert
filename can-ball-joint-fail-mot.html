Quick Answer

Can a Ball Joint Fail an MOT?

Yes. A suspension ball joint can fail an MOT when it is excessively worn. Excessive wear is a Major defect. If the joint has deteriorated far enough that it is likely to become detached, it is a Dangerous defect.

The ball-joint dust cover is assessed separately from actual joint wear. A severely deteriorated suspension-joint dust cover is a Minor defect. If the cover is missing or no longer prevents dirt entering the joint, it is a Major defect.

The important distinction

Do not assume every split ball-joint boot means the joint itself has failed, and do not assume every visible movement means excessive wear. Why this matters before replacing parts

A knock from the front suspension does not prove that the ball joint is faulty. Track rod ends, anti-roll bar links, suspension bushes, strut mounts and other components can create similar symptoms. The source of the movement should be confirmed before parts are authorised.

UK MOT Rules

When Does a Ball Joint Fail an MOT?

A suspension ball joint can fail an MOT when the joint is excessively worn. The important point is that the tester is assessing the condition and security of the joint — not simply whether the suspension moves when it is inspected.

A ball joint is designed to articulate as the suspension and steering move. What matters is whether there is excessive unwanted clearance caused by wear, whether the joint remains secure and whether its condition creates a risk of separation.

Why “play” needs context

Seeing a wheel or suspension component move does not, by itself, prove the ball joint has failed. The movement has to be traced to the relevant joint and judged using the correct inspection method for that suspension design. We cover that process in detail in the MOT inspection section.

Defect Severity

Ball Joint MOT Result: Minor, Major or Dangerous?

The MOT result depends on what is actually defective and how serious it is. Joint wear and dust-cover condition should not be treated as though they are the same defect.

Condition found Typical MOT classification What it means What to do
Suspension joint excessively worn Major The joint has excessive wear and the vehicle fails its MOT. Confirm the affected joint and repair it before retest.
Suspension joint worn and likely to become detached Dangerous Wear has progressed to a serious safety condition. Do not treat this as a routine advisory. Arrange repair immediately.
Suspension-joint dust cover severely deteriorated Minor The protective cover has deteriorated, but its condition has not reached the Major threshold described below. Repair promptly before contamination accelerates joint wear.
Dust cover missing or no longer prevents dirt entering Major The joint is no longer being adequately protected from contamination. Repair the protection problem and inspect the joint itself for wear.
Major and Dangerous are not interchangeable

Both result in an MOT failure, but a Dangerous classification represents the more serious condition. If the joint is considered likely to become detached, the issue has moved beyond ordinary wear into an immediate safety concern.

Dust Cover Rules

Can a Split Ball Joint Dust Cover Fail an MOT?

Yes, it can — but the condition of the cover matters. A damaged ball-joint boot should not automatically be described as excessive ball-joint wear.

The flexible dust cover helps keep water, road dirt and abrasive contamination away from the lubricated ball-and-socket surfaces. Once protection is lost, contamination can accelerate wear inside the joint even if excessive play is not yet obvious.

Severe deterioration of a suspension-joint dust cover is treated differently from a cover that has stopped protecting the joint.

Useful distinction for drivers

Boot damage is a protection problem. Ball-joint play is a wear problem. They can occur together, and a damaged boot can contribute to future wear, but they should still be diagnosed separately.

Know the Component

Suspension Ball Joint vs Steering Ball Joint

The term “ball joint” can cause confusion because ball-and-socket joints are used in more than one part of the vehicle. This guide is primarily about suspension ball joints.

Suspension

Suspension Ball Joint

Commonly connects a suspension arm to the steering knuckle or hub carrier. It allows controlled angular movement while helping locate the wheel assembly.

  • Part of the suspension assembly
  • Moves as suspension travels
  • Can influence wheel control and geometry
  • Excessive wear can cause an MOT failure
Steering

Steering Ball Joint / Track Rod End

A track rod end also uses a ball-and-socket joint, but it forms part of the steering linkage and transmits steering movement towards the road wheel.

  • Part of the steering linkage
  • Transfers steering input
  • Can also develop unwanted play
  • Assessed under steering requirements
Why the distinction matters

If a garage says “the ball joint has play”, ask which joint they mean. A lower suspension ball joint and a track rod end are different components, perform different jobs and can require different repairs.

How It Works

What Does a Suspension Ball Joint Actually Do?

A suspension ball joint provides a strong movable connection between suspension components while allowing the wheel assembly to change angle as the suspension and, on many designs, the steering move.

Inside the joint, a ball-ended pin moves within a closely controlled socket. Lubrication reduces friction, while the dust cover helps keep water and abrasive contamination away from the working surfaces.

Locate

Controls Position

The joint forms part of the suspension geometry that keeps the wheel assembly located relative to the vehicle.

Articulate

Allows Movement

It must move through changing angles as the suspension rises and falls and, depending on the design, as the wheel steers.

Carry Load

Works Under Road Forces

The joint operates while the suspension is exposed to braking, cornering, road-impact and vehicle-load forces.

Movement is part of its job

This is why a useful diagnosis separates normal articulation from unwanted internal clearance. A ball joint that moves through its designed range is not the same as a worn joint with excessive play between its internal surfaces.

Wear Progression

How Does a Ball Joint Become Worn?

Ball-joint wear is normally progressive. Age and mileage matter, but road conditions, contamination, suspension loads, component quality and the condition of the protective boot can all affect how quickly deterioration develops.

Stage 1

Protection Deteriorates

The dust cover can age, crack, split or become damaged. If its sealing ability is lost, water and dirt can reach the joint.

Stage 2

Internal Surfaces Wear

Lubrication loss and contamination can increase friction and abrasive wear between the ball pin, bearing surfaces and socket.

Stage 3

Clearance Develops

As material wears, unwanted clearance can develop. The joint may then knock, allow unwanted wheel movement or affect suspension control.

Do not wait for dramatic symptoms

A joint does not have to be making a loud knock before wear can be present. Conversely, a loud front-end knock does not prove the ball joint is responsible. Inspection should establish the source rather than relying on noise alone.

Symptoms

What Are the Symptoms of a Worn Ball Joint?

A worn ball joint can produce noise, steering changes, unwanted suspension movement or abnormal tyre wear — but none of those symptoms proves the ball joint is faulty on its own.

The most useful clue is the pattern: when the symptom occurs, which side it comes from, whether steering or braking changes it, and whether physical play can be confirmed at the joint.

Noise Diagnosis

Can a Worn Ball Joint Cause Knocking or Clunking?

Yes. If clearance develops inside a ball joint, changing suspension loads can allow the worn surfaces to move against each other and produce a knock or clunk.

The noise may be more noticeable over broken road surfaces, potholes, speed humps or when suspension load changes. However, front-suspension knocking has many possible causes, so the sound alone is not enough to condemn the joint.

Ball Joint Becomes More Plausible When

  • Physical play can be traced to the joint
  • The dust cover is damaged or contamination is evident
  • The knock changes as suspension load changes
  • Related steering or tyre-wear symptoms are also present

Do Not Forget Other Causes

  • Anti-roll bar link wear
  • Suspension arm bush wear
  • Strut top mount wear
  • Damper or spring-related movement
  • Steering-joint play
Diagnose the movement, not just the sound

A useful workshop inspection reproduces or loads the suspension while watching and feeling the suspected components. That is far more reliable than replacing the first component associated with a generic “front-end knock”.

Road Behaviour

Can a Worn Ball Joint Affect Steering?

It can. A sufficiently worn suspension ball joint can allow unwanted wheel movement, which may contribute to vague steering, wandering or a less settled feel on the road.

But steering symptoms should be diagnosed as a system. Tyres, wheel alignment, track rod ends, steering rack components, suspension bushes and wheel bearings can produce overlapping symptoms.

Vague Steering

Vehicle Feels Less Precise

Unwanted movement in suspension or steering components can make the vehicle feel less accurately connected to steering input.

Wandering

Needs Frequent Correction

A vehicle that wanders should be checked for tyre, alignment, steering and suspension faults rather than blaming one joint without inspection.

Pulling

Do Not Assume Ball Joint

Pulling to one side is commonly influenced by alignment, tyres, brakes and suspension geometry. Ball-joint wear is one possible contributor, not an automatic diagnosis.

Sudden deterioration needs attention

If steering behaviour changes suddenly, the vehicle develops obvious wheel movement, or a heavy clunk is accompanied by poor control, do not keep driving merely to see whether the symptom gets worse. Have the suspension and steering inspected.

Tyre Evidence

Can a Worn Ball Joint Cause Uneven Tyre Wear?

Yes, it can contribute. Excessive movement at a suspension ball joint can allow wheel geometry to vary instead of remaining properly controlled.

However, tyre wear is evidence that something needs investigating — it is not a ball-joint diagnosis by itself. Incorrect wheel alignment, tyre pressure, worn bushes, damaged suspension parts and other steering or suspension faults can create similar wear.

Mechanical Play

Repair Wear First

If a joint or bush has excessive play, correct that mechanical fault before expecting a wheel-alignment adjustment to remain meaningful.

Tyre Pattern

Use Wear as a Clue

Compare inner and outer shoulders, both tyres on the axle and any difference from side to side. The pattern can help guide the inspection.

After Repair

Recheck Geometry

Once worn components have been corrected, wheel alignment may be appropriate — particularly where tyre wear, pulling or steering centre problems were already present.

Useful diagnostic order

Tyres → mechanical play → damaged components → repair → alignment. Aligning a vehicle while a suspension joint is excessively worn can hide the real problem and may not produce a stable result.

Avoid Misdiagnosis

Ball Joint vs Track Rod End: Which One Has Play?

A suspension ball joint and a track rod end can both develop unwanted play, but they are different components. Before ordering parts, the movement needs to be traced to the joint that is actually worn.

A suspension ball joint helps locate the wheel assembly while allowing suspension articulation. A track rod end forms part of the steering linkage and transfers steering movement towards the wheel. Because both use ball-and-socket joints, the symptoms can overlap.

Tester Checks

How Does an MOT Tester Check a Ball Joint?

The tester checks suspension joints for excessive wear, play and security using an inspection method appropriate to the suspension design and the equipment available.

That distinction matters. A useful inspection does not simply shake the wheel and fail whatever appears to move. The suspension has to be loaded or unloaded appropriately, the movement reproduced and the actual source of any excessive play identified.

Step 1

Load the Suspension Correctly

The correct inspection method depends on the suspension layout. Some joints need the suspension positioned or unloaded in a particular way before wear can be assessed properly.

Step 2

Apply Controlled Movement

Wheel play detectors may be used where available. Other inspection methods can involve rocking the wheel or carefully levering the suspension while the relevant joints are observed.

Step 3

Identify the Source

The tester needs to distinguish ball-joint wear from movement in wheel bearings, steering joints, bushes and other suspension components.

The inspection method matters

Suspension designs differ. A method that exposes play clearly on one vehicle may load the joint differently on another. This is why the inspection should follow the appropriate procedure rather than treating every suspension layout identically.

Do not copy an MOT inspection unsafely at home

Working around a raised vehicle or applying force to suspension components can be dangerous without suitable lifting equipment, support points and experience. If the source of the movement is unclear, have the vehicle inspected professionally.

Mechanic Diagnosis

How to Confirm Whether the Ball Joint Is Actually Worn

Good diagnosis answers one question before parts are ordered: where is the unwanted movement coming from?

A wheel can move because of a ball joint, wheel bearing, track rod end, inner steering joint, suspension bush or another component. Several worn parts can also exist at the same time. The diagnosis therefore needs to isolate each possible source.

1. Verify

Confirm the Complaint

Establish whether the concern is an MOT result, knocking, steering looseness, uneven tyre wear or movement discovered during inspection.

2. Inspect

Look Before Applying Force

Check the joint, dust cover, mounting points and nearby components for obvious deterioration, damage, contamination or displacement.

3. Reproduce

Create the Movement

Load the suspension appropriately and reproduce the suspected play while the individual joints and components are observed.

4. Isolate

Trace the Exact Source

Determine whether the movement is inside the ball joint or is being transferred from a bearing, bush, steering joint or other component.

5. Compare

Separate Wear From Design

Consider the suspension design, manufacturer information and whether the observed movement represents intended articulation rather than excessive clearance.

6. Decide

Repair the Confirmed Fault

Only after the source and severity are established should the repair decision be made — whether that means a separate ball joint, complete suspension arm or another component entirely.

Critical Distinction

Designed Movement vs Excessive Ball Joint Wear

Not every movement seen during a suspension inspection is excessive wear. Suspension systems contain joints and compliant components that are specifically designed to move.

A ball joint must articulate. Rubber bushes can deflect. Suspension arms change angle as the wheel travels. The diagnostic task is to distinguish those intended movements from unwanted clearance caused by deterioration.

Designed Movement

What May Be Normal

  • Ball-joint articulation through its intended angle
  • Controlled rubber-bush deflection
  • Suspension-arm movement as the wheel rises and falls
  • Movement created by normal suspension geometry
  • Component behaviour permitted by its design
Wear

What Needs Investigation

  • Unwanted clearance inside a joint
  • Movement accompanied by knocking or looseness
  • Visible separation between surfaces that should remain located
  • Movement beyond an applicable limit
  • A joint becoming insecure or at risk of detachment
Why false diagnosis happens

If a component is tested while loaded incorrectly, movement from another joint or a compliant bush can be mistaken for ball-joint play. The reverse can also happen: suspension load can mask wear that becomes clearer when the joint is inspected using the correct method.

The question is not “does it move?”

The useful question is: “Is this movement intended by the design, or is there excessive unwanted clearance in the component?”

Measurement & Limits

How Much Ball Joint Play Is Too Much?

There is no useful universal rule that says “any movement means failure” or that one single measurement applies to every ball joint on every vehicle.

Ball-joint design, suspension layout and manufacturer limits can differ. Where an applicable manufacturer wear limit exists, that information matters. MOT inspection guidance also provides general excessive-play criteria for relevant suspension pins where specific manufacturer limits are not available.

First

Identify the Joint

Confirm which suspension joint is being assessed and whether the observed movement is actually occurring inside that joint.

Then

Check the Correct Standard

Use applicable manufacturer information and the correct MOT inspection criteria rather than inventing a single limit for every vehicle.

Finally

Judge the Actual Wear

Compare the confirmed movement with the appropriate standard and consider whether the joint remains secure and serviceable.

Be cautious with internet “play limits”

A measurement quoted for one suspension design should not be applied blindly to another vehicle. The inspection method and applicable limit need to match the component being assessed.

Why this protects drivers from unnecessary repairs

Properly distinguishing designed movement from excessive wear reduces the risk of replacing a serviceable joint. It also reduces the opposite risk — dismissing genuine wear because the movement was checked using the wrong suspension position or inspection method.

Safety Decision

Is It Safe to Drive With a Worn Ball Joint?

A confirmed worn ball joint should not be ignored. The urgency depends on the severity of the wear, but deterioration can progress and severe wear can eventually threaten the security of the joint.

The safest decision is based on what has actually been found — not simply whether the car still feels driveable. A vehicle can appear relatively normal from the driver's seat while measurable suspension wear is present underneath.

Do not judge safety by noise alone

A quiet joint is not automatically healthy, and a noisy suspension is not automatically dangerous. What matters is the confirmed mechanical condition. If excessive wear has been identified, make the repair decision from the severity of that wear rather than from how loud the symptom is.

Next decision

Once the defective component and severity have been confirmed, move to the repair section. The next question is whether the ball joint can be replaced separately, whether the complete suspension arm is required and what additional work — such as wheel alignment — should be considered.

Repair Options

How Is a Worn Ball Joint Repaired?

A worn suspension ball joint normally needs replacement rather than adjustment. The important question is whether the ball joint is available separately or forms part of a complete suspension arm assembly.

This is one reason two cars with apparently similar ball-joint faults can receive very different repair quotes. On one vehicle the garage may replace a relatively inexpensive separate joint. On another, the correct repair may involve replacing the complete lower arm or wishbone.

What Should Be Checked Before Ordering Parts?

  • Confirm which ball joint has excessive wear
  • Confirm whether the joint is replaceable separately
  • Check whether the complete suspension arm is required
  • Inspect the arm bushes while the area is accessible
  • Check the opposite side for similar deterioration
  • Inspect the dust cover and surrounding suspension components
  • Check whether wheel alignment should follow the repair
Ask what the quotation actually includes

A useful quote should make clear whether the garage is replacing the ball joint only or the complete suspension arm, whether labour and VAT are included, and whether wheel alignment is included or charged separately.

Should both sides be replaced together?

Not automatically. A ball joint is not normally replaced in pairs simply because one side has failed. However, if both sides are a similar age and mileage, inspecting the opposite joint and its dust cover is sensible. Replace components according to their actual condition rather than creating unnecessary work.

After Suspension Work

Do You Need Wheel Alignment After Replacing a Ball Joint?

Wheel alignment may be advisable after ball-joint or suspension-arm replacement, but whether adjustment is necessary depends on the vehicle design and the work carried out.

If the old joint had substantial play, the wheel may have been moving away from its intended geometry before the repair. Replacing a complete suspension arm can also affect component positioning, particularly where mounting points or adjustable geometry have been disturbed.

Recommended

Uneven Tyre Wear Was Present

If the worn joint was associated with abnormal tyre wear, check geometry once the mechanical fault has been corrected.

Recommended

Vehicle Pulls or Steering Is Off-Centre

Persistent pulling or an off-centre steering wheel after the repair deserves further investigation rather than being accepted as normal.

Consider

Complete Arm Was Replaced

Where a wishbone or lower arm has been removed and replaced, checking alignment provides useful confirmation that geometry is within specification.

Repair mechanical play before alignment

Wheel alignment is not a substitute for repairing a worn ball joint. If suspension or steering components still have excessive play, attempting to align the vehicle first can produce misleading measurements and an unstable result.

Correct order

Diagnose → repair excessive play → inspect tyres → check alignment → road test. This avoids paying for alignment before the component responsible for uncontrolled wheel movement has been repaired.

After Repair

What Should Be Checked After Ball Joint Replacement?

Replacing the worn component is only part of a good repair. The vehicle should leave the workshop with the joint secure, surrounding components checked and the original complaint reassessed.

Security

Fasteners and Joint Location

Confirm the replacement joint or suspension arm is correctly located and all relevant fixings are tightened using the appropriate procedure.

Protection

Dust Cover Condition

Check that the new joint's protective boot is correctly seated, undamaged and not twisted or trapped during installation.

Road Behaviour

Confirm the Original Symptom

If the vehicle originally knocked, wandered or pulled, verify whether the repair has corrected that complaint rather than assuming it has.

Tyres

Inspect Existing Wear

Replacing the joint does not reverse tyre damage that has already occurred. Check tread condition and remaining legal usability.

Geometry

Check Alignment Where Appropriate

Confirm geometry where the repair, previous wear pattern or road behaviour gives a reason to do so.

Other Side

Compare the Opposite Joint

The opposite side does not automatically need replacing, but its condition is worth checking where both components share similar age and mileage.

If the knock remains after replacement

Do not automatically replace another ball joint. Return to the diagnosis. Anti-roll bar links, suspension bushes, top mounts, dampers and steering components can all produce similar noises. The remaining symptom needs to be traced to its source.

MOT Retest

What Happens After a Ball Joint MOT Failure?

Once the defect has been repaired, the vehicle needs the appropriate MOT retest before you treat the original failure as resolved.

The exact retest arrangements and whether a fee applies depend on factors including where the repair is carried out, when the vehicle is returned and the applicable MOT retest rules. Do not assume every retest is automatically free.

Step 1

Repair the Correct Defect

Use the MOT result together with proper diagnosis to make sure the component responsible for the excessive wear has actually been repaired.

Step 2

Keep the Repair Evidence

Retain the invoice or repair record, particularly if you plan to sell the vehicle later. It helps explain how the recorded MOT failure was resolved.

Step 3

Complete the Retest

Follow the applicable retest procedure rather than assuming the vehicle's MOT status changes simply because the component has been replaced.

A repair invoice is not an MOT pass

Repairing the ball joint does not itself change the recorded MOT result. Where a vehicle has failed its MOT, follow the correct retest process and confirm the vehicle's current MOT status.

Retest arrangements can depend on the circumstances of the repair and return to the test station. Use the current GOV.UK MOT retest guidance when deciding whether the vehicle can be retested partially and whether a retest fee applies.

Used-Car Check

Should You Buy a Used Car With a Ball Joint MOT Failure?

A previous ball-joint MOT failure is not automatically a reason to reject a used car. What matters is whether the defect was repaired properly and whether the MOT history suggests an isolated repair or a wider pattern of neglected suspension maintenance.

Ball joints are wear components. On an older or higher-mileage car, replacement at some point is not unusual. The buying risk increases when the same defect keeps returning, several suspension defects appear together, tyre wear is poor or the seller cannot show what was repaired.

Failure Followed by Clear Repair

A single historic failure followed by a prompt pass and supporting repair evidence is generally easier to explain.

Repeated ball-joint, bush, tyre or suspension advisories can suggest developing wear or maintenance being postponed.

Multiple suspension faults, poor tyres, steering symptoms and no evidence of repair can turn a modest ball-joint job into a much larger suspension bill.

Used-Car Ball Joint Checklist

  • Read several years of MOT history, not only the latest test
  • Look for repeated suspension-joint advisories or failures
  • Check for repeated tyre-wear or steering-related entries
  • Ask for invoices showing which joint or suspension arm was replaced
  • Inspect the current tyre wear across both front tyres
  • Listen for knocking or clunking during the test drive
  • Check whether the steering feels vague or the vehicle pulls
  • Obtain an independent inspection if the history is unclear
One repaired ball joint is not the real buying problem

The bigger warning sign is a pattern: repeated suspension advisories, recurring tyre wear, several worn joints, poor maintenance and no evidence that defects were properly repaired. Judge the vehicle's history as a whole rather than rejecting it simply because a wear component once failed an MOT.

Budget before negotiating

If the vehicle still has a ball-joint or suspension concern, get a realistic repair estimate before agreeing a price. A cheap separate joint and a complete suspension-arm repair are financially very different — especially if tyres, alignment or additional suspension components are also required.

FAQs

Ball Joint MOT FAQs

Can a ball joint fail an MOT?

Yes. A suspension ball joint can fail an MOT if it is excessively worn. Excessive wear is a Major defect. If the joint is worn badly enough that it is likely to become detached, it is a Dangerous defect.

Is a worn ball joint a Major MOT defect?

Yes. Excessive wear in a suspension ball joint is classified as a Major defect and causes an MOT failure.

Can a ball joint be a Dangerous MOT defect?

Yes. If a suspension ball joint is worn badly enough that it is likely to become detached, it can be classified as a Dangerous defect.

Can a damaged ball joint dust cover fail an MOT?

Yes, depending on its condition. A suspension-joint dust cover that is severely deteriorated is a Minor defect. If the cover is missing or no longer prevents dirt entering the joint, it is a Major defect.

Will a split ball joint boot fail an MOT?

It can. The MOT result depends on whether the dust cover still protects the joint. Severe deterioration can be a Minor defect, while a cover that is missing or no longer prevents dirt entering the joint is a Major defect.

How does an MOT tester check a ball joint?

The tester checks suspension joints for excessive wear or play using the appropriate inspection method for the suspension design. This can include wheel play detectors, rocking or shaking the wheel and levering the suspension while observing the relevant components.

What are the symptoms of a worn ball joint?

Possible symptoms include knocking or clunking over bumps, vague steering, wandering, uneven tyre wear and unwanted movement in the suspension. These symptoms are not unique to ball joints, so the source of the play should be confirmed before replacing parts.

Can a worn ball joint cause knocking?

Yes. A worn ball joint can cause knocking or clunking as clearance develops inside the joint, although anti-roll bar links, bushes, strut mounts and other suspension components can produce similar noises.

Can a worn ball joint cause uneven tyre wear?

Yes. Excessive movement in a worn ball joint can allow suspension geometry to change and may contribute to uneven tyre wear, although alignment faults and other worn suspension components can produce similar patterns.

Is a ball joint the same as a track rod end?

No. Both use ball-and-socket joints, but a suspension ball joint connects suspension components and allows controlled articulation, while a track rod end forms part of the steering linkage.

Can you drive with a worn ball joint?

Driving with a confirmed worn ball joint is not something to ignore. Wear can worsen and severe deterioration can create a risk of joint separation. The vehicle should be inspected and the severity established promptly.

Can a ball joint suddenly fail?

A badly worn or damaged ball joint can deteriorate to the point that separation becomes possible. This is why severe wear and a joint considered likely to become detached are treated seriously.

Do ball joints need to be replaced in pairs?

Not automatically. Replacement should be based on the condition of each joint, although the opposite side should be inspected because components of similar age and mileage can wear at similar rates.

Do you need wheel alignment after replacing a ball joint?

Alignment should be considered after ball joint replacement, particularly where the repair disturbs suspension geometry or where uneven tyre wear, pulling or steering-centre problems are present.

How much does ball joint replacement cost in the UK?

The final cost depends on the vehicle, labour time and whether the ball joint is replaceable separately or supplied as part of a complete suspension arm. Diagnosis and wheel alignment can also affect the total bill.

Can a ball joint pass an MOT with slight play?

Not every visible movement means the joint is defective. Suspension designs can contain normal articulation or compliant movement. The tester must distinguish designed movement from excessive wear before deciding the MOT result.

Can a new ball joint still have movement?

Some apparent movement can come from normal suspension articulation, compliant bushes or the way the suspension is loaded during inspection. Unwanted internal clearance in the ball-and-socket joint is different and should be assessed using the correct procedure.

Should I buy a used car with a ball joint MOT advisory?

A previous ball joint advisory is not automatically a reason to reject a used car, but you should check whether the defect was repaired, whether it appears repeatedly in the MOT history and whether related tyre, steering or suspension wear is present.
