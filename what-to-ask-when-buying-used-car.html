UK used-car buying questions guide

What to Ask When Buying a Used Car in the UK

A used car can look excellent in photographs while hiding expensive faults, questionable history or unresolved paperwork. Asking the right questions before viewing can help you identify warning signs, request evidence and avoid wasting money on the wrong vehicle. This UK guide explains exactly what to ask private sellers and dealers, how to judge their answers and when to walk away.

Quick answer

What should you ask when buying a used car?

Before buying a used car in the UK, ask why the vehicle is being sold, how long the seller has owned it, whether the mileage is genuine, what servicing and repairs have been carried out, whether there are outstanding mechanical faults and whether the vehicle has previously suffered accident damage. You should also ask about outstanding finance, MOT advisories, ownership documents and the evidence available to support the seller's claims.

A trustworthy seller should be willing to answer reasonable questions, explain any gaps in the vehicle's history and allow appropriate independent checks. An answer alone is not proof: important claims should be supported by records or verified through an independent service.

Ask about the history

Confirm how long the seller has owned the vehicle, whether the mileage is supported by records and whether any accident damage or significant repairs have been disclosed.

Ask about the condition

Find out about known faults, warning lights, recent repairs, servicing, tyre condition and any work that may be needed immediately after purchase.

Ask for evidence

Request the V5C details, service invoices, MOT information and other supporting documents. Check finance, theft and write-off history independently where appropriate.

Mechanic insight:

The most useful question is often not simply whether a car has a fault, but what evidence supports the seller's answer. A seller who says the timing belt has been replaced should ideally be able to provide an invoice showing when the work was completed. The same principle applies to gearbox repairs, clutch replacement, MOT advisories and other expensive work.

The seven essential questions

1. Why are you selling the car?

Listen for a clear explanation and check whether the reason for sale is consistent with the advert and ownership history.

2. How long have you owned it?

Very short ownership is not automatically suspicious, but it may justify further questions about the vehicle's condition and the seller's circumstances.

3. Does it have any known faults?

Ask specifically about warning lights, engine problems, gearbox faults, unusual noises, leaks and intermittent electrical issues.

4. Is the service history available?

Ask for invoices or digital service records, not simply an assurance that the car has been regularly maintained.

5. Have MOT advisories been repaired?

Ask which advisories remain unresolved and request supporting invoices for repairs the seller claims have been completed.

6. Is there outstanding finance?

Ask directly and arrange an independent vehicle history check before paying. A seller's assurance is not enough.

7. Can I arrange an independent inspection?

A reasonable request for an inspection can help identify hidden problems. Be cautious if the seller refuses without a convincing explanation.

Important distinction

This page concentrates on questions to ask a seller and how to assess their responses. For the wider process of choosing, inspecting and purchasing a vehicle, see our complete used-car buying guide. For a detailed MOT record review, use our MOT history checking guide.

Before arranging a viewing

Questions to ask before viewing a used car

Before travelling to inspect a used car, contact the seller and establish whether the vehicle is genuinely worth viewing. A short conversation can reveal missing service records, undisclosed mechanical problems, ownership concerns or differences between the advertisement and the vehicle's actual condition.

Ask clear, specific questions rather than relying on descriptions such as "excellent condition", "drives perfectly" or "no issues". These phrases are subjective and do not confirm that the vehicle has been properly maintained or is free from faults.

Is the car still available?

Confirm that the advertised vehicle is available and that the asking price, mileage, registration and specification are correct before arranging a journey.

Can you confirm the registration?

Request the registration number so you can check the MOT history and compare the advertised details with official vehicle information.

Are there any known problems?

Ask whether the vehicle has engine, gearbox, clutch, suspension, braking or electrical faults, including problems that occur only occasionally.

Can the car be viewed from cold?

Ask whether the engine can remain switched off before your arrival. Some starting problems, rattles and excessive smoke are easier to identify when cold.

Are the documents available?

Confirm that the seller can show the V5C registration certificate, service records and any invoices supporting significant maintenance or repairs.

Will you allow an inspection?

Ask whether an independent mechanic or vehicle inspection service can examine the car before you make a final purchasing decision.

Questions to ask before travelling

Question What a useful answer includes When to investigate further
Is the advertised mileage correct? A confirmed odometer reading supported by MOT or maintenance records. The mileage differs from the advert or the seller cannot explain inconsistencies.
Does the car have any warning lights? A clear explanation of any current or previously diagnosed warning lights. The seller dismisses warning lights as harmless without supporting diagnostic evidence.
Are there any leaks? Disclosure of known oil, coolant, gearbox or other fluid leaks and any repairs completed. The seller reports regular fluid top-ups but cannot explain where the fluid is going.
Has major repair work been completed? Dates, mileage and invoices for significant repairs or component replacements. Expensive work is claimed without records or a convincing explanation.
Can I inspect the car independently? Reasonable access for a viewing, test drive and professional inspection where appropriate. The seller pressures you to buy without checking the vehicle properly.
Practical buying advice

Keep a copy of the original advertisement and important written answers from the seller. If the description changes during the viewing, you can compare what was originally claimed with the condition and documents presented.

Seller identity and ownership

Questions about ownership and reasons for selling

Understanding who is selling the vehicle is an important part of buying safely. A genuine private seller should normally be able to explain their connection to the car, how long they have owned it and why they have decided to sell.

However, the V5C registration certificate identifies the registered keeper, not necessarily the legal owner. It should be treated as an important registration document rather than proof of ownership on its own.

How long have you owned the car?

Longer ownership can make it easier to establish how the vehicle has been maintained. Very short ownership deserves an explanation but does not automatically mean the vehicle is unsuitable.

Why are you selling it?

Ask for the reason behind the sale. A change of vehicle or circumstances may be straightforward, but vague or inconsistent explanations deserve closer attention.

Are you the registered keeper?

Ask whether the seller is named on the V5C and, if not, why they are selling the vehicle on someone else's behalf.

Where is the car normally kept?

A private sale at the seller's normal address may help you check that the circumstances are consistent with the documents presented.

How many previous keepers?

Ask about the number of previous registered keepers and whether the seller knows anything about earlier use or maintenance.

Who has been using the vehicle?

Ask whether the car has mainly been used privately, for business, as a taxi or for another demanding purpose.

Ownership answers that need further checking

Seller's explanation What it might mean Recommended action
"I've owned it for several years." The seller may be familiar with the vehicle's maintenance and previous problems. Compare the claim with the V5C, service records and other available evidence.
"I only bought it recently." There may be a reasonable explanation, or the vehicle may have developed an unexpected problem. Ask why it is being sold so soon and investigate its mechanical condition.
"I'm selling it for a friend." The person advertising the vehicle may not be the registered keeper or owner. Establish the seller's authority to sell and verify the relevant details.
"I don't have the V5C." The document may be lost, delayed or unavailable for another reason. Do not assume ownership is legitimate. Resolve the documentation issue before proceeding.
"We must meet somewhere else." There may be a genuine reason, but the location could make identity verification more difficult. Ask why and take additional steps to confirm the seller's identity and connection to the vehicle.
Important ownership warning

Do not assume that possession of the keys or V5C proves someone has the legal right to sell a car. If the seller's identity, authority or explanation cannot be established satisfactorily, do not transfer money.

MOT history questions

What to ask a seller about MOT history

A vehicle's MOT history can help you identify previous failures, repeated advisories and defects that may still require attention. Rather than simply asking whether the car has a valid MOT, ask what problems were recorded during previous tests and whether the relevant repairs have been completed.

An MOT pass confirms that the vehicle met the applicable test requirements at the time of inspection. It does not guarantee that the vehicle is mechanically fault-free or that every component will remain reliable.

When was the last MOT?

Confirm the most recent test date and whether the vehicle currently has a valid MOT certificate.

Were there any advisories?

Ask which items were highlighted and whether the seller has arranged repairs since the test.

Have previous failures been repaired?

Ask for evidence of repairs following earlier MOT failures, especially where significant safety components were involved.

Has corrosion been reported?

Ask whether corrosion affecting structural areas, brake pipes or suspension mountings has been inspected and repaired.

Have brake defects been addressed?

Request details of repairs involving brake discs, pads, pipes, hoses, calipers or braking performance.

Are repair invoices available?

Ask for dated invoices showing what work was completed rather than relying on a verbal assurance.

Questions to ask about common MOT history patterns

MOT history pattern Question to ask What to verify
Repeated tyre advisories "Have the tyres been replaced, and was the cause of the wear checked?" Current tyre condition and any evidence of alignment or suspension-related repairs.
Brake wear advisories "Were the affected brakes inspected or replaced after the advisory?" Repair invoices and the present condition of the braking components.
Suspension defects "Which suspension parts were repaired, and are there any remaining noises?" Supporting invoices and evidence that the reported defect was addressed.
Corrosion advisories "Has the affected area been inspected, treated or professionally repaired?" The current condition and whether any repairs are properly documented.
Repeated MOT failures "What caused the failures, and what work was carried out?" Whether the faults were repaired and whether the same problems returned in later tests.
Mileage inconsistencies "Can you explain the recorded mileage difference?" Service records, instrument replacement evidence and other supporting documentation.
Check the official MOT record

Use the official GOV.UK MOT history service to review recorded tests independently. For a detailed explanation of how to interpret failures, advisories and mileage records, read our guide to checking MOT history before buying a car.

Mechanic insight

Pay particular attention when a seller claims that an advisory has been repaired but cannot provide evidence. A previous MOT pass or subsequent test does not necessarily prove that every earlier advisory was repaired. Ask what work was completed and inspect the current condition independently.

Servicing and maintenance

Questions about service history and previous repairs

Service history helps establish whether a vehicle has received appropriate maintenance throughout its life. A well-maintained used car should have records that are reasonably consistent with its age, mileage and manufacturer servicing schedule.

Ask about specific maintenance work rather than simply asking whether the vehicle has a full service history. Different vehicles have different requirements, and a service stamp alone may not establish exactly which components were replaced.

When was the last service?

Ask for the date, mileage and work completed during the most recent scheduled service.

How often was the oil changed?

Request evidence of engine oil and filter changes at the appropriate intervals for the vehicle.

Has the timing belt been replaced?

Where the engine uses a scheduled timing belt, ask when it was replaced and whether an invoice is available.

Has the gearbox been serviced?

Ask whether the gearbox has received the maintenance specified by the manufacturer, particularly on automatic vehicles.

Have major repairs been completed?

Ask about previous clutch, gearbox, engine, turbocharger, cooling-system or suspension repairs.

Are there outstanding maintenance items?

Ask whether any servicing, repairs or replacement parts are currently overdue or expected soon.

Important servicing questions and supporting evidence

Maintenance item Question to ask Evidence to request
Engine oil and filters "When were the oil and filters last changed?" Service invoice, digital record or documented maintenance entry.
Timing belt "Has the belt been replaced according to the manufacturer's schedule?" Invoice confirming the replacement date, mileage and work carried out.
Timing chain "Has the engine had any timing-chain noise, repairs or related problems?" Relevant diagnostic or repair records if work has been completed.
Automatic gearbox "Has the gearbox been serviced at the required intervals?" Records showing the correct maintenance for that transmission.
Cooling system "Have there been any overheating problems or coolant leaks?" Repair invoices and evidence of completed cooling-system work.
Brakes and suspension "Which components have been replaced recently?" Dated invoices identifying the parts and repairs.
Important maintenance warning

A vehicle advertised with "full service history" may still have overdue maintenance. Compare the records against the manufacturer's schedule and the vehicle's actual mileage. Missing evidence of an expensive scheduled job, such as a timing belt replacement, should be considered before agreeing on a price.

Mechanic insight

Ask whether a repair fixed the original problem completely. For example, if the seller mentions a previous coolant leak, find out which component failed, what was replaced and whether the system has remained stable since the repair. The explanation matters as much as the fact that money was spent.

Accident and damage questions

Questions to ask about accident damage and previous repairs

Previous accident damage does not automatically make a used car unsuitable. However, buyers should understand the nature of any damage, how it was repaired and whether the work affected structural or safety-related components.

Ask direct questions about collisions, insurance write-offs, body repairs, structural damage and replacement panels. Do not assume that a clean-looking exterior proves a vehicle has never been damaged.

Has the car been involved in an accident?

Ask about previous collisions, including incidents that were repaired privately without an insurance claim.

Has it been an insurance write-off?

Ask whether the vehicle has ever received an insurance salvage category and verify the answer through a suitable history check.

Have any body panels been replaced?

Ask about replacement doors, wings, bumpers, bonnets, tailgates and other repaired exterior components.

Was structural damage repaired?

Ask whether the chassis, body structure, suspension mountings or other important structural areas were affected.

Were airbags deployed?

Ask whether any airbags, seatbelt pretensioners or related safety systems required replacement.

Are repair records available?

Request invoices, inspection reports or other evidence showing the extent and quality of previous repairs.

How to assess answers about previous damage

Seller's answer Potential concern Recommended response
"It only had a bumper repaired." Damage may have been cosmetic, but hidden mounting or structural damage is possible. Ask for photographs and repair evidence. Inspect the affected area.
"It was repaired after an accident." The seriousness of the original damage is unclear. Establish what was damaged, who repaired it and whether safety-related components were involved.
"It was a Category N write-off." The insurer classified the vehicle as having non-structural damage, but repairs may still have been significant. Request detailed repair evidence and arrange an independent inspection.
"It was a Category S write-off." The insurer classified the vehicle as having structural damage. Treat it as a higher-risk purchase and obtain specialist evidence of repair quality.
"It has never been damaged." The claim may be accurate, but it is not independently verified. Compare the claim with vehicle history records and physical inspection.
Accident history warning

A vehicle history check can identify certain recorded insurance losses, but it cannot guarantee that every previous collision or privately funded repair will appear. Where accident damage is suspected, independent physical inspection remains important.

Keep this page focused on seller questions

The purpose of these questions is to establish what the seller knows, what they are willing to disclose and which claims can be verified. Detailed bodywork inspection and accident repair assessment should form part of a separate physical vehicle inspection.

Mechanical condition

Questions to ask about mechanical faults before buying a used car

Mechanical condition is one of the most important subjects to discuss with a used-car seller. A vehicle may start, drive and appear presentable while still having intermittent engine faults, gearbox problems, cooling-system issues or developing suspension defects. Ask about known symptoms, previous diagnoses and repairs rather than accepting a general statement that the car drives well.

Your aim is not to diagnose every component during the conversation. It is to establish what the seller knows, identify contradictions and decide which areas require closer inspection. A seller's answers should be compared with the vehicle's actual behaviour, maintenance records and any independent inspection findings.

Does the engine have any known faults?

Ask about difficult starting, rough idling, misfires, hesitation, loss of power, excessive smoke and unusual engine noises. Find out whether any symptoms occur intermittently or only under particular conditions.

Have any warning lights appeared?

Ask whether the engine management, ABS, airbag, gearbox or other warning lights have appeared recently. Request diagnostic or repair evidence where faults have previously been investigated.

Does the gearbox operate correctly?

For manual cars, ask about difficult gear selection, clutch slipping and judder. For automatics, ask about delayed engagement, slipping, harsh changes or transmission warning messages.

Has the car overheated?

Ask about previous overheating incidents, coolant loss, temperature warnings, radiator repairs and unexplained cooling-system pressure. Request details of any completed repairs.

Are there unusual noises or vibrations?

Ask about knocking over bumps, wheel-bearing noise, steering vibration, brake judder and rattles during acceleration. Establish whether the cause has been identified.

Are any repairs currently needed?

Ask directly whether the vehicle needs work now or is likely to need expensive maintenance soon. Clarify whether any parts have been recommended for replacement by a garage.

Mechanical fault questions and answers to investigate

Question Seller's answer Recommended action
Has the engine management light appeared? "It came on once, but it went away." Ask when it happened, whether diagnostic codes were recorded and what repair was completed. An extinguished warning light does not establish that the fault has been resolved.
Does the engine use oil? "I top it up regularly." Ask how much oil is added, over what mileage, and whether consumption or leakage has been investigated.
Does the car lose coolant? "It needs a little coolant occasionally." Ask about pressure testing, visible leaks, overheating and previous cooling-system repairs. Unexplained coolant loss needs investigation.
Does the gearbox change smoothly? "It sometimes hesitates, but they all do that." Do not accept the explanation without evidence. Ask whether the behaviour has been diagnosed and consider an independent inspection.
Are there suspension noises? "There is a small knock over bumps." Ask which area is affected, whether a garage has inspected it and what repair has been recommended.
Have any faults been repaired recently? "Everything has been sorted." Request invoices identifying the original faults, replacement parts and dates of repair.
Important mechanical warning

Be particularly cautious when a seller describes overheating, persistent coolant loss, significant oil consumption, gearbox slipping or recurring warning lights as normal characteristics. These symptoms may indicate expensive faults. Obtain a satisfactory diagnosis before agreeing to buy.

Mechanic insight

Ask whether a fault was diagnosed or merely treated. Replacing a sensor, clearing a warning light or topping up a fluid does not necessarily correct the underlying cause. A useful repair record should identify the reported problem, the work completed and ideally the result of subsequent checks.

Test-drive questions

What to ask during a used-car test drive

A test drive provides an opportunity to compare the seller's description with the vehicle's actual behaviour. Before starting, ask whether there are any known issues that could affect the drive, whether the vehicle is insured for your use and whether a suitable route can be arranged.

The questions below focus on the seller's explanations and disclosures. They are not a replacement for a complete mechanical test-drive inspection.

Can I see a cold start?

Ask whether the engine has already been running. If it has, find out why. Cold starting may reveal symptoms that are less noticeable once warm.

Is that noise normal for this car?

If you hear knocking, rattling, whining or scraping, ask whether the seller has noticed it before and whether a garage has investigated.

Has the clutch or gearbox been repaired?

If gear engagement feels unusual, ask whether similar symptoms have occurred previously and what work has been completed.

Has the steering or suspension been checked?

Ask about previous alignment work, steering repairs or suspension replacements if the car pulls, vibrates or makes noises.

Have the brakes caused any problems?

If braking feels uneven or produces vibration, ask whether the seller has experienced the same behaviour and whether repairs were advised.

Does everything work as advertised?

Ask specifically about air conditioning, heating, windows, central locking, parking sensors, infotainment and other equipment.

Questions when something unusual happens during the drive

What you notice Question to ask the seller Why the answer matters
Engine hesitation "Has it hesitated or lost power before, and has it been diagnosed?" The seller may know about an intermittent fault that was not disclosed earlier.
Gearbox hesitation or jerking "Has the transmission behaved like this previously, and are there repair records?" A previous diagnosis may help establish whether the behaviour is a known problem.
Knocking over bumps "Has a garage inspected that noise?" The answer may reveal previously recommended suspension or steering repairs.
Brake vibration "Have the brakes been checked or replaced recently?" Recent work or unresolved faults may explain the symptom, but inspection is still necessary.
Warning light appears "Has that warning appeared before, and what caused it?" Previous fault codes or repair records may identify a recurring problem.
Temperature rises abnormally "Has the car overheated or needed coolant?" Overheating can indicate a serious mechanical problem. Stop the drive safely rather than continuing the test.
Test-drive safety

Do not continue driving a vehicle that develops a serious braking problem, severe overheating, loss of steering control or another potentially dangerous fault. Stop safely and arrange an appropriate inspection. Confirm that you are legally insured before driving the vehicle on public roads.

Finance and legal checks

Questions about outstanding finance, theft and vehicle identity

Before paying for a used car, ask whether there is outstanding finance, whether the vehicle has ever been reported stolen and whether its identity and registration details are correct. These are important questions because a vehicle can appear mechanically sound while having legal or financial complications.

A seller's answer should be treated as a starting point. Use suitable independent checks and resolve discrepancies before completing the transaction.

Is there outstanding finance?

Ask whether the vehicle is subject to hire purchase, personal contract purchase or another finance agreement that may affect the seller's right to transfer ownership.

Has the finance been settled?

If the seller says an agreement has been paid off, request appropriate confirmation and independently verify the finance status before purchasing.

Has the vehicle been reported stolen?

Ask directly, but do not rely on the answer alone. A suitable vehicle history check may identify relevant theft records.

Does the VIN match?

Ask to inspect the vehicle identification number and compare it with the V5C and other available vehicle records.

Is the V5C available?

Confirm that the registration certificate is available and that its details are consistent with the vehicle. Remember that the V5C is not proof of legal ownership.

Are there any identity discrepancies?

Ask about registration changes, imported status, replacement identification plates or differences between the documents and the vehicle.

Finance and identity questions before agreeing to buy

Question What to verify When to stop the purchase
Is the vehicle finance-free? Independent finance status and any evidence of settlement. Finance remains unresolved or the seller cannot demonstrate authority to complete the sale.
Is the registration genuine? Registration details against official vehicle information. Significant inconsistencies cannot be explained.
Does the VIN match the documents? The physical VIN and the corresponding documentation. Identification numbers appear altered or do not match.
Is the seller entitled to sell? Seller identity, authority and supporting documentation. The seller's authority remains unclear or inconsistent.
Are theft records clear? Appropriate independent vehicle history information. A theft concern is identified and cannot be satisfactorily resolved.
Important finance warning

Do not assume that a seller can transfer clear title simply because they possess the keys and V5C. Outstanding finance can create ownership complications, although the legal position depends on the circumstances and may include protections for certain private purchasers. Resolve finance concerns before paying rather than relying on a promise that the balance will be settled later.

Seller type

Questions to ask a dealer versus a private seller

The questions you ask should reflect whether the vehicle is being sold by a motor trader or a genuine private seller. Both should provide accurate information, but your legal rights and the evidence available may differ.

When buying from a trader, ask about the vehicle's preparation, inspection, warranty arrangements and how problems will be handled after purchase. With a private seller, pay particular attention to the seller's identity, ownership history and what they personally know about the car.

Questions for a motor dealer

  • ✓What pre-sale inspection has been completed?
  • ✓Were any faults identified during preparation?
  • ✓Which repairs were completed before advertising?
  • ✓What service and vehicle history records are supplied?
  • ✓What warranty is included, and what does it cover?
  • ✓Who should I contact if a fault develops?
  • ✓Are there additional fees or conditions?

Questions for a private seller

  • ✓How long have you owned the car?
  • ✓Why are you selling it?
  • ✓Are you the registered keeper?
  • ✓What faults have you experienced?
  • ✓Which repairs have been completed?
  • ✓Can I inspect the vehicle at your address?
  • ✓Can I arrange an independent inspection?

Dealer and private seller questions compared

Buying consideration Motor dealer Private seller
Vehicle history Ask what checks the business completed before offering the car. Ask what the seller knows from their ownership and records.
Mechanical condition Ask about preparation inspections, identified faults and repairs. Ask about symptoms experienced during everyday use.
After-sale problems Ask how the trader handles complaints and statutory rights. Establish what representations the seller is making about the vehicle's condition.
Warranty Ask whether a commercial warranty is included and request its terms. A genuine private sale will not normally include a commercial dealer warranty.
Seller identity Verify the trading business and its contact details. Verify the individual's identity and authority to sell.
Know the difference in your rights

When buying from a trader, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 generally requires goods to be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described, taking relevant circumstances into account. A genuine private sale normally provides fewer statutory protections, although the vehicle must still be accurately described and the seller must have the right to sell it. A dealer cannot remove statutory consumer rights simply by offering a limited warranty.

Watch for disguised trading

Be cautious if someone appears to sell vehicles regularly while claiming every transaction is a private sale. The seller's actual trading activity and circumstances matter more than the label used in an advertisement. Do not assume you are buying privately without checking.

Buying warning signs

Seller red flags that should make you reconsider a used car

A seller's behaviour can be as informative as the vehicle's condition. A genuine seller may not know every technical detail, but should generally be willing to answer reasonable questions honestly and allow appropriate checks.

Individual warning signs do not always prove wrongdoing. However, several inconsistent explanations, missing documents or attempts to prevent inspection can significantly increase the risk of a poor purchase.

Refusing an independent inspection

High concern

A seller who will not allow reasonable checks may be making it difficult to identify faults or verify the vehicle's condition.

Pressuring you to pay immediately

High concern

Claims that you must transfer money immediately to secure the car can discourage proper checks and considered decisions.

Inconsistent ownership explanations

High concern

Changing stories about who owns the vehicle, where it is kept or why it is being sold should be investigated before proceeding.

Missing maintenance evidence

Investigate

Missing service records do not automatically make a car unsuitable, but they increase uncertainty about previous maintenance.

Unexplained mileage discrepancies

High concern

Mileage inconsistencies require a credible explanation supported by records before you can assess the vehicle confidently.

Dismissed mechanical symptoms

Investigate

Be cautious when obvious faults are described as normal without a diagnosis or supporting manufacturer information.

Common seller statements and what to do next

Seller statement Why it matters Buyer response
"There is no time for an inspection." You may be unable to establish the vehicle's true condition. Do not proceed until suitable inspection arrangements can be made.
"The warning light is only a sensor." The explanation may be a guess rather than a confirmed diagnosis. Request diagnostic evidence and a repair estimate.
"I have lost all the paperwork." Important history and identity information may be unavailable. Independently verify the vehicle and resolve documentation concerns.
"You must pay a deposit now." Urgency may be used to discourage verification. Do not send money until you are satisfied with the seller and vehicle.
"It passed its MOT, so nothing is wrong." An MOT pass is not a comprehensive mechanical condition guarantee. Review the history and arrange an appropriate mechanical inspection.
"The finance will be cleared after you pay." The vehicle may still be subject to an unresolved agreement. Resolve and independently verify the finance position before buying.
When the seller's answers are not enough

If important claims cannot be verified, do not allow a low price, attractive specification or pressure from the seller to override the evidence. Walking away from an uncertain purchase is usually preferable to accepting an unresolved financial, legal or mechanical risk.

Buying decision

Should you buy, negotiate or walk away based on the seller's answers?

Once you have questioned the seller and reviewed the available evidence, assess whether the remaining uncertainty is acceptable. A missing minor service invoice is different from unresolved finance, an unidentified vehicle or a serious mechanical fault.

Consider buying

Lower uncertainty

The seller's explanations are consistent, important claims are supported by evidence, independent checks are satisfactory and the vehicle's condition matches the agreed price.

Consider negotiating

Known costs

Minor faults or overdue maintenance have been identified, repair costs are reasonably established and there are no unresolved ownership or serious safety concerns.

Walk away or pause

Unresolved risk

The seller cannot establish authority to sell, significant records conflict, serious faults remain unexplained or appropriate inspections are refused.

Finding Decision Reason
Consistent history and satisfactory inspection Consider buying Important questions have been answered and the evidence supports the purchase.
Minor documented maintenance due Negotiate A known repair or service cost may reasonably affect the price.
Missing service records Investigate or negotiate Maintenance uncertainty may justify further inspection and budgeting.
Unexplained warning lights or gearbox symptoms Pause pending diagnosis The cause and potential repair cost have not been established.
Unresolved finance or identity discrepancies Do not proceed Legal or ownership concerns must be resolved before payment.
Refusal of reasonable inspection Walk away The buyer cannot obtain adequate confidence in the vehicle's condition.
Serious safety defect identified Do not drive or purchase without resolution Safety must take priority over price or convenience.
Mechanic's buying advice

Negotiate using evidence rather than assumptions. If a garage identifies a specific repair, obtain a realistic estimate before discussing the price. Avoid accepting an unknown major fault simply because the seller offers a small discount.

Before making payment

Final questions to ask before paying for a used car

Before transferring money, make sure the seller's answers are consistent with the documents, vehicle history and inspection findings. This is your final opportunity to clarify outstanding concerns, confirm what is included in the sale and establish exactly who will receive the payment.

Do not assume that agreeing a price means every question has been resolved. Important matters such as outstanding finance, missing documents, promised repairs and additional charges should be settled before completing the transaction.

Is the agreed price final?

Confirm the total amount payable and whether any administration, preparation or other charges have been added to the advertised price.

What exactly is included?

Ask about spare keys, service records, accessories, charging cables where applicable and any equipment mentioned in the advertisement.

Have promised repairs been completed?

If the seller agreed to replace tyres, repair a warning light or complete servicing, request evidence before making the final payment.

Who should receive the payment?

Confirm the recipient's identity and the payment arrangements. Be cautious if the seller unexpectedly asks you to transfer money to an unrelated person.

Will I receive a written receipt?

Request a dated receipt identifying the vehicle, seller, buyer, agreed price and any relevant written conditions of the transaction.

How will the keeper change be completed?

Confirm how the DVLA registered keeper notification will be handled and ensure you receive the correct new keeper details or documentation.

Final payment questions and what to verify

Question What to confirm Why it matters
Is the vehicle still as described? The specification, mileage, condition and included equipment match the agreement. Prevents misunderstandings about what you are actually purchasing.
Are all agreed repairs complete? Promised work has been carried out and supporting evidence is available. Avoids relying on repairs that may never be completed.
Is the finance position clear? Any relevant finance agreement has been satisfactorily resolved. Reduces the risk of ownership disputes after payment.
Are the keys and documents available? The agreed keys, records and relevant vehicle documents will be supplied. Missing items can create inconvenience and additional costs.
Will the transaction be recorded? A written invoice or receipt identifies the vehicle, parties and payment. Provides important evidence of the transaction.
Can the vehicle be driven away legally? Insurance, vehicle tax, MOT requirements and roadworthiness have been addressed. Buying a vehicle does not automatically make it legal or safe to drive.
Important payment warning

Never transfer a substantial payment simply because the seller promises to provide documents, clear outstanding finance or complete essential repairs afterwards. Resolve important conditions before payment, and use a payment method that provides an appropriate record of the transaction.

Deposits and agreements

Questions to ask before leaving a used-car deposit

Some sellers request a deposit to reserve a vehicle. Before agreeing, ask what the deposit covers, whether it is refundable and what happens if the car fails an independent inspection or the seller cannot meet an agreed condition.

Deposit arrangements can have legal consequences. Do not assume that every deposit is automatically refundable or non-refundable. The circumstances, agreed terms and applicable consumer law matter.

Questions about the deposit

  • ✓How much is the deposit?
  • ✓Is it refundable, and under what conditions?
  • ✓How long will the vehicle be reserved?
  • ✓Will the deposit count towards the purchase price?
  • ✓What happens if the inspection identifies a serious fault?
  • ✓Will the terms be confirmed in writing?

Questions about the agreement

  • ✓Is the vehicle clearly identified by registration and VIN?
  • ✓Are promised repairs recorded in writing?
  • ✓Are any outstanding checks still required?
  • ✓Is the final price clearly stated?
  • ✓Are collection arrangements confirmed?
  • ✓Who is responsible if agreed conditions are not met?
Practical deposit advice

Where possible, complete the important vehicle and seller checks before paying a deposit. If you do agree to one, obtain a written record of the amount, vehicle details, conditions and any agreed refund arrangements. Avoid relying on verbal promises.

Key takeaways

What matters most when questioning a used-car seller?

The purpose of asking questions is not to find a vehicle with a perfect history. Most used cars have experienced normal wear, servicing and occasional repairs. What matters is whether the seller gives a clear account of the vehicle, whether important claims can be verified and whether the remaining risks are acceptable.

Ask specific questions

Good preparation

Questions about individual faults, servicing, ownership and repairs are more useful than simply asking whether the car is reliable.

Verify important answers

Evidence matters

Check the MOT history, vehicle identity, finance status and available maintenance records independently where appropriate.

Do not ignore contradictions

Stop and investigate

Inconsistent ownership details, unexplained mechanical faults or pressure to pay before checking should never be dismissed.

Final mechanic-style advice

A good used-car purchase starts with clear questions, continues with independent verification and ends with a decision based on evidence rather than pressure. If the seller cannot satisfactorily explain an important issue, investigate it before agreeing to buy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What to Ask When Buying a Used Car FAQs

These are some of the most common questions UK buyers ask about speaking to used-car sellers, checking their answers, requesting evidence and deciding whether to proceed.

What are the most important questions to ask when buying a used car?

Ask why the car is being sold, how long the seller has owned it, whether it has any known faults, what servicing has been completed, whether MOT advisories were repaired, whether it has accident history and whether any finance remains outstanding. Request evidence for important claims.

What should I ask a private seller before viewing a car?

Ask for the registration number, current mileage, ownership history, reason for selling, service records, known faults and whether the car can be viewed from cold. Confirm that the seller can show the V5C and allow appropriate vehicle checks.

What should I ask a used-car dealer?

Ask what pre-sale inspections have been completed, whether any faults were identified, what repairs were carried out, which documents are supplied, what warranty is included and whether additional charges apply. Ask how problems after purchase will be handled.

Should I ask why someone is selling their car?

Yes. The reason for selling can provide useful context. A straightforward explanation is reassuring, while changing or contradictory answers deserve further investigation. A reasonable explanation alone does not prove the car is fault-free.

What should I ask about a used car's service history?

Ask when the vehicle was last serviced, which maintenance items were completed, whether major scheduled work is due and whether dated invoices or digital service records are available. Compare the evidence with the manufacturer's maintenance requirements.

What should I ask about previous MOT advisories?

Ask which advisories were recorded, whether the affected components were repaired and whether invoices are available. Check the official MOT history independently because a later pass does not necessarily prove every earlier advisory was repaired.

Should I ask whether a used car has outstanding finance?

Yes. Ask whether any finance agreement remains attached to the vehicle and whether previous agreements have been settled. Independently verify the position before paying rather than relying solely on the seller's assurance.

What if the seller refuses an independent inspection?

Ask why the inspection is being refused. If you cannot arrange reasonable checks or obtain satisfactory evidence of the vehicle's condition, reconsider the purchase. You should not be pressured into buying without understanding the risks.

What questions should I ask before paying a deposit?

Ask whether the deposit is refundable, what conditions apply, how long the vehicle will be reserved and what happens if an inspection identifies a serious fault. Obtain the agreed terms in writing before paying.

When should I walk away from a used-car seller?

Walk away or pause the purchase if vehicle identity cannot be verified, ownership or finance concerns remain unresolved, the seller refuses reasonable checks, serious faults are unexplained or you are pressured to transfer money before completing essential verification.
