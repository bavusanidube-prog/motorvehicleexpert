Quick answer

What does an MOT advisory mean?

An MOT advisory means the vehicle has passed the MOT test, but the tester has recorded an issue that should be monitored, maintained or repaired. It is not a failure, but it is still important because it can show wear, deterioration, corrosion, leakage or a developing safety concern.

The safest way to read an advisory is this: the car was good enough to pass at the time of the test, but it may not remain that way if the issue is ignored. For a driver, it is a repair-planning warning. For a used car buyer, it is a clue about future spending and past maintenance.

It passed today

An advisory is not the same as a major or dangerous MOT failure.

It needs attention

The tester has noticed something worth monitoring, checking or repairing.

It can get worse

Tyres, brakes, suspension, corrosion and leaks can become future failures.

Mechanic-style rule:

Do not judge a car by “MOT passed” alone. Always read the advisory wording and check whether the same issue appears in previous years.

Real experience

What I normally see with MOT advisories

In real workshop situations, many drivers see a pass and forget about the advisory section. That is where problems begin. Advisories are often early warnings that a component is wearing, corroding, leaking or getting close to a limit.

A single advisory on an older car is not always serious. What matters more is the type of advisory and whether it repeats. A repeated advisory for tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, corrosion or leaks can show delayed maintenance.

When buying a used car, advisories are extremely useful. They can show where money may need to be spent next, whether the seller has maintained the vehicle properly and whether the asking price reflects the real condition of the car.

Workshop note:

A pass with advisories is still a pass, but it is not a clean bill of health. A car can pass an MOT and still need repairs, servicing or preventative maintenance.

Plain English meaning

What an MOT advisory actually means

An MOT advisory is a note from the MOT tester. It tells you that something was noticed during the inspection, but it was not bad enough to fail the vehicle at that moment.

The advisory may relate to wear, age, corrosion, play, deterioration, minor leakage, tyre condition, brake condition, suspension movement, steering components, exhaust condition or other visible issues.

Advisory does not mean failed

The car can still receive a valid MOT certificate if it only has advisories.

Advisory does not mean ignore

The advisory is recorded because the issue may need checking, maintenance or repair.

Advisory can show future cost

Brake pads, tyres, brake pipes, suspension arms and corrosion can become expensive later.

Advisory history matters

Repeated advisories can reveal whether a vehicle has been neglected or properly maintained.

MOT defect classes

MOT advisory vs minor defect vs major defect

An advisory is not the same as a defect category, but drivers often confuse them. The easiest way to understand it is that an advisory is a warning note, while a major or dangerous defect is a failure issue.

Advisory

The vehicle passes, but the tester has recorded something that should be monitored or repaired before it worsens.

Minor defect

A defect exists but is not serious enough to fail the MOT. It should still be repaired because it can worsen.

Major or dangerous defect

The vehicle fails the MOT. A dangerous defect should not be driven until repaired.

Repair priority

Which MOT advisories should you worry about most?

The most professional way to judge MOT advisories is not by counting them. You should group them by safety risk, repair urgency and whether they are likely to become more expensive if ignored.

Fix urgently

Brake pipe corrosion, structural corrosion, steering play, tyre damage, tyres close to the legal limit, brake fluid leaks, serious suspension wear and any advisory that affects control or braking.

Book in soon

Brake pads wearing thin, brake discs worn or corroded, suspension bushes deteriorating, shock absorber misting, exhaust corrosion and oil leaks that need tracing.

Monitor carefully

Light component wear, early deterioration, minor surface corrosion, small exhaust deterioration or advisory wording that clearly suggests early-stage wear.

Best rule:

Tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, corrosion and leaks should always be treated as the highest-priority advisory groups because they affect safety, repair cost and future MOT risk.

Safety priority

Are MOT advisories serious?

Some MOT advisories are minor, but others are early warnings of safety-related wear or expensive future repairs. The seriousness depends on the part affected, the wording used by the tester, the age of the vehicle and whether the same advisory has appeared before.

Lower risk

Light wear, early deterioration or minor non-safety notes that simply need monitoring.

Medium risk

Tyres wearing low, brake wear, suspension bushes, light leaks, exhaust corrosion or early rust.

High risk

Brake pipe corrosion, structural corrosion, steering play, serious suspension wear, tyre damage or fluid leaks.

The wording matters. “Slightly worn” is usually less urgent than “worn close to limit”, “corroded”, “deteriorated”, “leaking”, “insecure” or “excessive”.

Tester wording

MOT advisory wording clues

Do not only count advisories. Read the wording. Two cars can both have three advisories, but one may be much more risky depending on the words used.

“Slightly worn”

Usually early wear. Monitor it and ask when replacement may be needed.

“Worn close to limit”

More urgent. The part may soon become a failure or safety issue.

“Corroded”

Needs careful checking, especially on brake pipes, sills, subframes and suspension mountings.

“Deteriorated”

Often means rubber, metal, mounting or component condition is declining.

“Leaking”

Leaks can worsen quickly. Identify the fluid and source before the repair becomes expensive.

Same wording every year

Repeated wording suggests the issue has not been properly repaired.

Risk of delay

What happens if you ignore MOT advisories?

Ignoring an advisory does not automatically mean the vehicle is illegal if it passed the MOT, but it can still lead to bigger repair costs, worse safety risk and a future MOT failure.

  • !Tyre advisories can become illegal tread, exposed cords, sidewall damage or poor wet-weather grip.
  • !Brake advisories can become grinding brakes, poor braking performance, imbalance or MOT failure.
  • !Suspension advisories can become knocking, poor handling, uneven tyre wear or failed components.
  • !Corrosion advisories can become structural failures, brake pipe failures or expensive welding repairs.
  • !Leaks can worsen and lead to low oil, coolant loss, brake fluid loss or power steering problems.
  • !Repeated advisories can reduce buyer confidence when you sell the vehicle.
MOT history pattern

Repeated MOT advisories: why they matter

One MOT advisory is not always a big problem. The bigger warning sign is when the same advisory appears year after year. Repeated advisories usually mean the issue has been ignored, delayed or only partly repaired.

Repeated tyre advisories

Can suggest poor tracking, worn suspension, bad alignment or poor maintenance.

Repeated brake advisories

Can mean pads, discs, pipes or hoses are being left until they eventually fail.

Repeated rust advisories

Rust usually gets worse unless repaired properly, so repeated corrosion is a strong warning.

Used car buying tip:

A single advisory is rarely a reason to walk away. Repeated safety-related advisories are much more important because they show maintenance behaviour over time.

Driving advice

How long can you drive with an MOT advisory?

There is no fixed time limit for driving with an MOT advisory because the seriousness depends on the issue. A light advisory may be monitored, but safety-related advisories should be inspected and repaired as soon as practical.

Monitor only

Light wear or early deterioration may be checked during routine servicing if there are no symptoms.

Book soon

Tyres, brakes, suspension, steering or small leaks should be checked before they worsen.

Do not delay

Brake pipe corrosion, structural corrosion, tyre damage, steering play or worsening leaks need urgent attention.

Used car buying

Why MOT advisories matter when buying a used car

MOT advisories are one of the best ways to understand a used car before you buy it. They can show upcoming repair costs, repeated faults, poor maintenance and whether the seller has been honest about the vehicle.

A clean MOT pass is reassuring, but a pass with advisories needs closer reading. You should look at the latest MOT and previous MOT history together. If advisories disappear, ask whether the work was repaired. If advisories repeat, ask why they were not fixed.

One minor advisory

Usually not a major problem on an older car if the price, history and condition are fair.

Several safety advisories

Tyres, brakes, steering, suspension and corrosion advisories should affect your buying decision.

Repeated advisories

The same issue appearing every year can suggest neglect or delayed repairs.

Advisories disappear suddenly

Ask for repair evidence. Sometimes repairs were done; sometimes the wording changed.

Buyer red flags

MOT advisory red flags when buying a used car

  • !Repeated structural corrosion: rust normally gets worse unless properly repaired.
  • !Repeated brake pipe corrosion: this can become a serious braking safety issue.
  • !Multiple suspension advisories: this may mean several worn parts, not one small repair.
  • !Tyre wear every year: this may suggest poor tracking, worn suspension or neglect.
  • !Oil leak plus coolant issues: this combination can suggest bigger engine-related risk.
  • !No receipts after advisories disappear: ask for proof that repairs were completed.
Buying advice:

A few minor advisories may be acceptable. A pattern of repeated safety-related advisories should make you slow down, inspect carefully and price the repair risk.

Insurance

Do MOT advisories affect insurance?

MOT advisories do not automatically mean your insurance is invalid because an advisory is not the same as an MOT failure. However, the vehicle still needs to be roadworthy.

If the advisory relates to tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, corrosion or leaks, the safest approach is to repair it quickly and keep receipts. If an accident happens and the vehicle is found to be poorly maintained, ignored safety-related advisories may make the situation harder to explain.

  • ✓Keep receipts after repairing advisory items.
  • ✓Prioritise safety advisories over cosmetic or minor items.
  • ✓Do not drive a vehicle that has become unsafe.
  • ✓Check your insurance policy wording if unsure about roadworthiness duties.

Selling advice

Selling a car with MOT advisories

You can sell a car with MOT advisories, but honesty matters. Advisories are normally visible in the MOT history, so buyers can check them before viewing the car.

If you have repaired the advisory, keep the invoice or receipt. This helps show the buyer that the issue was dealt with properly and can make the vehicle easier to sell.

If advisories are repaired

Show receipts, explain what was fixed and mention the repair clearly in the advert.

If advisories are not repaired

Be realistic on price because buyers may use advisory repair costs during negotiation.

If advisories repeat

Expect more questions. Repeated advisories often make buyers think maintenance has been delayed.

If the car is older

Some minor advisories are normal, but safety-related advisories still need proper explanation.

MOT history

How to read MOT advisory history properly

The most useful part of an MOT advisory is often the pattern over time. One advisory may not tell the full story, but several years of MOT history can show whether a car has been maintained properly or whether problems have been ignored.

  • !Repeated advisories: the same issue appearing every year can suggest delayed maintenance.
  • !Worsening advisories: wording changing from slight wear to corrosion, deterioration or excessive wear is a warning sign.
  • !Disappearing advisories: ask for receipts to confirm the fault was repaired.
  • !Low mileage but many advisories: this may suggest age-related corrosion, poor storage or lack of maintenance.
  • !Tyre advisories every year: this may point to tracking, suspension or alignment problems.
Best buying rule:

Do not judge one MOT in isolation. Read the car's MOT history year by year and look for repeated problems, worsening wording and missing repair evidence.

History examples

Example MOT advisory patterns and what they suggest

Tyre advisory every year

This may be normal if the car does high mileage, but it can also point to poor wheel alignment, worn suspension or cheap tyres being fitted late.

Brake pipe corrosion repeats

This is a serious warning because brake pipe corrosion can progress. Ask whether the pipe has been cleaned, treated or replaced.

Structural rust appears then disappears

Ask for photos or invoices. Rust may have been repaired properly, but you should not assume it was fixed without evidence.

Oil leak advisory appears for several years

A small leak may stay minor, but repeated oil leaks can hide larger engine, gearbox, turbo or gasket issues.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What does an MOT advisory mean?

An MOT advisory means the car passed, but the tester noticed an issue that should be monitored, maintained or repaired before it becomes worse.

Does an MOT advisory mean the car failed?

No. An advisory means the car passed. It is not a failure, but it is still worth taking seriously.

Can I ignore an MOT advisory?

You should not ignore an MOT advisory. The vehicle may have passed, but advisories can lead to future failures, repair bills or safety issues.

Are MOT advisories serious?

Some are minor, but advisories involving tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, corrosion or leaks should be treated seriously.

Should I fix MOT advisories straight away?

Safety-related advisories such as tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, corrosion and leaks should be prioritised. Less urgent items may be monitored, but should not be forgotten.

Can an advisory become a failure next year?

Yes. Wear, corrosion, leaks, tyre condition and brake issues can get worse and become MOT failures later.

Do MOT advisories show online?

Yes. MOT history normally shows previous advisories, failures and mileage records, which can help you judge how well a car has been maintained.

How long can you drive with an MOT advisory?

There is no fixed time limit. A light advisory may be monitored, but safety-related advisories should be inspected and repaired as soon as practical.

Do MOT advisories affect insurance?

They do not normally affect insurance directly, but the vehicle must remain roadworthy. Ignoring a safety-related advisory can create problems if the defect contributes to an accident.

Can MOT advisories reduce car value?

Yes. Advisories can reduce value because buyers may need to pay for repairs shortly after purchase.

How many advisories are too many?

There is no fixed number. The type, severity and repeat pattern of advisories are more important than the quantity.

What is the most serious MOT advisory?

Brake pipe corrosion, structural corrosion, tyres close to the legal limit, brake defects and steering or suspension play are among the advisories that should be treated most seriously.
