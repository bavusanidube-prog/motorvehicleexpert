Quick answer

What should you do when your car shows an OBD fault code?

An OBD fault code tells you which system the car has detected a problem with. It is a starting point, not a guaranteed parts list. For example, a P0420 code may point towards catalyst efficiency, but the real cause could be an exhaust leak, oxygen sensor issue, misfire damage, fuel mixture problem or a failing catalytic converter.

1. Read the code properly

Use a proper scanner and write down all stored, pending and permanent codes before clearing anything.

2. Check symptoms

Misfire, smoke, rough idle, poor economy, limp mode and warning lights matter as much as the code itself.

3. Diagnose before replacing parts

Many codes are caused by wiring, air leaks, fuel pressure, blocked pipes or previous faults rather than the sensor named in the code.

Understanding OBD codes

How OBD fault codes work

Most modern cars monitor engine, emissions, gearbox, braking, airbag and stability systems using control modules. When a sensor reading falls outside the expected range, or a system does not respond correctly, the module stores a diagnostic trouble code.

Codes beginning with P normally relate to powertrain faults such as engine, fuel, ignition, emissions or gearbox systems. Codes beginning with C often relate to chassis systems such as ABS or stability control. Codes beginning with B relate to body systems, and U codes usually relate to communication faults between modules.

✅ Stored code: the fault happened and has been saved.

✅ Pending code: the car has seen a possible fault but may need another drive cycle to confirm it.

✅ Permanent code: the fault has been confirmed and may only clear once the car proves the repair worked.

✅ Freeze frame data: shows engine conditions when the fault was logged.

Mechanic advice

Do not diagnose from the code alone

A fault code does not always mean the named part is faulty. A P0101 mass air flow code does not automatically mean the MAF sensor is bad. A split intake pipe, blocked air filter, EGR issue, wiring fault or air leak can trigger the same code.

A good diagnosis looks at the code, symptoms, live data, wiring, service history and common faults for that engine. This is why replacing parts blindly can get expensive quickly.

Common OBD fault codes explained

P0420

P0420 — Catalyst System Efficiency Below Threshold

Meaning: The ECU thinks the catalytic converter is not cleaning exhaust gases efficiently enough.

Symptoms: Engine light, poor fuel economy, sulphur smell, failed emissions test, sometimes no obvious symptoms.

Common causes: Worn catalytic converter, exhaust leak, faulty oxygen sensor, misfire damage, rich running, oil burning or previous engine fault.

UK repair cost guide: Around £80–£250 for diagnosis or oxygen sensor work, but a catalytic converter can be several hundred pounds depending on the car.

Can you still drive? Usually short journeys may be possible if the car drives normally, but do not ignore misfires, smoke or loss of power.

MOT impact: High risk if emissions are too high or the engine warning light is on for an emissions-related fault.

P0300 — Random or Multiple Cylinder Misfire

Meaning: The engine is misfiring on more than one cylinder or the ECU cannot isolate one exact cylinder.

Symptoms: Shaking, rough idle, flashing engine light, poor acceleration, fuel smell, juddering and possible limp mode.

Common causes: Spark plugs, ignition coils, injector fault, low compression, vacuum leak, fuel pressure fault or timing issue.

UK repair cost guide: Basic ignition repairs may be £80–£300, but injector, compression or timing faults can cost much more.

Can you still drive? Avoid driving if the engine light is flashing or the car is shaking badly because unburnt fuel can damage the catalytic converter.

MOT impact: A misfiring car can fail on emissions, warning lights, smoke or poor running condition.

Read full P0300 guide → P0171

P0171 — System Too Lean Bank 1

Meaning: The engine is running with too much air or not enough fuel on bank 1.

Symptoms: Rough idle, hesitation, poor acceleration, engine light, stalling, high fuel trims and sometimes a whistling air leak.

Common causes: Intake leak, split vacuum hose, weak fuel pump, blocked fuel filter, dirty MAF sensor, exhaust leak or faulty oxygen sensor reading.

UK repair cost guide: Small air leak repairs may be affordable, but fuel pump, injector or sensor work can increase the cost.

Can you still drive? Short careful driving may be possible if mild, but lean running can damage the engine if ignored.

MOT impact: Can cause emissions failure or engine warning light concern.

Read full P0171 guide → P0011

P0011 — Camshaft Position Timing Over Advanced

Meaning: The ECU has detected that the camshaft timing is more advanced than expected.

Symptoms: Rough running, poor starting, rattling noise, reduced power, poor economy or engine light.

Common causes: Low or dirty oil, VVT solenoid fault, blocked oil galleries, timing chain stretch, camshaft sensor fault or incorrect timing.

UK repair cost guide: Oil and solenoid checks may be moderate, but timing chain repairs can be expensive.

Can you still drive? Be careful. If there is rattling, poor running or oil pressure concern, stop and get it checked.

MOT impact: May fail if emissions are affected or the engine warning light remains on.

Read full P0011 guide → P0401

P0401 — Exhaust Gas Recirculation Flow Insufficient

Meaning: The ECU expected more EGR flow than it detected.

Symptoms: Engine light, poor acceleration, diesel smoke, rough running, limp mode or increased NOx emissions.

Common causes: Blocked EGR valve, carbon build-up, faulty EGR cooler, vacuum fault, wiring fault or blocked EGR pipework.

UK repair cost guide: Cleaning may be cheaper, while EGR valve or cooler replacement can be several hundred pounds.

Can you still drive? Usually possible if the car drives normally, but limp mode, smoke or overheating needs attention.

MOT impact: Can contribute to emissions failure or visible smoke issues, especially on diesel cars.

Read full P0401 guide → P0101

P0101 — Mass Air Flow Sensor Range or Performance

Meaning: The airflow reading is outside the range the ECU expects.

Symptoms: Hesitation, poor acceleration, rough idle, stalling, poor fuel economy or engine light.

Common causes: Dirty or faulty MAF sensor, split intake hose, blocked air filter, air leak, EGR fault or wiring issue.

UK repair cost guide: Cleaning or intake repairs can be low cost, while quality MAF replacement varies depending on the car.

Can you still drive? Often yes if the car drives normally, but poor running and stalling should be diagnosed quickly.

MOT impact: Can affect emissions if fuel mixture becomes incorrect.

Emissions and oxygen sensor codes

More emissions-related OBD codes

These codes are common on UK petrol and diesel vehicles and often relate to emissions, oxygen sensors, catalytic converters, EVAP systems or exhaust monitoring.

P0130 — Oxygen Sensor Circuit Bank 1 Sensor 1

Can be caused by sensor failure, wiring, exhaust leaks or fuelling problems.

P0135 — Oxygen Sensor Heater Circuit

Often points to oxygen sensor heater circuit, fuse, wiring or sensor faults.

P0141 — Oxygen Sensor Heater Bank 1 Sensor 2

Common after-cat oxygen sensor heater fault that can trigger the engine light.

P0442 — Small EVAP Leak Detected

Can be a fuel cap seal, EVAP hose leak, purge valve or charcoal canister issue.

P0446 — EVAP Vent Control Circuit

Often relates to EVAP vent valve, wiring, blockage or tank ventilation issues.

P0455 — Large EVAP Leak Detected

Loose fuel cap, split hose, tank leak or EVAP system fault can trigger this code.

P2002 — Diesel Particulate Filter Efficiency

DPF restriction, failed regeneration, sensor fault or exhaust pressure issue.

P2463 — DPF Soot Accumulation

Diesel soot loading is too high and may cause limp mode or failed regeneration.

P20EE — SCR NOx Catalyst Efficiency

Often seen on AdBlue/SCR diesel systems where NOx reduction is not effective.

Misfire codes

Misfire and rough running fault codes

Misfire codes should not be ignored. A severe misfire can damage the catalytic converter and make the car unsafe or unreliable.

P0301 — Cylinder 1 Misfire

Check spark plug, ignition coil, injector, compression and wiring for cylinder 1.

P0302 — Cylinder 2 Misfire

A cylinder-specific misfire that may follow the coil or plug if swapped during diagnosis.

P0303 — Cylinder 3 Misfire

Common causes include ignition, injector, compression, intake leak or valve issue.

P0304 — Cylinder 4 Misfire

Check ignition components first, then fuel delivery and mechanical compression.

P0313 — Misfire Detected With Low Fuel

Can happen when fuel level is low, but do not assume fuel is the only cause.

P0351–P0354 — Ignition Coil Circuit Codes

Usually relates to coil circuit, wiring, ECU driver or coil pack faults.

Air, fuel and sensor codes

Sensor and fuel mixture fault codes

These codes often appear with poor acceleration, bad fuel economy, rough idle, hesitation or limp mode.

P0113 — Intake Air Temperature Sensor High

May be caused by sensor fault, wiring issue or intake air temperature reading error.

P0128 — Coolant Thermostat Below Temperature

Often caused by a thermostat stuck open, coolant temperature sensor issue or slow warm-up.

P0172 — System Too Rich Bank 1

Too much fuel or not enough air. Check injectors, MAF, fuel pressure and oxygen sensors.

P0191 — Fuel Rail Pressure Sensor Range

Can involve fuel pressure sensor, pump, wiring, filter restriction or rail pressure control.

P0299 — Turbo Underboost

Common causes include boost leak, turbo actuator fault, vacuum fault, EGR or DPF restriction.

P0234 — Turbo Overboost

May be caused by sticking turbo vanes, actuator issue, boost control fault or sensor reading problem.

P0340 — Camshaft Position Sensor Circuit

Can affect starting, timing control and engine running.

P0335 — Crankshaft Position Sensor Circuit

Common no-start, cutting out or intermittent stalling code.

P0562 — System Voltage Low

Often linked to battery, alternator, charging system, wiring or poor earth connection.

MOT implications

Will an OBD fault code fail an MOT?

The MOT tester does not normally fail a car just because a scan tool shows a stored OBD code. The problem is what the code represents. If the fault causes an illuminated engine warning light, poor emissions, smoke, braking faults, ABS warning lights, airbag warning lights or unsafe running, it can lead to an MOT failure.

⚠️ High MOT risk: engine light with emissions fault, ABS light, airbag light, visible smoke, poor braking or serious misfire.

⚠️ Medium risk: oxygen sensor, catalyst, EGR, DPF, fuel mixture or boost faults.

✅ Lower risk: historic codes where the fault is repaired and no warning light or symptoms remain.

Real experience

Real mechanic-style advice

In real diagnostics, the cheapest repair is often the correct test, not the cheapest part. A car with P0171 may need a smoke test for air leaks before any sensor is replaced. A car with P0420 may need misfire history checked before fitting a new catalytic converter. A car with P0299 may need boost pipes checked before blaming the turbo.

The best approach is to diagnose the system in order: code scan, visual checks, live data, wiring checks, pressure or smoke testing, then part replacement only when the evidence supports it.

UK repair cost guide

Typical UK repair costs for fault code problems

Prices vary by vehicle, engine access, parts quality and labour rate, but these rough ranges help you understand what may be involved.

Diagnostic scan

Usually around £40–£120 depending on garage and depth of testing.

Smoke test or leak test

Often used for P0171, boost leaks and vacuum leaks. Usually cheaper than guessing parts.

Ignition coil or spark plug repair

Common for misfire codes. Costs depend on engine layout and parts quality.

Oxygen sensor replacement

Common with emissions codes, but should be tested before replacement.

EGR repair or replacement

Can range from cleaning to full valve or cooler replacement on diesel vehicles.

Catalytic converter replacement

Can be expensive, especially on vehicles where the cat is built into the manifold.

MAF sensor or intake repair

Often linked to P0101, hesitation and fuel mixture issues.

Timing chain or VVT repair

Can be costly if P0011 is caused by chain stretch or oil control problems.

DPF or turbo diagnosis

Requires careful testing because DPF, EGR and boost faults can trigger each other.

Best mechanic-style checks

Checks to do before replacing parts

✅ Check battery voltage and charging system.

✅ Inspect intake pipes for splits or loose clips.

✅ Check for exhaust leaks before oxygen sensor diagnosis.

✅ Look at live data, not just stored codes.

✅ Check fuel trims for lean or rich running.

✅ Check service history, oil level and oil condition.

✅ Do not clear codes before recording freeze frame data.

✅ Road test after repair and recheck pending codes.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently Asked Questions About OBD Fault Codes

Find answers to common questions about fault codes, engine warning lights, diagnostic scans, safe driving advice and MOT implications for UK drivers.

What does an OBD fault code mean?

An OBD fault code is a diagnostic trouble code stored by the car when the engine computer or another control module detects a fault. It helps identify the affected system, but it does not always confirm the exact failed part.

Should I replace the part named in the code?

Not straight away. A code can point towards a sensor or system, but wiring faults, air leaks, low voltage, blocked pipes, poor servicing or another fault can trigger the same code. Diagnosis should come before parts replacement.

Can I drive with a fault code?

It depends on the code and symptoms. A steady engine light with no running problem may allow short careful driving, but flashing engine lights, misfires, overheating, smoke, brake warnings or loss of power should be treated as urgent.

Can a fault code cause an MOT failure?

The code itself is not normally what fails the car. The fault behind the code can cause an MOT failure if it affects emissions, warning lights, braking, ABS, airbag systems, visible smoke or vehicle safety.

Are fault code readers always accurate?

Basic code readers are useful, but they may only show engine codes and may miss ABS, airbag, gearbox or manufacturer-specific faults. A professional diagnostic scan can give more detail, including live data and freeze frame information.

Should I clear the code before fixing the fault?

It is better to record the codes and freeze frame data first. Clearing codes too early can remove useful diagnostic evidence and make the fault harder to trace if it is intermittent.
