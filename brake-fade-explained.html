Brake Fade Explained

Brake fade happens when your braking system becomes too hot and loses stopping power. It can make the brake pedal feel less effective, increase stopping distances and become dangerous during heavy braking, long descents, towing, spirited driving or repeated stop-start use.

This complete guide explains what brake fade is, why heat affects brake pads, discs and brake fluid, the main types of brake fade, warning signs to watch for, MOT relevance, inspection checks and how to prevent brake fade before it becomes a serious safety problem.

Understanding brake fade

Why Do Brakes Lose Stopping Power?

Every time you press the brake pedal, the vehicle's kinetic energy is converted into heat through friction between the brake pads and brake discs. Under normal driving conditions this heat is released into the surrounding air. Brake fade occurs when heat builds faster than the braking system can dissipate it.

As temperatures continue to rise, the friction material on the brake pads becomes less effective, brake discs retain more heat and old brake fluid may begin to boil inside the hydraulic system. These changes reduce braking efficiency and increase stopping distances until the brakes cool.

Workshop Explanation

Brake fade is not normally caused by a single failed component. Instead, it is the result of excessive heat overwhelming the braking system's ability to transfer and dissipate energy safely.

Some driving conditions generate far more heat than others. Understanding when brake fade is most likely helps drivers adapt their driving style before braking performance begins to deteriorate.

Everyday Driving

Four types

The Four Main Types Of Brake Fade

Although drivers often describe every loss of braking performance as "brake fade", mechanics recognise several different forms of brake fade. Each develops for a different reason and requires a different solution.

Friction Fade

Brake pad friction material overheats and produces less stopping force.

Brake Fluid Fade

Brake fluid boils inside the hydraulic system, producing vapour that compresses under pedal pressure.

Green Fade

Occurs when brand-new brake pads are not fully bedded in and gases reduce friction.

Mechanical Fade

Brake components become so hot that mechanical efficiency and braking force begin to decrease.

Brake science

How Heat Causes Brake Fade

Every time you press the brake pedal, your vehicle's kinetic energy must be converted into another form of energy before the car can slow down. The braking system achieves this by converting movement into heat through friction between the brake pads and brake discs. Under normal driving conditions this heat is quickly released into the surrounding air, allowing the brakes to continue operating efficiently.

Brake fade begins when heat is produced faster than the braking system can dissipate it. As temperatures continue to rise, brake pads lose friction, brake discs retain more heat and brake fluid can eventually boil inside the hydraulic system. The result is reduced braking performance and noticeably longer stopping distances.

Heat is the real enemy.

Brake fade is almost always a heat-management problem rather than a single failed component. The hotter the braking system becomes, the more likely braking performance will begin to decrease.

Type 1

Friction Fade

Friction fade is the most common type of brake fade experienced by everyday drivers. It occurs when the brake pad friction material becomes so hot that it can no longer generate the same level of grip against the brake disc. Although the driver presses the brake pedal with the same force, less braking energy is produced.

This type of brake fade is commonly seen during long downhill descents, repeated emergency braking, spirited driving or when towing heavy loads. Lower-quality brake pads generally reach friction fade much sooner than premium braking materials.

Common Causes

  • ✓Repeated heavy braking
  • ✓Overheated brake pads
  • ✓Cheap friction material
  • ✓Mountain driving

Driver Symptoms

  • ✓Longer stopping distance
  • ✓Burning smell
  • ✓Reduced braking bite
  • ✓Brakes improve after cooling
Type 2

Brake Fluid Fade

Brake fluid fade occurs when brake fluid becomes so hot that it begins to boil inside the hydraulic braking system. Unlike brake fluid, vapour bubbles can be compressed. Instead of transmitting hydraulic pressure directly to the brake calipers, part of the pedal movement compresses the vapour, making the brake pedal feel soft or spongy.

Older brake fluid absorbs moisture over time, lowering its boiling point and making brake fluid fade much more likely during heavy braking. This is why regular brake fluid replacement is such an important preventative maintenance task.

Soft Brake Pedal

Classic Symptom

The brake pedal feels soft because vapour bubbles compress inside the hydraulic system.

Old Brake Fluid

Major Cause

Moisture contamination dramatically lowers brake fluid boiling point.

Hydraulic Pressure Loss

Reduced Braking

Less hydraulic pressure reaches the brake calipers during braking.

Type 3

Green Fade

Green fade occurs almost exclusively with brand-new brake pads. During the bedding-in process, binding resins within the friction material release gases as temperatures rise. These gases create a thin layer between the brake pad and brake disc, temporarily reducing friction until the pads are fully bedded in.

Usually Affects

Recently fitted brake pads that have not yet completed the bedding-in process.

Solution

Follow the manufacturer's recommended bedding-in procedure and avoid repeated emergency stops immediately after installation.

Type 4

Mechanical Brake Fade

Mechanical fade occurs when extreme temperatures affect the physical braking components themselves. Brake discs may expand significantly, brake pad backing plates can distort and brake calipers may struggle to maintain consistent clamping force.

Although less common than friction or brake fluid fade on everyday road cars, mechanical fade becomes increasingly important during repeated high-speed braking, motorsport and heavy commercial vehicle use.

Mechanical fade usually indicates extremely high brake temperatures.

Repeated overheating shortens the life of brake pads, brake discs, calipers, seals and brake fluid, often resulting in expensive repairs.

Modern braking

Why Modern Braking Systems Resist Brake Fade Better

Modern vehicles are designed to manage braking heat far more effectively than older systems. Improved brake materials, larger ventilated discs, advanced brake fluids and electronic braking systems all help reduce the likelihood of brake fade during normal road driving.

Ventilated Brake Discs

Internal cooling vanes increase airflow and remove heat more efficiently.

Improved Brake Pads

Modern friction materials maintain braking performance at much higher temperatures.

Higher Quality Brake Fluid

Modern DOT 4 and DOT 5.1 fluids have higher boiling points than older specifications.

Larger Brake Calipers

More even pressure distribution improves braking efficiency and heat control.

ABS & Electronic Stability Systems

Electronic braking systems help maximise braking performance under demanding conditions.

Better Cooling

Modern wheel and brake designs improve airflow, reducing heat build-up during everyday driving.

Recognising the problem

Common Brake Fade Symptoms

Brake fade rarely happens without warning. In most cases the braking system gives several clear signs that temperatures are becoming excessive before braking performance reduces significantly. Learning to recognise these symptoms early allows the driver to reduce speed safely, allow the brakes to cool and prevent further damage to brake pads, brake discs, brake fluid and hydraulic components.

Although many of these symptoms disappear once the brakes cool down, repeated brake fade should never be ignored because it often indicates excessive operating temperatures, worn braking components or poor maintenance.

Safety Warning

If your vehicle suddenly takes longer to stop, the brake pedal becomes soft, you smell burning brakes or smoke is visible from a wheel, reduce speed immediately, avoid further heavy braking and allow the braking system to cool before continuing your journey.

If brake fade occurs more than once, the entire braking system should be inspected rather than simply allowing the brakes to cool and continuing to drive.

Workshop diagnosis

How Mechanics Diagnose Brake Fade

Brake fade is often temporary, so the vehicle may feel normal again by the time it reaches a garage. A proper inspection therefore looks for evidence of overheating, poor hydraulic condition, worn friction material and faults that allow heat to build up repeatedly.

A professional diagnosis should not focus on one part only. Brake pads, brake discs, brake fluid, calipers, hoses, warning lights and the road test all matter because brake fade can be caused by a combination of heat, maintenance condition and driving load.

Inspection principle:

The aim is to answer three questions: did the brakes overheat, why did they overheat, and has any permanent damage been caused?

Workshop Brake Fade Inspection Checklist

1. Road Test

Confirm symptom

A controlled road test checks pedal feel, stopping distance, pulling, vibration, brake noise and whether the symptoms return as temperatures rise.

2. Pedal Feel

Hydraulic clue

A soft, long or sinking pedal can point toward overheated brake fluid, air in the system or hydraulic pressure loss.

3. Brake Pads

Friction check

Pads should be checked for glazing, cracking, uneven wear, low thickness, contamination and heat damage.

4. Brake Discs

Heat evidence

Discs should be inspected for blue heat marks, scoring, cracks, surface damage, thickness variation and excessive wear.

5. Brake Fluid

Boiling risk

Fluid colour, level, moisture content and service history should be checked, especially after soft pedal complaints.

6. Brake Calipers

Drag risk

A sticking caliper can keep a pad touching the disc, creating constant heat and repeated brake fade symptoms.

7. Brake Hoses

Flow issue

Flexible hoses should be inspected for cracking, swelling, internal restriction and fluid leaks.

8. ABS & Warning Lights

System check

Brake, ABS or stability control warning lights should be scanned and diagnosed before the vehicle is returned to use.

  • ✓Dark or moisture-contaminated brake fluid

Do not diagnose brake fade from symptoms alone.

A burning smell or long stopping distance may suggest brake fade, but similar symptoms can also come from seized calipers, worn pads, contaminated discs, hydraulic leaks or old brake fluid. A full inspection is the safest approach.

Root causes

Why Brake Fade Happens

Brake fade is rarely caused by a single fault. In most cases it develops because excessive heat combines with worn braking components, poor maintenance or demanding driving conditions. Understanding the root cause is essential because simply replacing one component may not prevent the problem from returning.

Professional technicians therefore investigate the complete braking system rather than focusing on one part. Brake pads, brake discs, brake fluid, calipers, driving conditions and vehicle load all influence how quickly brake temperatures rise.

Workshop Principle

The braking system should always be treated as one complete system. Replacing only the overheated component without correcting the underlying cause often results in repeated brake fade.

If you suspect excessive brake temperatures rather than worn components alone, see Brake Overheating Explained for a complete diagnosis guide.

Driving safety

Can You Drive With Brake Fade?

You should treat brake fade as a serious warning that the braking system has become too hot or is not coping with the driving conditions. If the brakes return to normal after cooling, the vehicle may appear fine again, but repeated brake fade means the system needs inspection.

The safest decision depends on how severe the symptoms are. Mild fade after a long descent is different from smoke, a soft pedal, a warning light or a sudden loss of stopping power.

Mild Fade

Slow down

Reduce speed, avoid further heavy braking, use a lower gear where safe and allow the brakes to cool.

Repeated Fade

Book inspection

If brake fade happens more than once, arrange a full braking system inspection before relying on the vehicle.

Severe Fade

Stop safely

If stopping power suddenly drops, pull over safely and do not continue normal driving until the brakes cool and are checked.

Soft Brake Pedal

Hydraulic risk

A soft pedal may suggest brake fluid fade or air in the hydraulic system and should be inspected urgently.

Smoke Or Burning

Do not ignore

Smoke or a strong burning smell means the braking system is operating at dangerous temperatures.

Warning Light

Diagnose

A brake or ABS warning light should be diagnosed before further regular driving.

Stop driving and arrange inspection if:

The brake pedal sinks, smoke appears, the brake warning light comes on, stopping distances increase dramatically, or the vehicle no longer slows down normally.

MOT advice

Will Brake Fade Fail An MOT?

Brake fade itself may not be present during an MOT test because it often appears only when the brakes are extremely hot. However, the faults that cause brake fade can lead to MOT failure if they affect braking efficiency, balance, component condition or warning lights.

Poor Brake Efficiency

Failure risk

If braking force is below requirement during the roller brake test, the vehicle can fail.

Brake Imbalance

Failure risk

Uneven braking between wheels on the same axle can fail if outside allowed limits.

Brake Fluid Leak

Serious fault

Visible brake fluid leaks are safety-critical and can cause MOT failure.

Worn Pads Or Discs

Inspection item

Excessively worn, damaged or insecure braking components can fail inspection.

Warning Lights

Dashboard issue

ABS, ESC or brake system warning lights may lead to failure depending on the fault.

Seized Caliper

Heat source

A binding brake can cause poor efficiency, imbalance and repeated overheating.

Prevention

Brake fade prevention is about controlling heat. The right driving technique, good maintenance and quality braking components all help keep brake temperatures within a safe working range.

Best technique

Money-saving advice

How To Avoid Expensive Brake Fade Repairs

The cheapest way to deal with brake fade is to prevent repeated overheating. Once pads are glazed, discs are heat damaged or brake fluid has boiled, repair costs rise quickly.

Service Brake Fluid

Regular brake fluid replacement is cheaper than ABS, caliper or master cylinder repairs.

Replace Pads Early

Worn pads overheat faster and can damage brake discs.

Fix Binding Brakes

A sticking caliper can destroy pads and discs quickly if ignored.

Do Not Buy Cheapest Pads

Low-quality pads may fade earlier and wear discs faster.

Use Correct Driving Technique

Engine braking and controlled braking reduce heat on long descents.

Inspect After Overheating

Checking the system early can prevent a small issue becoming a full brake overhaul.

Best value rule:

Brake fade repairs become expensive when overheating is repeated. Early inspection, fresh fluid and good-quality brake components are usually cheaper than replacing damaged pads, discs, calipers and hydraulic parts later.

Used car buying

Brake Fade Checks When Buying A Used Car

Brake fade is not usually something you can see by looking at a parked vehicle, but previous overheating often leaves clear evidence elsewhere in the braking system. A careful inspection before buying can reveal whether the brakes have been maintained correctly or repeatedly subjected to excessive heat.

During a test drive, pay close attention to brake pedal feel, stopping performance, steering stability and any burning smells after repeated braking. Combined with a visual inspection and service history, these checks provide an excellent indication of the overall condition of the braking system.

Frequently asked questions

Brake Fade Explained FAQs

What is brake fade?

Brake fade is a temporary reduction in braking performance caused by excessive heat. It can make the vehicle take longer to stop and may make the pedal feel less effective.

What causes brake fade?

Brake fade is usually caused by repeated heavy braking, long downhill driving, towing, overheated pads and discs, old brake fluid, seized calipers or poor driving technique.

Is brake fade dangerous?

Yes. Brake fade is dangerous because it reduces stopping power and increases stopping distances, especially during emergency braking.

Can brake fluid cause brake fade?

Yes. Old or moisture-contaminated brake fluid can boil when overheated, creating vapour in the hydraulic system and causing a soft or spongy brake pedal.

Does brake fade go away?

Brake fade often improves once the brakes cool down, but repeated overheating can permanently damage pads, discs, brake fluid, calipers and hydraulic seals.

Can worn brake pads cause brake fade?

Yes. Thin, glazed or poor-quality brake pads can overheat more quickly and lose friction under repeated braking.

Can ABS prevent brake fade?

No. ABS can help prevent wheel lock-up, but it cannot stop brake pads, discs or brake fluid from overheating.

Will brake fade fail an MOT?

Brake fade itself may not appear during an MOT, but the causes of brake fade — such as poor brake efficiency, imbalance, fluid leaks, worn components or warning lights — can result in MOT failure.

How do you prevent brake fade?

Use engine braking on descents, avoid riding the brakes, maintain brake fluid, fit quality pads and discs, inspect calipers and allow brakes to cool after heavy use.

Should I replace brakes after brake fade?

Not always, but the system should be inspected. Replacement may be needed if pads are glazed, discs are heat damaged, brake fluid has boiled or a caliper is sticking.
