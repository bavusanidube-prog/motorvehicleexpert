Quick answer

Why Won’t My Automatic Gearbox Change Gear?

An automatic gearbox can refuse to change gear because the control system does not know when to shift, cannot command the shift correctly, or the hydraulic and mechanical parts inside the transmission cannot carry it out.

Common causes include transmission speed-sensor faults, selector or range-sensor problems, sticking shift solenoids, valve-body wear, low or incorrect transmission fluid, electrical or control-module faults, limp mode and internal clutch or mechanical damage.

Temperature-dependent faults are especially useful diagnostically. A gearbox may change normally when cold but stop shifting correctly once hot because hydraulic leakage increases, a solenoid becomes heat-sensitive or the control module detects an abnormal condition.

The opposite can happen too: some transmissions shift poorly from cold because of fluid condition, slow hydraulic response or sticking valves that improve after warming.

Workshop diagnostic process

How Should an Automatic Gearbox That Won’t Change Gear Be Diagnosed?

Diagnosis should identify exactly which shift is missing, determine whether the gearbox is being deliberately restricted by fail-safe mode, and separate incorrect sensor or electrical inputs from hydraulic and internal transmission faults.

1. Define the Exact No-Shift Symptom

Record whether the gearbox will not upshift, will not downshift, stays in one gear or ignores manual requests.

2. Identify the Gearbox Type

Confirm conventional automatic, DCT, CVT or another transmission before applying the diagnostic strategy.

3. Test From Cold

Record first-shift behaviour before the transmission has warmed.

4. Repeat Fully Hot

Reproduce the fault at the temperature where the driver normally experiences it.

5. Check Manual Mode

Compare automatic shift behaviour with direct driver-requested upshifts and downshifts.

6. Check for Limp Mode

Determine whether the fixed or limited gear operation is a deliberate protective strategy.

7. Inspect Fluid & Leaks

Check fluid level, condition, specification and external leakage using the correct manufacturer procedure.

8. Check Battery & Charging Voltage

Confirm stable supply voltage before chasing electronic gearbox faults.

9. Scan All Relevant Modules

Read transmission, engine and network faults before clearing any stored information.

10. Check Speed-Sensor Data

Compare input speed, output speed and calculated ratio for plausibility.

11. Check Selector / Range Data

Confirm the TCM sees the same range the driver has selected.

12. Review Requested vs Actual Gear

Establish whether the TCM is asking for the missing shift and whether the transmission actually achieves it.

13. Test Solenoid Control

Check electrical command, circuit integrity and hydraulic response where appropriate.

14. Assess Valve-Body / Pressure Operation

Confirm whether hydraulic pressure is being routed correctly to the required clutch circuit.

15. Assess Internal Gearbox Condition

Only after control and hydraulic faults are excluded should internal clutches, seals or mechanical components be condemned.

The diagnosis should explain why the shift is missing

A strong conclusion should identify whether the TCM never requested the shift, the command failed electrically, hydraulic pressure did not reach the clutch, or the internal component could not hold the requested ratio.

Safety decision

Is It Safe to Drive If the Automatic Gearbox Won’t Change Gear?

It depends on which gear is affected and how the vehicle behaves. A gearbox that simply delays one shift is very different from one that is stuck in a single ratio, repeatedly drops into Neutral, slips badly or refuses to select a safe gear for the road conditions.

If the transmission is holding an unsuitable gear, acceleration and engine braking can become unpredictable. That can make overtaking, joining traffic and low-speed manoeuvring unsafe even if the car still moves.

UK MOT rules

Will an Automatic Gearbox That Won’t Change Gear Fail an MOT?

An automatic gearbox refusing to upshift or downshift is not itself tested as a dedicated internal gearbox-performance item during the MOT. The MOT does not road-test shift logic, solenoid timing, valve-body function or clutch application in the same way a transmission specialist would.

However, related driveline, mounting and fluid-leak defects can still fail where they fall within the MOT inspection.

Repair cost depends on whether the missing shift is caused by a sensor, selector, solenoid, valve-body problem, electrical fault or genuine internal gearbox damage. The same symptom can therefore range from a relatively modest repair to a complete transmission rebuild.

Does a Gearbox That Won’t Change Gear Need Replacing?

Not necessarily. A transmission can refuse shifts because of a sensor, wiring, solenoid or hydraulic-control problem while the major internal components remain serviceable.

Repair Existing Gearbox

Control Fault Proven
Usually Preferred

Sensors, selectors, wiring, solenoids and some valve-body faults can often be repaired without replacing the transmission.

Rebuild / Recondition

Internal Wear Proven
Specialist Route

Internal clutch, seal or geartrain faults may justify a controlled rebuild where the main unit is reusable.

Replace Gearbox

Severe / Widespread Damage
High Cost

Replacement becomes more attractive where internal damage is extensive or repair cost exceeds a suitable replacement option.

A replacement transmission will not fix a faulty selector, damaged wiring, unstable voltage or unresolved control-module input problem.

Used car buying

Treat any missing shift as an unknown repair bill until the cause has been diagnosed. A faulty sensor or solenoid may be relatively manageable, but internal clutch or mechanical damage can cost thousands of pounds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Automatic Gearbox Won’t Change Gear FAQs

Why won’t my automatic gearbox change gear?

Common causes include low transmission fluid, speed-sensor faults, selector or range-sensor problems, sticking shift solenoids, valve-body wear, electrical faults, gearbox control-module problems, limp mode and internal clutch or mechanical damage.

Why won’t my automatic gearbox shift up?

A gearbox that refuses to upshift may be receiving incorrect speed or load information, have a shift-solenoid or valve-body fault, be operating in limp mode or have an internal clutch problem affecting the next ratio.

Why won’t my automatic gearbox shift down?

No-downshift faults can involve speed or throttle information, selector inputs, solenoid control, hydraulic faults or fail-safe operation that keeps the gearbox in a higher ratio.

What does it mean if the gearbox changes manually but not automatically?

If manual requests work but normal automatic shifts do not, the gearbox may still be mechanically capable of changing ratio. Sensor information, automatic shift logic, adaptation or control faults then become more important.

Why does my gearbox change gear when cold but not when hot?

Hot-only no-shift faults can involve hydraulic leakage, heat-sensitive solenoids, valve-body wear or protective strategies triggered once transmission temperature rises.

Why does my automatic gearbox stay in one gear?

Fixed-ratio operation often indicates limp or fail-safe mode, but speed-sensor faults, solenoid problems, valve-body faults and internal mechanical problems can create the same symptom.

Can limp mode stop an automatic gearbox changing gear?

Yes. The gearbox control module can deliberately restrict available gears after detecting a serious electrical, hydraulic, pressure or ratio fault.

Can low automatic gearbox fluid stop it changing gear?

Yes. Low fluid can reduce hydraulic pressure and prevent internal clutches from applying or releasing correctly, leading to missed shifts, slipping or fixed-ratio operation.

Can a speed sensor stop an automatic gearbox changing gear?

Yes. Input and output speed information is used for shift scheduling and ratio verification. Incorrect or missing speed data can cause missed shifts or limp mode.

Can a selector or range sensor stop the gearbox changing gear?

Yes. If the transmission control module receives incorrect selector information, it may restrict shifts, ignore driver requests or enter fail-safe operation.

Can a faulty shift solenoid stop the gearbox changing gear?

Yes. A sticking or electrically faulty shift solenoid can prevent hydraulic pressure being routed to the clutch or holding element needed for the next ratio.

Can valve-body wear cause missing gears?

Yes. Worn bores or sticking valves can leak or misroute hydraulic pressure and prevent the transmission from applying a particular clutch or completing certain shifts.

Can an electrical fault stop an automatic gearbox shifting?

Yes. Low voltage, damaged wiring, poor grounds, connector corrosion and communication faults can affect sensors, solenoids and the gearbox control module.

Is it safe to drive if the automatic gearbox won’t change gear?

A single occasional delayed shift may allow cautious use while arranging diagnosis, but fixed-ratio operation, severe slipping, repeated Neutral, overheating or loss of drive should be treated as reasons to limit driving or arrange recovery.

Will an automatic gearbox that won’t change gear fail an MOT?

Refusing to upshift or downshift is not itself tested as a standalone internal gearbox-performance item. However, serious transmission shaft, joint, bearing, mounting or excessive fluid-leak defects can fail the MOT.

Does a gearbox that won’t change gear need replacing?

Not necessarily. Sensor, selector, wiring, solenoid and some valve-body faults can often be repaired without replacing the complete gearbox. Replacement becomes more likely when severe internal damage is proven.

How much does it cost to fix an automatic gearbox that won’t change gear in the UK?

Diagnosis may cost around £50–£120+, speed-sensor work around £100–£350+, selector faults around £120–£400+, solenoid repairs around £200–£700+ and valve-body repairs around £500–£1,500+. Internal gearbox rebuilds can exceed £1,200–£3,500.

What should a garage check before replacing a no-shift gearbox?

Diagnosis should identify the exact missing shift, compare cold and hot behaviour, check manual mode and limp mode, inspect fluid and leaks, verify battery voltage, scan relevant modules, review speed and selector data, compare requested and actual gear and test solenoid and hydraulic operation before internal components are condemned.
