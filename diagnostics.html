Quick Answer

How should you diagnose a car fault?

Start with the symptom rather than a suspected component. Establish exactly what the car is doing, when the problem happens and whether anything else changes at the same time. A warning light, unusual noise, vibration, smell, leak, temperature change or loss of power can all help narrow down which system needs testing.

Step 1

Identify the symptom

Start with what you can prove: the engine will not start, the car overheats, a warning light is on, the steering shakes, acceleration is weak or there is an unusual smell or noise.

Step 2

Find the pattern

Note whether the fault appears when cold, hot, at idle, under acceleration, while braking, when turning, on a hill, in traffic or at motorway speed. The pattern can remove many unlikely causes.

Step 3

Confirm before replacing

Use inspection, measurements, fault-code information and system testing to prove the cause. A fault code or common symptom should guide diagnosis, not automatically decide which part to replace.

Mechanic diagnostic principle:

Diagnose the system before condemning the component. For example, a sensor fault code can be caused by the sensor itself, wiring, poor voltage supply, an air leak or a mechanical condition that causes the sensor reading to move outside its expected range.

Diagnostic Starting Point

Where should you start when diagnosing a car fault?

Start with the first symptom you can describe accurately. Do not begin by guessing which component has failed. Warning lights, noises, smells, leaks, vibration, poor starting, overheating and loss of power all point towards different systems and different diagnostic tests.

The most useful question is not simply “What part is faulty?” but “Under what conditions does the fault happen?” A problem that appears only when cold can have a different cause from one that appears only after the engine is hot. A vibration under braking points in a different direction from a vibration at idle or motorway speed.

Dashboard Warning Lights

Diagnose dashboard warning lights

A dashboard warning light tells you that the vehicle has detected a condition that needs attention, but the light itself does not always identify the failed component. Start by identifying the symbol and colour, then consider how the vehicle is behaving and whether other warnings are present.

Red

Red warning lights

Red warnings can indicate conditions such as low oil pressure, braking faults or serious overheating. These should be treated as potentially urgent until the cause is understood.

Priority: High
Amber

Amber warning lights

Amber warnings commonly indicate a system fault or a condition that requires diagnosis, such as an engine, ABS, DPF, EPC or traction-control problem.

Priority: Diagnose
Information

Green and blue indicators

Green and blue dashboard symbols are often status indicators rather than faults, although every symbol should still be identified correctly before making an assumption.

Priority: Identify
Do not ignore a warning light because the car still drives normally

ABS, airbag, emissions, charging and other electronically monitored systems can store a fault before the driver notices a major change in vehicle behaviour. The absence of an obvious driving symptom does not prove the system is operating correctly.

Cooling System Diagnosis

Diagnose overheating and coolant-loss problems

Cooling faults should be taken seriously because a small leak or circulation problem can become major engine damage if the vehicle continues to overheat. Start with coolant level and obvious leakage, then consider fan operation, coolant circulation, thermostat operation, radiator condition and possible internal engine faults.

Temperature

Engine temperature rising

Establish whether overheating occurs at idle, in traffic, at motorway speed, under load or only with the air conditioning switched on.

Coolant

Coolant level dropping

Check for external leaks, dried coolant staining, pressure loss and evidence that coolant could be entering the engine, exhaust or cabin heater system.

Circulation

Cooling system not circulating correctly

Thermostat faults, water-pump problems, airlocks, radiator restriction and fan faults can all disturb normal engine-temperature control.

Do not continue driving an engine that is seriously overheating

Continued overheating can distort the cylinder head, damage the head gasket, reduce lubrication and cause severe engine damage. Stop safely if the temperature enters the danger zone, a red temperature warning appears, steam is present or coolant is being lost rapidly.

Starting & Charging Diagnosis

Diagnose no-start, battery and charging faults

Starting problems become much easier to diagnose when you first separate the symptom into no crank, slow crank, normal crank but no start or starts but the battery keeps going flat. Each condition points towards a different group of tests.

1. No crank

Check battery state, battery connections, starter circuit, immobiliser operation and whether the starter receives a command.

2. Slow crank

A weak battery, poor connection, starter fault or excessive mechanical resistance can reduce cranking speed.

3. Cranks but will not start

Move beyond the starter system and check fuel, ignition, engine-speed signals, immobiliser status, compression and engine-management information.

4. Battery keeps going flat

Test battery health and charging output, then consider driving pattern and abnormal electrical drain while the vehicle is parked.

New battery does not automatically mean the charging system is healthy

A replacement battery can temporarily hide an alternator or parasitic-drain problem. If a new battery goes flat again, or the battery warning light remains illuminated, test the charging system rather than repeatedly replacing the battery.

Running & Acceleration Diagnosis

Diagnose hesitation, jerking, misfires and loss of power

Running faults should be diagnosed by establishing exactly when engine performance changes. A car that hesitates when the throttle is first pressed can have a different problem from one that loses power only uphill, stutters under heavy acceleration or revs normally without increasing road speed.

Engine-management faults are common causes of poor running, but not every acceleration complaint originates inside the engine. Clutch slip, transmission faults and drivetrain problems can also make a healthy engine feel as though it has lost power.

Engine

Engine speed changes with the symptom

Misfires, fuel-delivery faults, intake problems, ignition faults, sensor errors, turbo problems and exhaust restrictions can all reduce engine output.

Clutch

Revs rise but road speed does not

If engine speed rises strongly without matching vehicle acceleration, investigate clutch or transmission slip rather than assuming the engine itself lacks power.

Driving load

Fault appears only under load

Uphill driving, high gears and stronger acceleration increase engine and drivetrain load, which can expose faults that are not obvious during gentle driving.

Separate engine power loss from drivetrain slip

Watch the rev counter when the fault happens. If the engine struggles to gain rpm, investigate engine output. If rpm rises quickly but vehicle speed does not follow, clutch or transmission slip becomes much more likely.

Vibration, Brakes & Steering

Diagnose shaking, steering pull and wheel-related symptoms

Vibration diagnosis depends heavily on when the shaking occurs. A vibration at idle points towards different systems from a steering-wheel shake at 60 mph, a judder only when braking or a vibration that appears under acceleration.

Before replacing suspension or steering components, establish whether the symptom follows engine speed, road speed, brake application, steering input or acceleration load.

Engine speed

Shakes while stationary

If the vehicle vibrates while parked, concentrate on engine operation, mounts and rotating accessories before blaming wheels or tyres.

Road speed

Vibration increases with speed

Wheels, tyres, balancing, bearings, driveshafts and suspension become more likely when vibration follows vehicle speed rather than engine rpm.

Braking

Vibration appears during braking

Brake-disc variation, hub condition, wheel bearings, suspension movement and braking imbalance should be considered when the symptom begins with brake application.

Smells & Smoke Diagnosis

Diagnose burning smells, fuel smells and exhaust smoke

Smell and smoke are valuable diagnostic clues because the type of material being heated, burned or leaked often produces a recognisable pattern. The location of the smell, when it occurs and whether any fluid level is changing are just as important as the smell itself.

Burning

Burning smell

Hot brakes, clutch friction, leaking engine oil, auxiliary belts and electrical components can all produce different burning smells.

Fuel

Petrol or fuel smell

Fuel smells deserve immediate attention because leaks and vapour faults can present a fire risk as well as causing poor engine operation.

Exhaust

Visible exhaust smoke

White, blue, black and grey smoke can point towards coolant, oil, excessive fuel or other combustion and exhaust-system conditions.

Stop and investigate strong fuel smells, heavy smoke or visible fire

Do not continue normal driving when there is a strong petrol smell, electrical burning, heavy smoke from the engine bay or another sign of an active fire risk. Stop safely and arrange appropriate inspection or recovery.

Diagnose heater, air-conditioning and electrical faults

Not every diagnostic problem affects the engine or drivetrain. Heating, air conditioning, windows, remote locking and other electrical systems can fail independently while the vehicle otherwise drives normally.

Air conditioning

AC is not cooling

Refrigerant level, pressure, compressor operation, electrical control and condenser airflow can all affect cooling performance.

Heating

Heater stays cold

Low coolant, thermostat operation, airlocks, heater matrix flow, blend-door faults and blower problems can affect cabin heating.

Electrical

One electrical function stops working

Check the relevant fuse, switch, wiring, motor or actuator before assuming that a major control module has failed.

Professional Diagnostic Workflow

A mechanic-style process for diagnosing vehicle faults

Good diagnosis follows a sequence. The aim is to move from a broad symptom towards a confirmed cause without replacing parts unnecessarily. The exact tests will vary between systems, but the underlying process stays similar.

1. Confirm the symptom

Verify what the vehicle is actually doing rather than diagnosing from a description alone. Establish whether the fault is present now, intermittent or only appears under particular driving conditions.

2. Establish the conditions

Note engine temperature, road speed, engine speed, throttle load, gear, braking, steering input and whether the problem appears when cold, hot, idling or moving.

3. Check obvious safety issues

Before deeper diagnosis, inspect for major fluid leaks, overheating, loose wheels, dangerous tyre damage, braking faults, fuel leakage and anything else that may make further driving unsafe.

4. Read warning lights and fault codes

Identify dashboard warnings and retrieve diagnostic trouble codes where appropriate. Record the information before clearing anything so useful evidence is not lost.

5. Inspect the relevant system

Check wiring, connectors, hoses, fluid levels, vacuum lines, mechanical condition and other visible evidence related to the symptom and stored diagnostic information.

6. Test instead of guessing

Use measurements, live data, pressure checks, voltage tests, mechanical inspection or component testing to prove whether the suspected system is actually faulty.

7. Identify the root cause

Work out why the fault occurred. A damaged component may itself have failed because of a wiring problem, fluid contamination, overheating, poor adjustment or another underlying fault.

8. Repair the confirmed fault

Replace, repair, adjust or service the confirmed cause rather than fitting parts simply because they are common failures on similar vehicles.

9. Verify the repair

Recheck the system, confirm that warning lights and fault codes are resolved where appropriate, and verify that the original symptom no longer occurs under the same conditions.

A fault code is evidence, not a parts order

For example, a sensor-related code does not automatically prove that the sensor itself has failed. Wiring, connectors, reference voltage, air leaks, mechanical problems or incorrect operating conditions may cause the control unit to detect an abnormal sensor reading.

Record the fault before clearing codes

Stored codes, pending codes and freeze-frame information may contain clues about engine speed, temperature and load when the fault was detected.

Fault Urgency

Is the car safe to drive with a fault?

Whether a vehicle is safe to continue driving depends on the affected system, severity of the symptom and how quickly the condition is changing. A small comfort-system fault may be inconvenient but low risk, while loss of braking, oil pressure or severe overheating can require immediate action.

Use the guide below as a practical severity framework rather than permission to ignore a problem. If you are unsure about braking, steering, tyres, fuel leakage, overheating or another safety-critical system, professional inspection is the safer option.

Lower urgency

Monitor and arrange diagnosis

Examples can include a failed electric window, key-fob problem, mouldy air-conditioning smell or another fault that does not affect safe vehicle control.

Typical Risk: Lower
Medium urgency

Diagnose soon

Amber warning lights, intermittent misfires, minor vibration, weak charging symptoms and developing drivetrain faults may allow limited driving but should not be ignored.

Typical Risk: Medium
High urgency

Stop or avoid further driving

Serious brake faults, severe overheating, major steering problems, dangerous tyre damage, oil-pressure warnings, heavy fuel leaks and major mechanical noises can make continued driving unsafe or cause expensive damage.

Diagnostics & MOT

Can a diagnostic fault affect the MOT?

Yes. A diagnostic problem can affect an MOT when it relates to a system or defect covered by the inspection. The MOT is not a full mechanical diagnostic examination, but warning lights, emissions, braking, steering, suspension, tyres, lighting and other safety-related systems can all influence the result.

A vehicle can also have a genuine mechanical problem that is not directly tested in the same way during the MOT. Passing an MOT therefore does not prove that every component on the vehicle is healthy.

Warning lamps

Dashboard warnings

ABS, airbag, stability-control and certain emissions-related warning lamps can affect the MOT when the relevant system is included in the test.

Mechanical condition

Safety-related defects

Brake wear, steering defects, suspension problems, tyre damage and excessive corrosion may result in advisories or failures depending on condition and severity.

Emissions

Engine and exhaust faults

Misfires, emissions-control faults, exhaust leaks and engine-management problems may affect emissions results or dashboard warning requirements.

An MOT pass is not the same as a full diagnostic health check

The MOT checks defined safety and environmental items at the time of the test. It does not diagnose every engine, clutch, gearbox, air-conditioning or intermittent electrical problem. A vehicle may therefore pass an MOT while still needing mechanical repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Car Diagnostics FAQs

These frequently asked questions cover the main principles of diagnosing warning lights, fault codes and common vehicle symptoms before replacing parts or deciding whether further driving is appropriate.

What is a car diagnostics hub?

A car diagnostics hub brings together warning lights, fault codes and common vehicle symptoms so you can start with what the car is actually doing and then follow the most relevant diagnostic guide.

Where should I start when diagnosing a car fault?

Start with the first clear symptom. Note when it happens, whether warning lights are present, whether the engine is cold or hot and whether the problem appears at idle, under acceleration, while braking or at road speed.

Should I diagnose a car fault before replacing parts?

Yes. Replacing parts by guesswork can waste money and may leave the original fault unresolved. Confirm the symptom, inspect the relevant system and test likely causes before fitting parts.

Can an OBD fault code tell me exactly which part is faulty?

Not always. An OBD fault code identifies a system, circuit or operating condition detected by the vehicle. Further testing is often needed to determine whether the cause is a sensor, wiring fault, mechanical problem, air leak, fuel problem or another related component.

What is the difference between a symptom and a fault code?

A symptom is what the driver notices, such as hesitation, vibration, smoke or poor starting. A fault code is information stored by a control module when it detects a monitored condition outside its expected range.

Which car faults should be treated as urgent?

Oil pressure warnings, brake faults, overheating, serious coolant loss, fuel leaks, heavy smoke, severe steering problems, sudden loss of braking performance and major drivetrain noises should be treated as urgent.

Should I keep driving with a red dashboard warning light?

A red dashboard warning usually indicates a condition that needs immediate attention. Stop safely when appropriate and identify the warning before continuing, especially for oil pressure, braking, overheating or charging-system faults.

Can a warning light cause an MOT failure?

Yes. Certain warning lights can cause an MOT failure when they indicate a defect in a system included in the MOT inspection, such as airbags, ABS, electronic stability control or engine-emissions related systems.

Why does my car lose power when accelerating?

Power loss under acceleration can be caused by ignition faults, fuel-delivery problems, intake leaks, turbocharger faults, DPF restriction, sensor problems, clutch slip or transmission faults. Diagnosis should begin with the exact conditions under which power is lost.

Why does my car vibrate or shake?

Vibration can come from the engine, clutch, drivetrain, wheels, tyres, brakes, steering or suspension. The speed and driving condition at which the vibration appears are important diagnostic clues.

Why is my car overheating?

Common causes include low coolant, coolant leaks, radiator fan faults, thermostat problems, poor coolant circulation, a restricted radiator or more serious internal engine faults. Continuing to drive an overheating engine can cause major damage.

Why does my car crank but not start?

If the engine cranks but does not start, likely areas include fuel supply, ignition, engine-speed signals, immobiliser operation, compression and engine-management faults.

Why does my battery keep going flat?

A battery can repeatedly go flat because it is weak, the charging system is not working correctly, the vehicle is used mainly for short journeys or an electrical component is drawing current while the vehicle is parked.

What should I check if my car smells like burning?

A burning smell can come from overheated brakes, clutch friction, leaking oil, electrical wiring, auxiliary belts or other hot components. Stop and investigate promptly if the smell is strong, accompanied by smoke or linked to a change in vehicle operation.

Can I diagnose a car without a scan tool?

Many basic checks can be carried out without a scan tool by observing symptoms, fluid levels, leaks, noises, smells, electrical operation and visible component condition. A scan tool becomes especially useful when warning lights or electronically controlled systems are involved.

Does clearing a fault code fix the problem?

No. Clearing a fault code only removes stored diagnostic information. If the underlying problem remains, the code or warning light is likely to return after the vehicle performs the relevant system checks.

What information should I give a mechanic about a fault?

Explain exactly what the car does, when the problem started, whether it occurs hot or cold, at what speed or engine load it happens, which warning lights appear and whether any recent repairs or changes were made.

Yes. The Motor Vehicle Expert diagnostic app can help you work from warning lights, symptoms and OBD fault codes towards likely causes, urgency guidance and relevant diagnostic information before further inspection.
