Your first car should make life easier, not empty your bank account. This guide explains which cars make sensible first choices, what really affects insurance and running costs, what to check before buying and how to avoid turning a cheap car into an expensive mistake.

Quick answer

What are the best first cars in the UK?

Good first cars are usually small or medium-sized hatchbacks that are easy to drive, affordable to run, widely available and straightforward to repair. Popular choices include the Toyota Aygo, Hyundai i10, Kia Picanto, Volkswagen up!, Skoda Citigo, SEAT Mii, Suzuki Swift, Toyota Yaris, Ford Fiesta and Vauxhall Corsa.

But there is no single “best” first car for everybody. The right car is the one that gives you a sensible insurance quote, has been properly maintained and fits the journeys you actually make.

1. Quote insurance

Check the exact registration or vehicle details before paying for the car.

2. Check history

Look at MOT history, service records, mileage and previous repair evidence.

3. Inspect condition

Check tyres, brakes, warning lights, fluids, bodywork and how the car drives.

4. Keep money back

Leave part of your budget for servicing, tyres, repairs and unexpected costs.

First-car rule:

Buy the best-condition car you can sensibly afford rather than the most impressive badge you can afford to purchase.

Choosing wisely

What makes a good first car?

New drivers often start by looking at colour, styling, touchscreen size or engine power. Those things can matter, but they should come after the basics.

A good first car should be easy to live with every day and should not punish you financially every time something needs servicing or repairing.

Affordable insurance

Insurance can be one of the largest first-year costs. Always quote before buying rather than guessing from engine size or insurance group alone.

Good reliability

A simple, well-maintained car with a good history is usually a better first-car choice than a neglected premium model.

Easy to drive

Good visibility, manageable size, light controls and predictable handling make everyday driving and parking less stressful.

Reasonable fuel use

A car that uses less fuel can make a noticeable difference if you commute, attend college or drive regularly.

Affordable repairs

Common models usually have good parts availability and plenty of garages familiar with repairing them.

Good condition

Condition matters more than the badge. A cared-for older car can be a much better purchase than a newer neglected one.

Budget planning

Do not spend your whole budget on the car

One of the easiest first-car mistakes is thinking the purchase price is the total cost.

If you have £5,000 available, spending the full £5,000 on the car may leave you struggling to insure, service or repair it.

Purchase price

Only the beginning
Do not use every penny

Leave room in the budget for costs that arrive immediately after purchase.

Insurance

Quote before buying
Major new-driver cost

The premium can make an otherwise affordable car unrealistic.

Maintenance reserve

Keep money aside
Protect your budget

Used cars can need tyres, brakes, servicing, a battery or other maintenance soon after purchase.

Think in yearly cost, not just purchase price

Compare the cost of buying, insuring, taxing where applicable, fuelling, servicing and repairing the car. A slightly more expensive car in good condition can sometimes cost less overall than the cheapest car in the adverts.

Small first cars

Why small city cars make sense for many new drivers

You do not have to buy the smallest car available, but compact cars have several advantages when you are newly qualified.

Easier parking

Shorter cars are generally easier to position in parking spaces and easier to judge when reversing.

Lower running demands

Smaller tyres, brakes and other service parts can sometimes cost less than equivalent parts for larger cars.

Suitable for local driving

If most journeys are to work, college, shops or around town, a small petrol car may be all you actually need.

The trade-off is space. If you regularly carry passengers, luggage or spend long periods on faster roads, a slightly larger supermini may suit you better.

Larger options

When a larger first car makes more sense

Cars such as the Toyota Yaris, Suzuki Swift, Ford Fiesta and Vauxhall Corsa can give you more cabin and luggage space while still being manageable for a new driver.

Insurance

Check insurance before choosing your first car

For a newly qualified driver, insurance can matter just as much as the price of the car.

Do not assume a 1.0-litre car will automatically be cheap to insure. Insurers consider many factors, including the exact vehicle, driver, address, occupation, expected mileage, where the car is kept and previous claims or driving history.

Compare exact cars

Different engines and trim levels of the same model can produce different premiums.

Quote before paying

Do not buy the car and discover afterwards that the insurance is far beyond your budget.

Give accurate details

Answer insurance questions truthfully. Incorrect information can create serious problems if you later need to make a claim.

Gearbox choice

Manual or automatic for your first car?

The first question is simple: what does your licence allow you to drive?

If you passed your test in an automatic car, your licence will normally restrict you to automatic vehicles unless you later pass a manual test.

Manual first car

Manual cars can give used buyers a wide choice. If you learned in a manual, staying with one may also feel familiar immediately after passing.

Automatic first car

Automatic driving can be easier in heavy traffic because there is no clutch pedal or manual gear changing. Check the specific gearbox's service history and reliability before buying used.

Still learning?

If you have not passed yet, our Learning to Drive in the UK guide explains the difference between taking your test in a manual and an automatic.

Powertrain choice

Petrol, hybrid or electric first car?

The right choice depends on your budget, mileage, parking and access to charging. Do not choose a powertrain simply because it is fashionable.

Petrol

Small petrol hatchbacks remain a straightforward option for many first-time used-car buyers, especially for mixed or lower annual mileage.

Hybrid

A hybrid can suit town and mixed driving, but check the individual model, battery condition, maintenance history and insurance before buying.

Electric

An EV can make sense if you have suitable charging and the range fits your normal journeys. Used buyers should understand battery condition, charging speed and insurance costs.

Safety

Do not ignore safety when buying a cheap first car

Cheap does not need to mean unsafe. When comparing cars of different ages, look at the safety equipment fitted to the actual vehicle.

Electronic stability control

Stability systems can help the driver maintain control when the vehicle begins to lose grip.

Tyre condition

Good tyres matter whatever safety technology the car has. Cheap, damaged or badly worn tyres reduce grip.

Warning lights

Airbag, ABS, engine or stability warning lights should not be dismissed as harmless simply because the car still drives.

Do not accept “it's only a sensor” without evidence.

If a warning light is illuminated, diagnose the fault properly before buying. A seller's guess is not a diagnosis.

Vehicle history

Check the MOT history before viewing the car

A car's MOT history can tell you a lot before you even leave home.

One advisory is not automatically a reason to reject a car. What matters is the pattern. Repeated advisories for tyres, brakes, suspension, corrosion or oil leaks may suggest maintenance has been delayed.

Repeated advisories

If the same fault appears year after year, ask whether it was ever properly repaired.

Mileage changes

Look for a sensible mileage progression and investigate anything that does not appear consistent.

Recent clean MOT

Useful, but not proof that the entire car is mechanically perfect. An MOT is not the same as a full pre-purchase inspection.

Read our guide to checking MOT history before buying if you want to understand what previous results can reveal about a used car.

Test drive

What should a new driver check on the test drive?

A first-car test drive is not about seeing how fast the car goes. You are checking whether it starts, drives, steers, stops and operates properly.

Engine and gearbox

The engine should start properly, idle reasonably and accelerate without severe hesitation. Gear changes should feel normal for the vehicle.

Steering and suspension

Listen for knocks and clunks. The car should not pull strongly to one side or feel unstable.

Brakes

Braking should feel controlled without severe pulling, grinding, excessive vibration or warning lights.

If you do not know what you are checking, get help.

Taking an experienced driver, mechanic or arranging a professional inspection can be money well spent, especially if the car uses most of your savings.

Avoid expensive mistakes

What should you avoid in a first car?

A car can be cheap to buy for a reason. New drivers should be especially careful with vehicles that may create high insurance or repair bills.

Heavily modified cars

Think carefully

Suspension changes, engine modifications, non-standard wheels and other modifications can affect insurance, reliability and how the car drives.

Unexplained warning lights

Diagnose before buying

Never assume a dashboard warning is cheap to repair simply because the seller says so.

Poor service history

Higher uncertainty

Missing paperwork does not automatically make a car bad, but it gives you less evidence that important maintenance was completed.

Very cheap premium cars

Running costs remain premium

Depreciation can make an older premium car cheap to buy without making its tyres, brakes, suspension, electronics or repairs cheap.

Persistent MOT problems

Investigate carefully

Repeated corrosion, suspension, brake or tyre problems may point to a car that has been maintained on the minimum possible budget.

No insurance quote

Do not buy blind

Falling in love with a car before checking insurance is one of the easiest ways to wreck a first-car budget.

After purchase

What to do after buying your first car

Once the car is yours, start ownership properly rather than waiting for something to go wrong.

1. Insure it correctly

Make sure the correct policy is active before you drive.

2. Check maintenance

If service history is unclear, consider starting with a basic service and inspection.

3. Check tyres and pressures

Correct tyre condition and pressure matter for grip, braking and fuel use.

4. Learn the car

Find the lights, demisters, fuel flap, bonnet release, tyre information and important dashboard warnings.

Frequently Asked Questions

Best First Cars FAQs

These are some of the most common questions UK drivers ask when choosing and buying their first car.

What makes a good first car?

A good first car should be affordable to insure, reliable, easy to drive, economical to run and reasonably inexpensive to repair. Condition and maintenance history matter just as much as the make and model.

What are some good first cars in the UK?

Cars worth considering include the Toyota Aygo, Hyundai i10, Kia Picanto, Volkswagen up!, Skoda Citigo, SEAT Mii, Suzuki Swift, Toyota Yaris, Ford Fiesta and Vauxhall Corsa. Always compare the exact vehicle's insurance, condition and history before buying.

Should I check insurance before buying my first car?

Yes. Get a quote for the exact vehicle before paying for it. Insurance can be one of the largest costs for a newly qualified driver and similar cars can produce very different premiums.

Should a first car have a small engine?

A small petrol engine can be a sensible choice, but engine size alone does not decide insurance or running cost. Compare the complete vehicle, insurance quote, reliability and condition.

Is an older car better for a first-time driver?

Older cars can be cheaper to buy, but age alone does not make a car good value. Check the MOT history, service history, insurance, tyres, brakes, corrosion and general mechanical condition.

Should I buy a manual or automatic first car?

Choose a car your licence allows you to drive and that suits your normal journeys. Automatic cars can make traffic easier, while manual cars may provide more choice in some areas of the used market.

Final Thoughts

Buy a First Car You Can Afford to Own

Your first car does not need to impress anybody. It needs to start reliably, stop safely, fit your budget and help you build confidence as an independent driver.

Small cars such as the Toyota Aygo, Hyundai i10, Kia Picanto, Volkswagen up! and similar models can make sensible starting points, while slightly larger cars such as the Toyota Yaris, Suzuki Swift, Fiesta and Corsa can work well when you need more space.

But the individual car matters more than the shortlist. Check the insurance quote, MOT history, service records and mechanical condition before paying.
