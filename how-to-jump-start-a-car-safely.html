A flat car battery can leave you stranded at home, at work or in a car park. This practical UK guide explains how to jump-start a suitable 12V car safely, which jump lead connects first, where the black earth connection should go and what to do if the engine still will not start.

Quick answer

How do you jump-start a car safely?

To jump-start a suitable car with a flat 12V battery, use properly rated jump leads and a compatible donor vehicle. With both vehicles switched off, connect the red lead to the flat vehicle's positive (+) point first, then to the donor vehicle's positive (+) point. Connect the black lead to the donor's approved negative (-) point, then its other end to the flat vehicle's designated earth point, following both handbooks.

Start the donor vehicle only if its manufacturer permits the procedure, then follow the handbook instructions for attempting to start the vehicle with the flat battery. Once the engine starts, disconnect the leads in reverse order, keeping the clamps apart and clear of moving components.

What connects first?

The red positive lead connects first, using the approved positive terminals or remote jump-start points on both vehicles.

Where does black go?

Connect to the donor's approved negative point, then to the flat vehicle's designated earth point, away from the battery where specified.

What if it fails?

Stop repeated starting attempts. Check the handbook and connections safely, then seek assistance if the vehicle still will not start.

Correct jump-lead connection order

Order Lead Connection point
1 Red (+) Flat vehicle's approved positive (+) point.
2 Red (+) Donor vehicle's approved positive (+) point.
3 Black (-) Donor vehicle's approved negative (-) point.
4 Black (-) Flat vehicle's designated earth point.
Mechanic insight:

A jump-start is a way to get a suitable vehicle running, not proof that the battery is healthy. If the car starts but will not restart later, the battery may be discharged, worn out or affected by a charging-system fault. Proper battery and alternator testing is the next step.

Do not assume every car can be jump-started the same way

Modern vehicles may have battery monitoring sensors, remote jump-start terminals and manufacturer-specific procedures. Some hybrids and electric vehicles allow limited 12V battery assistance, while others have restrictions. Never use a hybrid or electric vehicle as a donor unless its manufacturer explicitly permits it. The vehicle handbook always takes priority over a general jump-start sequence.

Understanding the problem

Why does a car need a jump-start?

Most petrol and diesel cars use a 12V battery to power the starter motor, control units and electrical systems. If the battery becomes too discharged, it may not have enough power to turn the engine over. A jump-start can provide temporary electrical assistance to get a suitable vehicle running again.

A flat battery can happen after leaving lights switched on, making repeated short journeys, leaving a vehicle unused or because the battery is getting old. Sometimes the battery is not the main problem: a faulty alternator, poor electrical connection or excessive electrical drain can cause similar symptoms.

This guide explains how to prepare for a jump-start, connect the equipment safely and recognise when to stop. You do not need to be a mechanic, but you must follow the instructions in both vehicle handbooks and avoid guessing about battery terminals or electrical connections.

Slow engine cranking

The engine turns over more slowly than normal, sometimes accompanied by dim dashboard lights. A discharged battery is one possible cause.

Rapid clicking

You hear repeated clicking when trying to start. Low battery voltage or a poor electrical connection may be preventing the starter from operating properly.

No response when starting

The dashboard may remain dark or the starter may not operate. Check for a flat battery, but remember that immobiliser, wiring and starter faults can produce similar symptoms.

Mechanic insight:

A car that cranks at normal speed but will not fire may have a fuel, ignition, sensor or other engine fault. Jump-starting is unlikely to solve a problem that is not caused by insufficient electrical power.

Equipment checklist

What you need to jump-start a car safely

Before attempting a jump-start, make sure you have suitable equipment and enough space to work safely. Poor-quality, damaged or incorrectly connected jump leads can cause electrical damage, sparks or injury.

Essential jump-start equipment

Suitable jump leads

Use insulated, undamaged jump leads rated for the vehicles and their starting requirements. The red lead is for positive connections and the black lead is for the negative or designated earth connections.

Compatible donor vehicle

The assisting vehicle must have a compatible 12V system, sufficient starting capability and manufacturer approval for providing jump-start assistance. Do not assume every modern car can safely act as a donor.

Both vehicle handbooks

Check the specified jump-start terminals, connection sequence and restrictions. Some vehicles have remote terminals rather than connections directly on the battery.

Safe working area

Choose firm, level ground away from moving traffic. Ensure adequate ventilation and enough room to reach the correct connection points without stretching the leads.

Jump-start preparation checklist

  • Read the jump-start instructions for both vehicles.
  • Confirm that both electrical systems are compatible.
  • Check that the jump leads are undamaged and suitably rated.
  • Identify the correct positive and negative or earth points.
  • Inspect the battery for damage, leaks, swelling or freezing.
  • Keep jewellery, loose clothing and long hair away from moving parts.
  • Make sure the vehicles cannot roll or touch each other.
  • Keep children and bystanders away from the working area.
Never use unsuitable electrical equipment

Do not use household extension cables, improvised wires or equipment intended for a different voltage system. Never connect a 12V vehicle to a 24V system. If you cannot confirm compatibility, arrange roadside assistance.

Before connecting

How to prepare both vehicles for a safe jump-start

Correct preparation is just as important as the connection sequence. Rushing this stage increases the risk of incorrect polarity, short circuits and accidental contact with moving parts.

1. Choose a safe position

Park on firm, level ground away from moving traffic. Position the vehicles close enough for the leads to reach without stretching, but do not allow the vehicles to touch.

2. Secure both vehicles

Apply both parking brakes. Select neutral on a manual or Park on an automatic. Switch off both ignitions and keep the keys or electronic start controls secure.

3. Switch off electrical loads

Turn off headlights, heated windows, air conditioning and other unnecessary electrical accessories. Follow any additional shutdown instructions in the vehicle handbooks.

4. Identify the correct points

Find the approved positive terminals and negative or earth connections. Do not assume that a visible battery terminal is the correct jump-start point.

Inspect the battery before proceeding

Look for obvious physical damage without touching exposed conductors or disturbing battery connections. A battery that is leaking, swollen, cracked or frozen must not be jump-started.

Battery casing

Check for cracks, distortion, swelling or escaping liquid. Stop if anything looks abnormal.

Terminals and cables

Look for loose connections, damaged wiring or heavy corrosion. Do not attach clamps to unstable or unsafe connection points.

Surrounding area

Keep the leads clear of belts, pulleys, cooling fans and hot engine parts. Electric cooling fans may operate unexpectedly.

Do not jump-start a frozen or damaged battery

Lead-acid batteries can release flammable hydrogen gas. A spark near a damaged or frozen battery can be dangerous. Never smoke or use naked flames near the battery. If you notice leaking fluid, an unusual smell, hissing or visible damage, stop and seek professional help.

Where should the black jump lead connect?

On many conventional 12V vehicles, the final black clamp connects to a manufacturer-designated earth point on the vehicle with the flat battery. This is commonly an approved metal connection away from the battery, rather than directly to its negative terminal.

However, the correct location depends on the vehicle. Some manufacturers specify a dedicated negative jump-start terminal. Always use the point identified in the handbook, not an arbitrary bolt, fuel-system component, painted panel or moving engine part.

Modern vehicle precaution:

Cars with stop-start technology or battery monitoring systems may have sensors attached to the negative battery terminal. Using the manufacturer's designated earth point helps avoid incorrect connections and interference with the monitoring arrangement.

Step-by-step procedure

How to jump-start a car safely with jump leads

Once you have checked both vehicle handbooks, inspected the batteries and prepared the vehicles, you can begin the jump-start procedure. The following is a common method for compatible 12V petrol and diesel vehicles.

The order matters because incorrect connections can cause sparks, electrical damage or injury. If either manufacturer specifies a different procedure, follow that procedure instead. Never guess where a clamp should connect.

Before you begin

Both vehicles must be switched off before connecting the leads, unless their handbooks explicitly instruct otherwise. Apply the parking brakes, select neutral or Park, and keep the vehicles from touching. Do not continue if the battery is damaged, leaking, swollen or frozen.

Step 1 — Identify the positive and negative connections

Locate the approved jump-start points on both vehicles. The positive connection is normally marked + and may have a red protective cover. The negative connection is marked − or identified as an approved earth point.

Some modern vehicles have remote jump-start terminals under the bonnet because the battery is located elsewhere. Use the designated points in the handbook rather than assuming the battery posts are suitable.

Step 2 — Connect red to the flat vehicle's positive point

Take one end of the red positive (+) jump lead and attach it securely to the approved positive connection on the vehicle with the flat battery.

Make sure the clamp grips the connection firmly. Keep the other end of the red lead away from metal surfaces and other electrical connections.

Step 3 — Connect red to the donor vehicle's positive point

Attach the other end of the red lead to the positive (+) jump-start point on the compatible donor vehicle.

Check that both red clamps are secure. Do not allow either clamp to touch a negative connection, the vehicle body or another metal object.

Step 4 — Connect black to the donor vehicle's negative point

Take one end of the black negative (−) lead and connect it to the donor vehicle's approved negative terminal or designated negative jump-start point.

Use the connection specified in the donor vehicle's handbook. Keep the free black clamp away from the positive connections.

Step 5 — Connect black to the flat vehicle's earth point

Attach the remaining black clamp to the manufacturer-designated earth point on the vehicle with the flat battery.

On many vehicles, this is an approved metal point away from the battery. It may be a dedicated terminal or a specified engine or body earth. Do not choose an arbitrary bolt or metal surface.

Why the final black connection matters

Making the final connection at a designated earth point away from the discharged battery can reduce the risk of a spark close to battery gases. Never connect to fuel-system components, moving parts, painted surfaces or unapproved mounting points. If the handbook specifies a particular connection, that instruction takes priority.

Correct jump-lead connection order

Step Lead Where to connect Important check
1 Red (+) Flat vehicle positive (+) Use the approved positive point.
2 Red (+) Donor vehicle positive (+) Keep the clamp away from metal.
3 Black (−) Donor vehicle approved negative (−) Use the specified connection.
4 Black (−) Flat vehicle designated earth point Keep clear of the battery where specified.
Easy way to remember:

Red connects to the approved positive points on both vehicles first. Black then connects to the donor's approved negative point, followed by the flat vehicle's designated earth point. Always check both handbooks before using this sequence.

Step 6 — Check the leads before starting either vehicle

Before switching on an ignition, check that all four clamps are secure and that the leads cannot touch belts, pulleys, cooling fans or hot engine parts.

Confirm that the red and black connections have not been reversed. If you discover a mistake, do not start either vehicle. Stop and follow the appropriate safe disconnection instructions.

Step 7 — Start the donor vehicle if permitted

If the donor vehicle's handbook permits jump-start assistance with its engine running, start the donor vehicle and allow it to idle as instructed.

Some manufacturers specify a particular waiting period, engine speed or a procedure with the donor engine off. Follow those instructions rather than revving the engine or improvising.

Step 8 — Attempt to start the vehicle with the flat battery

Follow the flat vehicle's handbook and make a brief, normal starting attempt. If the engine starts, allow it to settle into a normal idle.

If the starter turns slowly or the engine does not start, stop and check the manufacturer instructions. Do not keep the starter engaged continuously or make repeated attempts without allowing the recommended recovery period.

Stop immediately if something seems wrong

Stop the procedure if you notice smoke, unusual heating, strong sparking, damaged insulation, a burning smell or an abnormal battery condition. Do not touch hot or damaged cables. Keep clear and seek professional assistance.

Step 9 — Allow the started vehicle to run as instructed

Once the flat vehicle starts, follow both handbooks for any required stabilisation period before disconnecting the leads.

Do not switch on unnecessary electrical accessories immediately. Keep everyone clear of the engine compartments and make sure the leads remain away from moving components.

Step 10 — Disconnect the jump leads in reverse order

Follow the manufacturer-approved disconnection procedure. For the common connection sequence described above, remove the clamps in reverse order.

1. Black from flat vehicle

Remove the black clamp from the flat vehicle's designated earth point.

2. Black from donor

Remove the black clamp from the donor's approved negative connection.

3. Red from donor

Remove the red clamp from the donor's positive connection.

4. Red from flat vehicle

Finally, remove the red clamp from the positive point of the vehicle that was jump-started.

Keep the loose clamps separated throughout removal. Do not let them touch each other, exposed metal or moving engine components.

Correct jump-lead removal order

Removal order Lead Disconnect from
1 Black (−) Flat vehicle's designated earth point.
2 Black (−) Donor vehicle's negative connection.
3 Red (+) Donor vehicle's positive connection.
4 Red (+) Flat vehicle's positive connection.

Step 11 — Secure the equipment and check the vehicle

Once all leads are disconnected, replace any protective terminal covers and check that no equipment has been left in either engine compartment.

If the engine is running normally, follow the vehicle handbook for what to do next. A battery that has needed a jump-start should be checked rather than assumed to be fully charged.

Mechanic insight:

If a vehicle starts with assistance but struggles to restart later, the underlying fault may still be present. A battery condition test, charging voltage test and electrical-system inspection can help identify whether the battery, alternator or another component is responsible.

Alternative starting method

How to use a portable car jump starter safely

A portable jump starter is a compact battery-powered device that can provide starting assistance without another vehicle. It can be particularly useful when you are parked at home, in a car park or somewhere a donor vehicle is unavailable.

Portable jump starters are not all operated in the same way. Some have built-in clamps, detachable smart cables, polarity protection or special activation modes. Always follow the jump starter's instructions and the vehicle handbook.

Step-by-step portable jump starter procedure

1. Check compatibility

Confirm the unit is suitable for the vehicle's 12V system, engine size and starting requirements. Check that the jump starter has sufficient charge.

2. Prepare the vehicle

Switch off the ignition and unnecessary electrical loads. Apply the parking brake and identify the approved jump-start points.

3. Connect the clamps

Follow the device instructions, normally connecting red to the approved positive point and black to the designated negative or earth point.

4. Start and disconnect

Activate the unit if required, make a brief starting attempt and disconnect the equipment in the order specified by its manufacturer.

Do not bypass jump starter safety features

Some portable units have an override or boost mode intended for specific low-voltage conditions. Do not activate this mode unless the manufacturer's instructions permit it and you have positively confirmed the connections. An incorrect override can defeat important protection features.

Practical UK driver tip:

If you keep a portable jump starter in your car, check its charge regularly and inspect its cables before travelling. A discharged jump starter may be of little use when you need it most.

Essential safety advice

Jump-start safety warnings and common mistakes

Jump-starting a car is a useful emergency procedure, but incorrect connections can damage electrical components, create sparks or cause injury. Modern vehicles contain sensitive electronics, so taking shortcuts is never worth the risk.

Most problems can be avoided by checking both vehicle handbooks, using suitable equipment and identifying the correct connection points before starting. If anything is unclear, stop and arrange professional roadside assistance.

Common jump-start mistakes to avoid

Reversing the jump leads

Connecting positive to negative can cause electrical damage, overheating or dangerous sparking. Always identify the correct terminals before attaching a clamp.

Using the wrong earth point

Do not attach the final black clamp to an arbitrary bolt, painted panel or fuel-system component. Use the earth point specified by the manufacturer.

Allowing clamps to touch

Keep loose clamps separated throughout the procedure. Contact between clamps or an exposed positive connection and vehicle metal can cause a short circuit.

Using damaged jump leads

Split insulation, loose clamps and unsuitable cable ratings can lead to overheating or poor electrical contact. Replace damaged equipment before use.

Ignoring battery damage

Never jump-start a battery that is leaking, swollen, cracked or frozen. Keep clear of unusual fumes, hissing or visible battery damage.

Repeated starting attempts

Continuous cranking can overheat the starter motor, cables and jump-start equipment. Follow the manufacturer's limits and stop if the engine will not start.

Never reverse the battery polarity

Reversed polarity can damage electronic control units, charging-system components, wiring and protective fuses. If you realise the leads have been connected incorrectly, do not switch on either ignition or attempt to start the vehicles. If there is heat, smoke or sparking, keep clear and seek professional assistance rather than handling the equipment.

Can you jump-start a car in the rain?

Light rain does not automatically prevent a jump-start, but the equipment and working conditions must be safe. Keep electrical connections away from standing water, avoid damaged insulation and follow the equipment manufacturer's instructions.

Do not attempt the procedure during flooding, severe weather or when you cannot safely identify and access the connection points. Roadside assistance is the better option when conditions are unsafe.

Mechanic insight:

A secure connection at the correct point is more important than simply making the clamps fit. Poor contact can cause voltage loss, overheating and unsuccessful starting attempts.

Modern vehicle precautions

Jump-starting modern cars, stop-start vehicles, hybrids and EVs

Many newer cars have battery monitoring systems, stop-start technology and sensitive electronic control units. Some have their 12V battery in the boot, beneath a seat or behind a panel, with separate jump-start points under the bonnet.

These vehicles may require a particular connection sequence or designated earth point. Always follow the handbook rather than applying a general procedure without checking.

Cars with stop-start technology

Stop-start vehicles may use AGM or EFB batteries and battery monitoring sensors. Connect only to the manufacturer-approved jump-start points and avoid disturbing the battery monitoring connections.

Cars with remote battery terminals

Some vehicles provide dedicated positive and negative or earth connections away from the battery. These are often the correct points for jump-start assistance.

Hybrid vehicles

Some hybrids allow assistance to their 12V system using a manufacturer-approved procedure. Never touch orange high-voltage cables or attempt to jump-start the high-voltage traction battery.

Electric vehicles

Some EVs have a separate low-voltage battery that can receive approved assistance. Procedures vary considerably. Never assume an EV can act as a donor vehicle, and never access high-voltage components.

Modern car ownership tip:

Find your vehicle's jump-start instructions before an emergency occurs. Knowing the location of the approved terminals can save time and reduce mistakes when the battery unexpectedly becomes discharged.

After the engine starts

What should you do after jump-starting a car?

A successful jump-start is only the beginning. The engine may be running, but the battery could still be heavily discharged or unable to hold a charge properly.

The next priority is to make sure the vehicle is operating normally and arrange the correct battery or charging-system checks.

Step 1 — Check for warning lights

Look at the dashboard once the engine is running. Some warning lights illuminate briefly during the normal ignition self-check, but they should behave as described in the vehicle handbook.

A battery or charging warning light that remains illuminated while the engine is running may indicate a charging-system fault. Do not assume the problem is fixed simply because the engine has started.

Step 2 — Check that the engine runs normally

Listen for unusual noises and check for warning messages, smoke or abnormal engine behaviour. If the vehicle is not operating normally, do not drive it simply to recharge the battery.

Step 3 — Arrange a proper battery recharge

Driving may restore some battery charge if the alternator and charging system are healthy. However, a short journey may not sufficiently recharge a deeply discharged battery.

A suitable smart battery charger is often a better way to restore charge fully, provided the battery is safe to recharge and the manufacturer's instructions are followed.

Step 4 — Have the battery and charging system tested

If the reason for the flat battery is unclear, arrange a battery condition test and charging-system check. A garage can assess battery health, alternator output and possible electrical drains.

Step 5 — Check whether the problem returns

If the car struggles to restart after being switched off, do not rely on repeated jump-starts. The battery may need replacing, or the vehicle may have a charging or electrical fault.

Starts and runs normally

Arrange proper charging and testing, particularly if the battery went flat without an obvious explanation.

Battery warning light stays on

A charging-system fault is possible. Avoid unnecessary driving and seek professional advice or recovery.

Goes flat again

Investigate battery condition, alternator performance, electrical connections and possible current drain.

Do not assume a long drive will fix every flat battery

A failing battery may not retain charge, and a faulty alternator may not recharge the battery at all. If the charging warning light remains on, the vehicle may stop once its remaining electrical power is exhausted. Seek assistance rather than relying on driving to resolve an unknown charging fault.

Starting problem diagnosis

What if your car still will not start after a jump-start?

If the car does not start after a correctly prepared jump-start attempt, do not assume it simply needs more attempts. A completely failed battery, poor connection, starter motor fault or another engine problem may be responsible.

The sound the vehicle makes when you attempt to start it can help narrow down the next checks, but it cannot confirm a fault by itself.

Common symptoms after an unsuccessful jump-start

Rapid clicking continues

Battery voltage may still be insufficient, the battery may have failed or an electrical connection may be poor.

One loud click

A starter motor, starter solenoid, battery or electrical connection problem may be present.

Engine cranks but will not fire

Fuel delivery, ignition, immobiliser, sensors or another engine fault may need investigation.

No dashboard lights

The vehicle may have a severe battery discharge, poor main electrical connection or another power-supply fault.

Starts then stalls

A charging fault or unrelated engine-management problem may be preventing continued operation.

Jump leads become hot

Excessive current, poor contact or unsuitable equipment may be involved. Stop and do not touch hot cables.

When roadside assistance is the safest choice

Contact roadside assistance if the battery is damaged, the connection points are unclear, the jump-start procedure is unsuccessful, the equipment overheats or the vehicle develops abnormal warning lights or behaviour. Do not continue experimenting with electrical connections when the cause is uncertain.

Final safety checklist

How to jump-start a car safely: key takeaways

A jump-start can get a suitable vehicle moving again after its 12V battery becomes discharged. However, the correct procedure depends on the vehicle, its electrical system and the manufacturer's instructions.

The safest approach is to prepare carefully, connect the leads in the approved order and investigate why the battery became discharged in the first place.

Before connecting

Check both handbooks, confirm 12V compatibility, inspect the battery and identify the correct positive and earth points.

During the jump-start

Follow the approved connection sequence, keep clamps separated and make sure cables remain clear of moving parts.

After the engine starts

Disconnect safely, check for warning lights and arrange charging and testing if the battery's condition is uncertain.

Final jump-start safety checklist

  • Both vehicle handbooks have been checked.
  • The vehicles and jump-start equipment are compatible.
  • The battery is not leaking, swollen, cracked or frozen.
  • The vehicles are safely parked and cannot touch or roll.
  • The approved positive and earth connections are identified.
  • The jump leads are undamaged and correctly rated.
  • The leads are connected in the approved order.
  • The clamps remain clear of moving engine components.
  • The starting attempts follow the manufacturer's limits.
  • The leads are removed in the approved reverse sequence.
  • The vehicle is checked for abnormal warning lights.
  • The battery and charging system are tested where necessary.
Most important safety rule

Never attempt a jump-start when the battery is damaged, the correct terminals cannot be identified or the manufacturer does not permit the procedure. If you are unsure, use professional roadside assistance.

Mechanic's final advice:

Do not treat a successful jump-start as a completed repair. If the battery goes flat again, investigate battery condition, charging performance and possible electrical drain before replacing parts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Jump-Starting a Car FAQs

These are some of the most common questions UK drivers ask about jump leads, flat batteries, portable jump starters and what to do after getting the engine running.

What is the correct order for connecting jump leads?

For the common 12V procedure, connect red to the flat vehicle's approved positive point, then red to the donor's positive point. Connect black to the donor's approved negative point, then black to the flat vehicle's designated earth point. Always follow both vehicle handbooks if they specify a different procedure.

Where does the black jump lead go on a car?

The final black clamp normally connects to the designated earth point on the vehicle with the flat battery. This may be a dedicated terminal or approved metal earth connection away from the battery. Use the exact point specified in the vehicle handbook.

How long should you leave a car running after a jump-start?

Follow the vehicle manufacturer's instructions. Running the engine or taking a short drive may restore some charge if the charging system is healthy, but it may not fully recharge a deeply discharged battery. Proper charging and battery testing are recommended when the battery's condition is uncertain.

Can jump-starting a car damage the electronics?

Yes. Incorrect polarity, unsuitable equipment or failure to follow manufacturer instructions can damage electronic components. Use compatible 12V equipment, the approved connection points and the procedure specified by the vehicle manufacturer.

Can you jump-start a hybrid or electric car?

Some hybrid and electric vehicles allow assistance to their separate low-voltage battery, but the procedure varies by manufacturer. Never connect jump leads to high-voltage components or assume the vehicle can act as a donor. Check the handbook and seek professional assistance if unsure.

Why does my car keep going flat after a jump-start?

The battery may be worn out, insufficiently recharged or affected by a charging-system fault or electrical drain while parked. A successful jump-start does not prove the battery or alternator is healthy. Arrange battery and charging-system testing before replacing parts.
