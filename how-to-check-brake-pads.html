Quick Answer

How Do You Check Brake Pads?

Brake pads can often be inspected without removing the wheel by looking through the alloy wheel spokes or inspection opening in the brake caliper. The important part to examine is the friction material attached to the metal backing plate. As this friction material wears down, the braking system becomes less effective and eventually the brake pads must be replaced.

Most garages recommend replacing brake pads when approximately 3 mm of friction material remains. Waiting until the pads are completely worn risks damaging the brake discs, increasing repair costs and reducing braking performance. Regular inspections help identify wear before it becomes a safety issue.

Quick inspection summary

A healthy brake pad has plenty of visible friction material remaining, wears evenly across its surface and produces smooth, quiet braking. If the friction material looks very thin, the wear indicator is contacting the disc or grinding noises are present, the pads should be inspected professionally and replaced if necessary.

Brake Components

What Are Brake Pads?

Brake pads are one of the most important safety components on any vehicle. They create the friction needed to slow and stop the car by pressing against the rotating brake discs whenever the brake pedal is applied. Every time you brake, a tiny amount of the friction material wears away, making regular inspection an essential part of vehicle maintenance.

Modern brake pads are manufactured from specially engineered friction materials designed to withstand extremely high temperatures while providing consistent stopping performance. Although they are designed to wear over time, allowing the pads to wear too far can damage the brake discs, reduce braking efficiency and significantly increase repair costs.

Simple explanation

Think of brake pads as the sacrificial part of the braking system. They are designed to wear gradually so that more expensive components such as the brake discs, calipers and hydraulic braking system remain protected.

Friction Material

The working surface

The friction material is the section that presses against the brake disc to create the stopping force. This is the part that should always be inspected for wear.

Backing Plate

Steel support

The steel backing plate supports the friction material and transfers braking force from the caliper to the brake pad.

Wear Indicator

Early warning

Many brake pads include a mechanical or electronic wear indicator that alerts the driver when replacement is becoming necessary.

Works With Brake Discs

Essential pairing

Brake pads are designed to work with the brake discs to provide safe and consistent stopping performance. Worn brake pads can quickly damage the disc surface if left unchecked. Learn more in our Brake Discs Explained guide.

Operated By Brake Calipers

Hydraulic pressure

Brake calipers use hydraulic pressure to clamp the brake pads against the brake discs whenever the brake pedal is pressed, creating the friction needed to slow or stop the vehicle. Learn more in our Brake Calipers Explained guide.

Part Of A Complete System

Multiple components

Brake pads are only one part of the braking system. Safe and consistent braking also depends on healthy brake fluid, brake pipes, the master cylinder, brake servo and hydraulic pressure working together. Learn more in our Hydraulic Braking System Explained guide.

Brake Pad Inspection

How To Check Brake Pads Step By Step

Checking your brake pads only takes a few minutes and can often be carried out without removing the wheels. Many modern alloy wheels allow the brake pads to be seen through the spokes, making it possible to estimate remaining pad thickness with a torch. If visibility is poor or steel wheels are fitted, removing the wheel provides the most accurate inspection.

The aim of the inspection is not simply to see whether brake pads are present. You should assess the remaining friction material, check that both pads are wearing evenly, inspect the brake disc surface and look for signs of overheating, contamination or sticking brake calipers.

Before you begin

Park on level ground, apply the parking brake, switch off the engine and allow the braking system to cool completely before carrying out any inspection. Never inspect hot brakes immediately after driving.

Brake Pad Thickness

Brake Pad Thickness Guide

Brake pad thickness is one of the easiest ways to judge the condition of your braking system. As the friction material gradually wears away through normal braking, stopping performance remains largely unchanged until the pads become very thin. Waiting until the pads are almost worn out increases the risk of brake disc damage, overheating and expensive repairs.

Although exact replacement recommendations vary slightly between manufacturers, most independent UK garages recommend replacing brake pads before the friction material reaches approximately 3 mm. This allows the pads to be replaced before braking performance is affected or metal components begin contacting the brake disc.

Remember

Always measure the friction material only. Do not include the steel backing plate when estimating brake pad thickness.

8–12 mm

Excellent

Typical thickness of new brake pads. No action required beyond routine inspections.

6–8 mm

Very Good

Plenty of friction material remains. Continue checking during routine servicing.

4–5 mm

Monitor

Brake pads still have useful life remaining, but replacement should be planned over the coming months.

Around 3 mm

Replace Soon

This is the point where most workshops recommend fitting new brake pads before excessive wear develops.

Below 3 mm

Immediate Attention

Brake pads are approaching the end of their service life and should be replaced without unnecessary delay.

Front Brake Pads

Wear Faster

Front brake pads usually wear more quickly because most braking force is transferred to the front wheels during deceleration.

Rear Brake Pads

Often Last Longer

Rear brake pads generally experience lower braking loads, although wear depends on driving style and vehicle design.

Uneven Thickness

Requires Inspection

Large differences in thickness between the inner and outer brake pads often indicate a sticking brake caliper, seized slider pins or another braking system fault. Learn more in our Brake Calipers Explained guide.

Good maintenance habit

Checking brake pad thickness every few months, before long journeys and before an MOT inspection makes it much less likely that you'll experience unexpected brake problems or expensive brake disc damage.

Brake Pad Symptoms

Signs Your Brake Pads Need Replacing

Brake pads rarely fail without warning. In most cases the braking system provides several clear signs that the friction material is becoming worn. Recognising these symptoms early helps prevent brake disc damage, maintains safe stopping performance and avoids more expensive repairs.

Some warning signs develop gradually over many months, while others appear suddenly when the brake pads become excessively worn or another braking fault develops. If several symptoms occur together, arrange a professional brake inspection as soon as possible.

Do not ignore warning signs

Continuing to drive with severely worn brake pads can damage the brake discs, increase stopping distances and reduce braking performance. In extreme cases, metal-to-metal contact may occur, greatly increasing repair costs.

Brake Pad Diagnosis

Inside Vs Outside Brake Pad Wear

Brake pads should normally wear at a similar rate on both sides of the brake disc. Small differences are perfectly normal, but significant variation between the inner and outer brake pads usually indicates that part of the braking system is not operating correctly. Identifying uneven brake pad wear early can prevent brake disc damage, improve braking performance and reduce long-term repair costs.

The inner brake pad often wears slightly faster because it sits closest to the brake caliper piston. However, if one pad has substantially less friction material remaining than the other, further inspection is recommended before simply fitting a new set of brake pads.

Inspection tip

Always inspect both brake pads on every wheel whenever possible. Looking only at the outer pad can hide problems affecting the inner pad, especially on floating brake calipers.

Inspection Advice

Common Brake Pad Inspection Mistakes

Checking brake pads is relatively straightforward, but many drivers unknowingly inspect the wrong components or overlook signs that indicate developing braking problems. A few simple mistakes can lead to incorrect conclusions, unnecessary repairs or, more seriously, unsafe driving with worn braking components.

A proper inspection should always consider the complete braking system rather than the brake pads alone. Brake discs, calipers, brake fluid and hydraulic components all influence braking performance and should be examined whenever brake pad condition is assessed.

Professional advice

Professional technicians never inspect brake pads in isolation. Every brake inspection includes the pads, brake discs, calipers, brake hoses, brake fluid and hydraulic operation to ensure the entire braking system is working correctly.

Only Checking The Outer Pad

Very Common

Many drivers only look at the outer brake pad because it is easier to see. The inner pad often wears faster and may be significantly thinner.

Ignoring The Brake Disc

Inspect Together

Healthy brake pads cannot compensate for damaged, warped or heavily scored brake discs. Both components should always be inspected together because worn brake pads can quickly damage the disc surface and reduce braking performance. Inspection Advice

Should You Check Brake Pads Yourself Or Visit A Garage?

Many brake pad inspections can be carried out safely at home, especially on vehicles fitted with alloy wheels that allow the brake pads to be seen through the spokes. A simple visual inspection can often reveal whether the brake pads still have sufficient friction material or whether further investigation is needed.

However, a visual inspection has its limits. Brake pad thickness is only one part of the braking system. A professional technician will also examine the brake discs, brake calipers, hydraulic system, brake fluid, flexible hoses, slider pins and overall braking performance before recommending repairs.

Good rule to follow

A DIY inspection is ideal for monitoring brake pad wear between services. If you notice uneven wear, warning lights, grinding noises, vibration or reduced braking performance, arrange a professional inspection rather than relying on a visual check alone.

Maintenance Schedule

How Often Should You Check Brake Pads?

Brake pads should be inspected regularly throughout the year rather than waiting until an MOT test or annual service. Brake pad wear depends on driving style, vehicle weight, road conditions and the type of journeys you make. Drivers who frequently travel in heavy traffic, tow trailers or drive on steep hills will usually wear their brake pads more quickly than motorists who mainly drive on motorways.

A quick visual inspection only takes a few minutes and can often identify worn brake pads before they begin damaging the brake discs or affecting braking performance. Regular inspections also help detect uneven brake wear, sticking brake calipers and early signs of brake overheating before expensive repairs become necessary.

Simple rule

If you cannot remember the last time your brake pads were inspected, now is probably a good time to check them.

Brake pads can reveal a surprising amount about how a used vehicle has been maintained. During a viewing or test drive, inspecting the brake pads alongside the brake discs and calipers can help identify neglected servicing, expensive upcoming repairs and potential safety concerns before you agree to buy the vehicle.

Although worn brake pads are considered routine maintenance, severely worn pads, damaged brake discs or uneven brake wear may indicate that the previous owner has delayed servicing or ignored developing brake problems. A careful inspection can often save hundreds of pounds in unexpected repair costs after purchase.

Buying tip

Never judge the condition of a used car by the brake pads alone. Inspect the complete braking system including the brake discs, brake calipers, brake fluid, hydraulic components and MOT history before making a purchase decision.

What To Check Before Buying

Brake Pad Thickness

Routine Check

Confirm that plenty of friction material remains on both the inner and outer brake pads.

Brake Disc Condition

Inspect Carefully

Look for deep grooves, heavy corrosion, cracks, scoring or blue heat marks that may indicate excessive wear or overheating.

Brake discs work together with the brake pads to provide safe, consistent stopping performance. Inspecting both components at the same time helps identify wear early, maintain braking efficiency and avoid unnecessary repair costs. Learn more about brake disc inspection, common faults and replacement advice in our Brake Discs Explained guide.

Brake Calipers

Check Operation

Inspect for fluid leaks, seized slider pins, damaged dust boots or uneven pad wear that could indicate sticking calipers.

Brake calipers apply hydraulic pressure to clamp the brake pads against the brake discs, creating the friction needed to slow the vehicle safely. A sticking caliper or seized slider pins can cause uneven brake pad wear, brake overheating and reduced braking performance. Learn more about how brake calipers work, common faults and diagnosis in our Brake Calipers Explained guide.

Brake Fluid

Service History

Check that the brake fluid appears clean and ask when it was last replaced during servicing.

Brake fluid transfers hydraulic pressure from the brake pedal to the brake calipers, making it an essential part of safe and consistent braking performance. Old or contaminated brake fluid can reduce braking efficiency and contribute to brake fade during demanding driving. Learn more about brake fluid maintenance, replacement intervals and common faults in our Brake Fluid Explained guide.

Road Test

Essential

The vehicle should brake smoothly without vibration, pulling, squealing, grinding or excessive pedal travel.

Dashboard Warning Lights

Investigate

Brake, ABS and traction control warning lights should illuminate briefly at start-up before switching off normally.

The Anti-lock Braking System (ABS) helps prevent the wheels from locking during heavy braking, allowing the driver to maintain steering control while reducing the risk of skidding. If the ABS warning light remains illuminated, the braking system should be inspected as soon as possible. Learn more about how ABS works, common warning lights and fault diagnosis in our ABS Explained guide.

Hidden repair costs

A vehicle requiring brake pads alone is rarely expensive to repair. However, if worn brake pads have already damaged the brake discs or caused brake caliper problems, the repair bill can increase significantly. Always inspect the complete braking system before agreeing a purchase price.

Frequently Asked Questions

How To Check Brake Pads FAQs

How do I check brake pads?

Look through the wheel spokes with a torch and identify the brake pad friction material between the brake caliper and brake disc. If very little material remains, the pads should be inspected properly and replaced if needed.

What thickness should brake pads be replaced at?

Most garages recommend replacing brake pads at around 3 mm of friction material. Below this, replacement should not be delayed.

Can worn brake pads fail an MOT?

Yes. Brake pads can fail an MOT if they are excessively worn, insecure, contaminated, missing or causing poor braking performance.

What do worn brake pads sound like?

Worn brake pads may squeal, scrape or grind. Grinding is serious because it often means the friction material has worn away and metal may be contacting the brake disc.

Should I check inner and outer brake pads?

Yes. The inner pad can wear faster than the outer pad, especially if a brake caliper is sticking or the slider pins are seized.

Can I check brake pads without removing the wheel?

Often yes, especially with alloy wheels. However, removing the wheel gives a clearer view of both pads, the brake disc and the caliper.

How often should I check brake pads?

Check brake pads every few months, before long journeys, before an MOT, during servicing and whenever brake noise, vibration or warning lights appear.

What happens if brake pads wear too low?

Very low brake pads can increase stopping distances, damage brake discs, cause grinding noises and lead to more expensive repairs.

Why do brake pads wear unevenly?

Uneven brake pad wear is often caused by sticking calipers, seized slider pins, damaged discs, poor installation or hydraulic braking faults.

Should brake pads and discs be checked together?

Yes. Brake pads and brake discs work together, so both should always be inspected at the same time.

Can overheating damage brake pads?

Yes. Excessive heat can glaze, crack or weaken brake pad friction material and may also damage discs, calipers and brake fluid.

Should I replace brake pads myself?

Only if you are competent, have the correct tools and understand safe vehicle lifting and brake system procedures. If unsure, use a qualified garage.
