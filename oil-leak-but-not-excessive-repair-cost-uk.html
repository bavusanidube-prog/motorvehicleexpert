Quick answer

How Much Does an “Oil Leak, But Not Excessive” Cost to Fix?

There is no single repair price for an “oil leak, but not excessive” MOT record because the wording describes the leakage observed during the test rather than the component causing it. A relatively accessible gasket, seal, switch or drain-area repair may cost roughly £80–£300, while a difficult timing-cover, crankshaft-seal or similarly labour-intensive leak can reach £400–£900+ depending on the vehicle and the dismantling required.

Accessible leak

Straightforward upper-engine gaskets, switches, drain-area faults and other accessible leaks can sit toward the lower end of the repair range when diagnosis is clear and dismantling is limited.

Often lower cost

Labour-heavy leak

A cheap seal can become an expensive repair when covers, belts, drivetrain parts, suspension components or other assemblies must be removed simply to reach it.

Access drives the bill

Unknown source

If the engine is heavily contaminated and the actual source has not been traced, avoid approving major parts replacement based only on where oil happens to be visible.

Diagnose first
Mechanic insight:

Oil travels. A leak beginning high on the engine can run down the block, collect around the sump or gearbox area and make a lower component look guilty. A good diagnosis looks for the highest or earliest point of fresh leakage rather than automatically replacing the component nearest the visible oil.

The seal price and the repair price are not the same thing

Many engine seals and gaskets are relatively inexpensive parts. Labour, access, cleaning, replacement fluids and related parts can make up most of the final invoice. This is why identifying the exact source is essential before comparing garage quotations.

Do not use “not excessive” as a safety guarantee

The wording describes what was observed at a particular MOT. If the oil level is now dropping quickly, a warning light appears, oil is reaching hot exhaust components, fresh oil is pooling underneath the vehicle or the leak has suddenly worsened, the current condition matters more than the historical MOT wording.

Motor Vehicle Expert cost rule:

Find the source first, understand the access second, then price the repair. The cheapest-looking seal can produce the largest labour bill, while a worrying amount of visible oil can sometimes trace back to a much simpler repair.

MOT wording

What Does “Oil Leak, But Not Excessive” Mean?

If your MOT history records “oil leak, but not excessive”, the important point for repair budgeting is that the wording describes the condition observed during the inspection. It does not tell you which gasket, seal, housing, pipe or component is actually leaking.

That distinction matters because two cars can receive similar oil-leak wording yet face completely different repair bills. One may have an accessible upper-engine gasket leaking down the outside of the engine. Another may have a difficult seal that requires substantial dismantling before it can be replaced.

What the wording tells you

Oil leakage was visible when the vehicle was inspected and its observed severity was recorded at that time.

Useful starting evidence

What it does not tell you

It does not identify the exact leaking component, the labour required to reach it or the final repair price.

Diagnosis still required

What matters now

Check the vehicle's present condition. An old MOT entry cannot prove that the leak has remained at the same severity since the test.

Current condition matters

Read the advisory as evidence, not a parts diagnosis.

The MOT record can tell you that leakage was noticed, but it should not be converted directly into “needs a sump gasket”, “needs a rear main seal” or any other specific repair without confirming the source.

Repeated MOT entries deserve closer attention.

If similar oil-leak wording appears across several MOT tests, ask whether the source was ever diagnosed or repaired. Repeated entries do not prove that the repair will be expensive, but they can show that the condition has persisted rather than appearing only once.

Cost factors

What Determines the Cost of Fixing an Oil Leak?

The biggest mistake when estimating an oil-leak repair is looking only at the price of the gasket or seal. The replacement part can be inexpensive while the job itself costs hundreds of pounds because of the labour needed to reach, clean, dismantle and correctly reseal the leaking area.

A useful estimate therefore separates parts cost, access and labour, diagnosis, associated work and repair risk. That gives a much better indication of the likely invoice than the MOT wording alone.

1. Leak location

An accessible gasket near the top of the engine is generally easier to repair than a seal hidden behind major engine or drivetrain components.

Major price driver

2. Labour access

Covers, belts, exhaust components, subframes, transmission parts or other assemblies may need to be removed before the leaking component can even be reached.

Labour can dominate

3. Diagnostic time

A heavily contaminated engine may need cleaning and reinspection before fresh leakage can be traced accurately.

Find the source first

4. Related parts

A repair may require new fasteners, seals, O-rings, filters, fluids or related components in addition to the part that first appears to be leaking.

Quote the whole job

5. Oil contamination

Oil can contaminate belts, hoses, mounts, undertrays and nearby components. Additional cleaning or replacement may be needed if deterioration has occurred.

Inspect surrounding parts

6. Vehicle design

The same type of seal can be straightforward on one engine and difficult on another because component layout and access vary significantly.

A £20 gasket does not mean a £20 repair.

When comparing quotes, ask how many labour hours are involved, what must be removed, whether fresh oil or coolant is required and whether related seals or single-use parts are included. The component price alone can seriously understate the real job.

Compare repair scope, not just the headline price.

A cheaper quotation may exclude diagnosis, cleaning, fluids or related seals that another garage has included. Ask each garage to identify the suspected source and what the quoted repair actually covers.

Diagnosis cost

How Much Does Oil Leak Diagnosis Cost in the UK?

Before spending hundreds of pounds on a repair, it can be worth paying specifically to identify the source. A straightforward visual inspection may be quick, while a dirty or intermittent leak can need cleaning, running, road testing and reinspection before fresh oil reveals where the problem begins.

As a practical UK budgeting allowance, an initial oil-leak inspection may commonly fall around £50–£120. More involved tracing can cost more where substantial cleaning, repeat inspection or additional diagnostic work is required. These are budgeting ranges rather than fixed garage quotations.

How Much Does a Rocker Cover Oil Leak Cost to Repair?

A rocker-cover or cam-cover leak is often one of the more manageable engine-oil leaks, particularly where the cover is easy to reach and only the perimeter gasket or related seals need replacing. This is also an important leak to identify correctly because oil from the top of the engine can run down the cylinder head and block. By the time it reaches the underside of the vehicle, it may look like a sump, timing-cover or gearbox-area leak.

Repair decision

Should You Repair or Monitor an Oil Leak That Is Not Excessive?

“Not excessive” does not mean “never repair it”. The sensible decision depends on the current leak rate, oil consumption, location, contamination risk, repair cost and whether the leak is stable or getting worse.

A small, diagnosed seep on an older vehicle may sometimes be monitored for a period where the oil level remains stable and there is no immediate safety or contamination concern. A worsening or unidentified leak deserves much less tolerance.

Monitor temporarily

More reasonable where the source is known, leakage is minor, the oil level remains stable and no vulnerable or hot components are being contaminated.

Lower urgency

Book a repair

Sensible where the source is confirmed, the leak is recurring or worsening, or repair now could prevent contamination and a larger bill later.

Plan the repair

Stop and investigate

Treat the problem more urgently if oil loss becomes significant, the oil-pressure warning appears, fresh oil pools underneath or oil reaches hot or safety-critical components.

Monitoring means checking it — not ignoring it.

If you decide to monitor a diagnosed minor leak, check the engine-oil level correctly and regularly, watch the ground where the vehicle is parked and look for any change in smell, smoke, warning lights or leakage rate.

A planned repair is usually cheaper than an emergency.

Once the source and likely cost are known, you can decide when to repair it and budget accordingly. Waiting until the oil level falls dangerously low or another component becomes contaminated can turn a manageable leak into a much larger bill.

UK MOT

Can an Oil Leak Fail an MOT?

Yes, an oil leak can affect the MOT, but the presence of some oil leakage does not automatically mean the vehicle must fail. The tester assesses the condition present at the time of the test against the applicable MOT inspection criteria.

Under the current DVSA MOT inspection framework, excessive leakage of fluid other than water that is likely to harm the environment or pose a safety risk is classed as a Major defect. Continuous leakage that is likely to pose a serious risk to road safety is classed as Dangerous.

Leak observed

A tester may record oil leakage that is present but does not meet the relevant rejection threshold.

The MOT is not an oil-leak diagnostic procedure.

The test result can be valuable evidence, but it does not replace workshop diagnosis. The tester is not providing a strip-down assessment of which gasket or seal has failed or what the final repair will cost.

Do not assume last year's wording protects this year's MOT.

A leak can worsen between tests. If fresh oil is now dripping, pooling or being lost at a meaningful rate, investigate the current condition rather than relying on an older “not excessive” record.

Used-car buying

Should You Buy a Car With an “Oil Leak, But Not Excessive” MOT?

An “oil leak, but not excessive” entry is not, by itself, a reason to reject a used car. It is a reason to find out where the oil is coming from and what fixing it is likely to cost before agreeing a price.

A documented £150–£250 gasket repair presents a very different financial risk from an unidentified leak around the engine and transmission that could require major dismantling. For a buyer, uncertainty is part of the cost.

Lower buying risk

Source confirmed, leakage minor, oil level stable, repair cost known and evidence shows the issue has been dealt with or priced realistically.

Lower risk

Medium buying risk

Leak remains present but appears limited, with no major oil loss. Obtain a diagnosis and repair quotation before deciding what the car is worth to you.

Price the repair first

High buying risk

Source unknown, engine heavily contaminated, repeated MOT entries, seller has no explanation, oil level is low or the suspected repair requires major dismantling.

Use the leak to improve the buying decision, not simply to reject the car.

If an independent inspection identifies an accessible repair and gives you a credible price, the leak can become a known negotiating figure. The bigger danger is paying full market value while taking on an unidentified repair that could cost many hundreds of pounds.

Be cautious if the engine has just been cleaned.

A clean engine is not automatically suspicious, but if the MOT history repeatedly records oil leakage and the engine bay has just been washed, look carefully for fresh oil after a proper test drive and consider an independent inspection before buying.

Motor Vehicle Expert buying rule:

Known minor leak + known repair cost = a decision you can price.
Unknown leak + unknown access + repeated history = financial risk you should not accept blindly.

FAQs

Oil Leak But Not Excessive Repair Cost FAQs

These are the key questions UK drivers and used-car buyers ask after finding an “oil leak, but not excessive” entry in an MOT history.

How much does it cost to fix an oil leak but not excessive in the UK?

The cost can range from a relatively inexpensive minor seal or connection repair to several hundred pounds or more where major dismantling is required. The MOT wording does not identify the leaking component, so the source and access required must be diagnosed before an accurate repair price can be given.

What does oil leak but not excessive mean on an MOT?

It means oil leakage was observed but the condition recorded at that MOT did not reach the applicable rejection threshold. It does not identify the failed gasket, seal, pipe or component, and it does not mean the leak should be ignored.

Is an oil leak but not excessive expensive to repair?

Not necessarily. Some leaks come from accessible seals, gaskets, switches or sump-plug areas and can be relatively inexpensive to repair. Others involve timing covers, crankshaft seals or components requiring substantial dismantling, so labour can make the repair much more expensive.

Should I fix an oil leak advisory before the next MOT?

It is sensible to diagnose the leak rather than simply waiting for the next MOT. A small leak can worsen, lower the fluid level or contaminate other components. Knowing the source also lets you budget for the repair before it develops into a more serious problem.

Can an oil leak but not excessive become an MOT failure?

Yes. A leak that was below the rejection threshold at one MOT can become more severe later. Current DVSA guidance classifies excessive fluid leakage likely to harm the environment or pose a safety risk as Major, while continuous leakage likely to pose a serious road-safety risk is Dangerous.

Why can a cheap oil seal cost hundreds of pounds to replace?

The seal or gasket itself may be inexpensive, but labour depends on where it is fitted and what must be removed to reach it. A difficult-access seal can require removal of covers, belts, subframes, transmission components or other assemblies, making labour the largest part of the bill.

How does a garage find where an oil leak is coming from?

A garage normally begins by checking the fluid level and inspecting the contaminated area. Where the source is unclear, the area may need to be cleaned before the engine is run or the vehicle is road-tested so fresh leakage can be traced back to its highest or earliest visible point. Dye or other diagnostic methods may also be used where appropriate.

Should I buy a used car with an oil leak but not excessive advisory?

Do not judge the car from the advisory wording alone. Ask where the leak is coming from, whether the fluid level is dropping, whether repair estimates exist and whether the advisory appears repeatedly in the MOT history. An inexpensive diagnosed gasket leak is a very different buying risk from an unidentified leak requiring major dismantling.
