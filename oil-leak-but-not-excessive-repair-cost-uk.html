Quick Answer

How Much Does an “Oil Leak, But Not Excessive” Cost to Fix?

An “oil leak, but not excessive” MOT record does not have one fixed repair price because the wording tells you that leakage was observed — not which component is leaking. On many UK cars, a straightforward accessible oil-leak repair can fall around £80–£300, while difficult-access leaks can rise to £400–£900+ when substantial dismantling is needed.

The important question is therefore not simply “How much is an oil leak?” but “Where is this oil coming from, how quickly is it escaping and what has to be removed to repair it?” The gasket or seal itself may be inexpensive; access and labour can dominate the final bill.

MOT Wording

What Does “Oil Leak, But Not Excessive” Mean for Repair Cost?

If an MOT history records “oil leak, but not excessive”, the wording tells you that leakage was observed during that inspection. It does not identify the leaking component, tell you how difficult that component is to reach or provide enough information to calculate the repair bill.

That is why two cars carrying very similar MOT wording can need completely different repairs. One may have oil escaping from an accessible upper-engine gasket, while another may have oil travelling down the engine from a seal hidden behind components that require several hours of dismantling.

MOT Finding

What the record tells you

Leakage was visible and recorded at the time of the MOT. The wording provides useful evidence that a leak existed, but it is not a mechanical diagnosis of the source.

Unknown

What it does not tell you

It does not tell you which gasket, seal, housing, pipe or component has failed, how quickly the leak is progressing or how much dismantling is required to repair it.

Cost Decision

What you need next

Identify the fluid and trace the source before relying on a repair estimate. The location and access requirements usually matter far more to the final bill than the MOT wording itself.

Price Factors

What Affects the Cost of Fixing an Oil Leak?

Oil-leak repair prices are driven by much more than the price of the replacement gasket or seal. The biggest difference is often how much labour is required to reach the source, replace the failed part, clean the affected area and confirm that the leak has stopped.

Vehicle design matters as well. The same type of seal may be easy to reach on one engine and buried behind major assemblies on another, so generic parts prices are a poor way to predict the final garage bill.

Factor 1

Leak location

Upper-engine and externally accessible leaks can be easier to repair than leaks located between major assemblies or behind covers, belts and transmission components.

Factor 2

Labour access

A low-cost seal can become an expensive repair if several hours of dismantling and reassembly are required before a technician can reach it.

Factor 3

Vehicle design

Engine layout, available working space and the position of surrounding components can substantially change labour time between makes, models and engines.

Factor 4

Parts required

The job may need more than one gasket or seal. New fasteners, fluids, filters, sealants, belts or other disturbed components may also be required depending on the repair procedure.

Factor 5

Contamination and cleaning

Heavy oil contamination can make the source difficult to identify and may require cleaning before and after repair. Contaminated components may need separate attention.

Factor 6

Garage labour rate

Labour rates vary across the UK and between independent garages, specialists and franchised workshops. Labour-heavy repairs therefore show particularly large price differences.

Be cautious with quotes based only on the MOT wording

A garage cannot reliably price an unidentified oil leak from “oil leak, but not excessive” alone. A useful estimate should identify the suspected source and explain what work is included.

Diagnosis Before Repair

How Much Does Oil Leak Diagnosis Cost in the UK?

Before paying for a large oil-leak repair, it can be worth paying specifically for diagnosis. A straightforward visible leak may be identified during a normal inspection, while a heavily contaminated, intermittent or difficult-to-trace leak can require cleaning and additional workshop time.

As a practical UK budgeting guide, allow roughly £50–£120 for an initial oil-leak inspection or diagnostic session. More involved investigations can cost more where the engine or underbody must be cleaned, covers removed or the vehicle reassessed after running or road testing.

What should a proper oil-leak diagnosis involve?

1. Confirm the fluid

Do not assume every dark wet patch is engine oil. Establish which fluid is escaping before deciding which system or component needs investigation.

2. Check the level

Check whether the relevant fluid level is correct and whether there is evidence of meaningful loss between checks.

3. Inspect from high to low

Oil runs downwards and can be blown rearwards while driving. The lowest wet point is therefore not automatically the source of the leak.

4. Clean contaminated areas

Where old oil hides the source, cleaning the affected area can make it possible to distinguish historic contamination from fresh leakage.

5. Recheck for fresh oil

The engine may need to be run or the vehicle reassessed under suitable conditions so the technician can identify where fresh fluid first appears.

6. Quote the proven repair

Once the likely source is established, the garage can estimate parts, labour, additional fluids and any related work required to reach the component.

Avoid “replace the lowest wet gasket first” diagnosis

Gravity and airflow can spread oil over the sump, gearbox, subframe and undertray. Replacing a component simply because it is wet can leave the original leak untouched.

Spending £50–£120 on diagnosis can protect a much larger repair budget.

If the alternative is authorising several hundred pounds of labour on the wrong component, identifying the source first can be the cheapest part of the entire repair.

Component Cost Guide

Rocker Cover / Cam Cover Oil Leak Repair Cost

A leaking rocker-cover or cam-cover gasket is one of the more familiar causes of oil appearing around the upper part of an engine. The gasket seals the cover above the cylinder head, and age, heat cycles, hardened sealing material or a distorted cover can eventually allow oil to escape.

Where the cover is easy to reach and only the gasket or sealing arrangement needs attention, this can sit toward the lower-to-middle end of oil-leak repair costs. However, some modern engines have crowded upper-engine layouts, integrated breather systems or complete covers that cost considerably more than a simple replacement gasket.

Why these prices are ranges rather than promises

UK repair prices vary with make and model, engine layout, labour rate, parts quality, corrosion, seized fasteners and the amount of dismantling required. A vehicle-specific written quotation should therefore take priority over any national guide price.

Ask for an itemised quotation

A useful quote should identify the suspected leak source and separate, where practical, the parts, labour, fluids and related work required. It should also make clear whether VAT is included. That makes competing quotations much easier to compare.

Do not choose the cheapest quote until you know what each garage is actually repairing.

A £180 quote for a gasket and a £450 quote for a complete housing assembly may describe different repairs rather than different prices for the same job. Compare the diagnosis and scope of work first, then compare the price.

Repair Decision

Should You Repair an Oil Leak But Not Excessive or Monitor It?

The words “but not excessive” should not be interpreted as “no repair required”. They describe the condition recorded at an inspection; they do not guarantee that the leak will remain at the same rate or that continued driving cannot create another problem.

The sensible decision depends on the leak rate, fluid level, leak location, repair cost, contamination risk, vehicle value and whether the condition is changing.

Monitor Briefly

Slight seepage, stable level

Where diagnosis confirms only slight seepage, the oil level remains stable and there is no contamination of vulnerable or hot components, short-term monitoring may be reasonable while planning the repair.

Safety or lubrication risk

A vehicle may have covered thousands of miles since the advisory was recorded. If the leak rate, oil level, smell, smoke or contamination has changed, assess the vehicle as it is now, not as it appeared at the previous test.

Monitoring means measuring, not ignoring

If a diagnosed minor leak is being monitored temporarily, check the relevant fluid level at suitable intervals and look for changes in the amount of fresh leakage. Monitoring without checking the level provides very little protection.

UK MOT

Can an Oil Leak But Not Excessive Fail the MOT?

An oil leak does not automatically mean an MOT failure. The result depends on the condition observed by the tester and whether the leakage meets the applicable rejection criteria at the time of the test.

Under the current DVSA MOT inspection manual, relevant fluid leaks are checked with the engine idling. A vehicle should fail where a leak creates a pool on the floor within five minutes that is more than 75 mm in diameter, or where multiple leaks collectively leak fluid at the same rate.

Used-Car Buying Risk

Should You Buy a Used Car With an “Oil Leak, But Not Excessive” Advisory?

An “oil leak, but not excessive” entry should not automatically make you reject a used car. But you should not value the car as though the advisory does not exist either.

The financial risk depends on whether the leak has been properly diagnosed, whether it repeatedly appears in the MOT history, how much oil is currently being lost and how much labour would be required to repair the source.

Lower Risk

Source known

A documented, accessible gasket or seal with a sensible written repair estimate is much easier to price into the purchase.

Advisory repeated

The same oil-leak wording appearing across several MOTs can suggest that the problem has been left unresolved or that a previous repair has not eliminated it.

Source unknown

A heavily contaminated engine, falling oil level and no proven source can expose you to a much larger repair bill after purchase.

How much should you allow when negotiating?

Do not automatically deduct an arbitrary £100 or £200 because the MOT says “oil leak, but not excessive”. If the source has not been diagnosed, the financial exposure could be much larger than that.

The strongest negotiating position is a pre-purchase inspection plus a written repair estimate. You can then decide whether the seller should repair the fault before purchase, reduce the price appropriately, or whether the potential repair is large enough to walk away.

Be especially cautious with a freshly cleaned engine

A clean engine is not automatically suspicious, but if the MOT history repeatedly mentions leakage and the engine has just been washed, there may be too little fresh evidence to judge the source during a quick viewing. An independent inspection can be worth far more than guessing.

Read the MOT history as a timeline

One advisory gives you one inspection snapshot. Repeated entries can tell you much more. Look at whether the oil leak first appeared recently, persisted for several tests, became more serious or disappeared after documented repair work.

Used-car rule:

Do not buy the MOT wording — buy the diagnosis. A confirmed £150–£250 repair can be negotiated. An unidentified leak that might require £1,000+ of labour is a very different financial risk.

FAQs

Oil Leak But Not Excessive Repair Cost FAQs

How much does it cost to fix an oil leak but not excessive in the UK?

The cost can range from a relatively inexpensive minor seal or connection repair to several hundred pounds or more where major dismantling is required. The MOT wording does not identify the leaking component, so the source and access required must be diagnosed before an accurate repair price can be given.

What does oil leak but not excessive mean on an MOT?

It means oil leakage was observed but the condition recorded at that MOT did not reach the applicable rejection threshold. It does not identify the failed gasket, seal, pipe or component, and it does not mean the leak should be ignored.

Is an oil leak but not excessive expensive to repair?

Not necessarily. Some leaks come from accessible seals, gaskets, switches or sump-plug areas and can be relatively inexpensive to repair. Others involve timing covers, crankshaft seals or components requiring substantial dismantling, so labour can make the repair much more expensive.

Should I fix an oil leak advisory before the next MOT?

It is sensible to diagnose the leak rather than simply waiting for the next MOT. A small leak can worsen, lower the fluid level or contaminate other components. Knowing the source also lets you budget for the repair before it develops into a more serious problem.

Can an oil leak but not excessive become an MOT failure?

Yes. A leak that was below the rejection threshold at one MOT can become more severe later. Current DVSA guidance classifies excessive fluid leakage likely to harm the environment or pose a safety risk as Major, while continuous leakage likely to pose a serious road-safety risk is Dangerous.

Why can a cheap oil seal cost hundreds of pounds to replace?

The seal or gasket itself may be inexpensive, but labour depends on where it is fitted and what must be removed to reach it. A difficult-access seal can require removal of covers, belts, subframes, transmission components or other assemblies, making labour the largest part of the bill.

How does a garage find where an oil leak is coming from?

A garage normally begins by checking the fluid level and inspecting the contaminated area. Where the source is unclear, the area may need to be cleaned before the engine is run or the vehicle is road-tested so fresh leakage can be traced back to its highest or earliest visible point. Dye or other diagnostic methods may also be used where appropriate.

Should I buy a used car with an oil leak but not excessive advisory?

Do not judge the car from the advisory wording alone. Ask where the leak is coming from, whether the fluid level is dropping, whether repair estimates exist and whether the advisory appears repeatedly in the MOT history. An inexpensive diagnosed gasket leak is a very different buying risk from an unidentified leak requiring major dismantling.
