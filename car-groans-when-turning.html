UK steering noise diagnostic guide

Car Groans When Turning?

A groaning noise when turning can come from the power steering system, steering rack, top mounts, suspension joints or other components placed under load as the front wheels change direction. This complete UK guide explains how to tell hydraulic power steering noise from EPS and suspension faults, how mechanics locate the real cause, when the problem becomes unsafe, what it could mean for the MOT and how much common repairs may cost.

Important steering safety note

A groaning sound does not tell you how serious the fault is. If the steering has also become heavy, jerky, notchy or inconsistent, the steering wheel does not return normally, an EPS or steering warning light appears, or power steering fluid is leaking, arrange diagnosis promptly. If steering control feels significantly affected, do not continue driving simply because the vehicle can still be steered.

This guide is written for UK drivers and used car buyers who want to understand a groaning steering noise before replacing parts. It separates hydraulic power steering faults from electric power steering, steering rack, top mount, suspension and wheel-end causes so diagnosis starts with the conditions that reproduce the noise rather than guessing from the sound alone.

Quick Answer

Why Does My Car Groan When I Turn?

A car that groans when turning is usually producing the noise because something in the steering or front suspension is under load while the wheels change direction. On a vehicle with hydraulic power steering, low or aerated fluid, a fluid leak, pump problems, restricted flow or steering-rack resistance are important possibilities. On an electric power-steering vehicle, diagnosis instead focuses on the EPS system and mechanical resistance because there is no hydraulic PAS fluid or hydraulic PAS pump.

Not every turning groan comes from the steering-assistance system. Top mounts, strut bearings, coil-spring wind-up, ball joints, suspension bushes and other components can groan as they articulate. These noises can travel through the body and steering structure, making a suspension fault sound surprisingly similar to a steering pump or rack problem.

Hydraulic PAS

Groan Changes With Steering Load

Check fluid level and condition, leaks, aeration, pump behaviour, hydraulic pipes and steering-rack resistance.

Top Mount

Groan at Strut Tower

A binding top mount or strut bearing can resist strut rotation and make the spring twist before releasing.

Suspension

Groan Changes With Body Movement

Ball joints and suspension bushes become stronger suspects when bumps, braking pitch or suspension movement also reproduce the sound.

High Concern

Groan + Heavy Steering

Heavy, jerky, notchy or inconsistent steering requires prompt investigation rather than treating the symptom as an ordinary noise complaint.

Start with the steering system fitted to the car

Do not diagnose every turning groan as “low power steering fluid”. Many modern cars use electric power steering and have no hydraulic PAS reservoir or pump. Identify the steering-assistance design before interpreting the noise.

First Diagnostic Split

Where Is the Groaning Noise Actually Coming From?

Location is one of the strongest early clues. A hydraulic pump, steering rack, top mount and lower suspension joint can all make a driver describe the symptom as “groaning when turning”, but the sound usually becomes strongest in a different part of the vehicle.

Reproduce the noise safely and listen for where it begins rather than where it is easiest to hear from the driver's seat.

Engine Bay

On hydraulic PAS vehicles, a groan strongest around the pump, reservoir, pipework or accessory-drive area moves hydraulic assistance higher on the list.

Bulkhead / Rack Area

Noise lower down around the rack or steering input requires rack, mounting, steering-shaft and linkage checks.

Strut Tower

A groan concentrated around the upper strut strongly supports checking the top mount, strut bearing and spring behaviour.

Wheel / Lower Suspension

Lower-arm bushes, ball joints and other articulating suspension components become more important when the noise is strongest around the wheel-end.

Hydraulic pump / reservoir area Low fluid, aeration, pump, hydraulic restriction Noise changes strongly with steering assistance demand That the pump itself has failed
Steering rack area Rack, hydraulic fault, mounting or mechanical resistance Uneven or notchy steering may also be present That every rack-area noise requires a new rack

Lower wheel-end Ball joint, lower-arm bush, steering joint Noise may change with suspension load That steering assistance is responsible
Inside cabin / column Column, intermediate shaft, EPS column or transmitted noise Noise follows steering-wheel input closely That the sound originates where the driver hears it
Test Condition

Groaning While Stationary vs Groaning While Moving

Whether the car must be moving is a major diagnostic separator. Steering while stationary creates high tyre-to-road resistance and can expose faults that are much quieter once the vehicle begins to roll.

Stationary

Groans Without Wheel Rotation

Concentrate on components that move or load purely because the steering wheel is turned: hydraulic or electric assistance, steering rack, top mounts, strut bearings, ball joints and steering linkage.

Diagnostic Value: High
Moving

Groans Only While Rolling

Wheel rotation, changing suspension position, tyre contact and drivetrain load now become additional variables. Reproduce the symptom at controlled low speed before blaming the steering assistance.

Compare With Stationary Test
A stationary steering test is useful, but avoid unnecessary loading

Repeatedly forcing the steering hard against full lock is not a useful diagnostic technique. Use only enough steering movement to reproduce the symptom and compare the sound through the normal steering sweep.

Assistance Comparison

Does the Groan Change With the Engine Running?

Where appropriate for the vehicle, comparing the symptom with steering assistance active and inactive can provide useful evidence. The result must be interpreted according to the steering system fitted rather than used as a universal test.

Much Louder With Assistance Active

On hydraulic systems, investigate fluid condition, aeration, pump operation and hydraulic load. On EPS vehicles, consider assistance-system noise alongside mechanical resistance.

Similar With Assistance Inactive

A mechanically generated groan from top mounts, joints, bushes, steering shaft or rack components becomes more plausible.

Steering Becomes Abnormally Heavy

Do not confuse the expected increase in steering effort without assistance with a fault. Diagnosis must account for the design and normal operating characteristics of the vehicle.

Critical Distinction

Hydraulic Power Steering vs Electric Power Steering Groans

This distinction prevents one of the most basic steering-noise diagnostic mistakes. A hydraulic PAS vehicle and an EPS vehicle do not have the same assistance components, so the same groaning symptom requires a different fault path.

Hydraulic PAS

Fluid-Driven Assistance

Depending on design, diagnosis can include the reservoir, fluid, hydraulic pump, drive system, pressure and return pipes, seals and hydraulic steering rack.

Low fluid, air in the system, fluid leakage, pump wear or excessive hydraulic load can all change steering noise.

EPS

Electrically Assisted Steering

EPS uses an electric assistance motor and associated control system rather than a conventional hydraulic PAS pump and fluid circuit.

Diagnosis may involve electrical supply, motor or reduction gear, steering sensors, fault information, rack or column assemblies and mechanical resistance elsewhere in the steering or suspension.

Do not look for PAS fluid on a car that uses pure EPS

Confirm the steering system from reliable vehicle information or physical inspection. Assuming every vehicle has a hydraulic PAS reservoir can send the diagnosis in the wrong direction before it has even started.

Vehicle Speed

Groaning at Low Speed vs Normal Road Speed

Parking manoeuvres place high steering load through the front tyres and usually require much greater steering angle than normal driving. That is why many steering and suspension groans are easiest to reproduce at walking pace.

Parking Speed Only

Hydraulic assistance, EPS load, top mounts, steering rack and articulating suspension joints become important.

Low and Higher Speeds

A fault that remains obvious with much lower steering effort may be mechanically pronounced or may also respond to suspension movement rather than steering load alone.

Only While Manoeuvring

Compare steering angle, throttle input, road surface and forward versus reverse movement before concluding that the PAS system is defective.

Steering Angle

Groaning During a Partial Turn vs Near Full Lock

Record where in the steering sweep the groan begins. A noise that appears throughout normal steering movement tells a different story from one that occurs only at very high steering angle.

Starts Immediately

Consider components moving through the complete steering sweep, including the assistance system, rack, top bearings and joints.

Only Near Full Lock

High assistance demand, maximum suspension articulation, tyre scrub and full-lock-specific contact become more relevant.

Continues at Steering Stop

Do not deliberately hold the steering against its stop to make the noise louder. Reproduce the symptom briefly and diagnose the cause instead.

Noise only on maximum steering angle?

Compare the symptom with our dedicated Car Makes Noise on Full Lock guide, which separates groaning from clicking, tyre scrub, rubbing, creaking and popping.

Direction

Does the Car Groan Turning Left, Right or Both Ways?

Steering direction changes the load and articulation of individual components. A one-direction-only symptom is useful evidence, but it does not automatically identify which side has failed.

Both Directions

A central assistance-system problem, rack resistance or a component that moves through the entire steering sweep remains plausible.

Mainly Left

Compare left-turn component loading, steering angle and suspension movement with the opposite direction.

Mainly Right

Use the same controlled comparison rather than automatically replacing a component on the side where the driver hears the noise.

Temperature

Why Does the Steering Groan More When Cold?

Temperature can change hydraulic-fluid behaviour, rubber stiffness, seal behaviour, lubricant characteristics and friction within bearings and joints. A groan that changes as the vehicle warms up is therefore valuable diagnostic evidence.

Cold Start Only

On hydraulic systems, compare fluid behaviour and pump noise. Also consider bearings, bushes and joints whose friction changes with temperature.

Improves When Warm

Temperature sensitivity supports further investigation but does not prove whether the cause is hydraulic or mechanical.

Worse When Warm

Do not dismiss the fault simply because the common cold-noise pattern does not fit. Reproduce the actual warm condition and inspect from there.

Weather Clue

Steering Groan in Wet Weather vs Dry Weather

A strong wet-versus-dry change can point away from a purely hydraulic explanation and towards friction at rubber bushes, mounts, joints or other exposed mechanical interfaces.

Changes After Rain

Moisture can temporarily alter friction at suspension bushes, mounts and exposed components. Record whether the sound becomes louder, quieter or disappears.

Unchanged by Weather

Hydraulic, EPS, rack and mechanically internal causes remain fully possible. Weather response is supporting evidence, not a pass-or-fail diagnostic test.

High-Value Clue

What Does the Steering Feel Like While It Groans?

Steering feel can be more important than the sound itself. A car that groans but steers smoothly is a different diagnostic and safety case from one that groans while becoming heavy, notchy or inconsistent.

Smooth & Normal

The fault still needs diagnosis, but there is less evidence of an immediate steering-control problem.

Book Diagnosis

Heavy

Investigate loss of assistance, hydraulic problems, EPS faults and excessive mechanical resistance.

Inspect Promptly

Notchy / Jerky

Binding top bearings, steering joints, rack problems or assistance faults become more concerning.

Steering Concern

Poor Self-Centring

Look for binding or abnormal resistance within steering and suspension components rather than treating the groan alone.

Diagnose Urgently
Noise plus a steering-control change raises the priority

Heavy steering, intermittent assistance, binding, sudden changes in effort, significant free play or steering that does not respond predictably should be treated as a steering fault requiring prompt professional assessment.

Hydraulic Evidence

Power-Steering Fluid and Leak Evidence

This section applies only where the vehicle actually uses hydraulic power steering. Low fluid is evidence of a problem, not a diagnosis by itself.

Low Fluid Level

Find out why the level is low. Repeated topping-up can hide an active leak without repairing it.

Foamy / Aerated Fluid

Air within the hydraulic system can change pump noise and assistance behaviour and requires the source to be identified.

Wet Hoses or Connections

Inspect pressure and return lines, unions and nearby components for genuine leakage rather than assuming every oily surface is PAS fluid.

Rack-Gaiter Area Wetness

On hydraulic racks, fluid evidence around rack components can support further leak investigation.

Use the correct fluid specification

Do not add a generic fluid simply because the reservoir is low. Steering systems can require specific fluid types and incorrect fluid can create additional problems. Follow the vehicle manufacturer's specification.

Fault Differentiation

Power-Steering Pump vs Rack vs Top Mount vs Suspension Groan

These faults overlap enough that replacing parts from the sound alone is poor diagnostic practice. Compare where the noise is strongest, what load reproduces it and whether steering feel changes.

Pump-Led

Follow Hydraulic Evidence

Fluid condition, air, leakage and where the sound originates are more useful than simply noticing that the noise occurs during steering.

Mount-Led

Watch the Spring

A spring that winds up rather than rotating smoothly can reveal a top-mount or strut-bearing problem.

Joint-Led

Reproduce Mechanical Load

Compare steering-only movement with bumps, body pitch and loaded suspension movement to separate joints and bushes from the assistance system.

Mechanic Insight

Diagnose the Load Path, Not Just the Groaning Sound

The useful question is not simply, “What makes a groaning noise?” The useful question is, “Which components were loaded or moved at the exact moment the groan occurred?”

If the car groans while completely stationary, wheel rotation is not required. If it changes dramatically when hydraulic assistance is active, investigate the hydraulic system and the load being placed on it. If the sound is concentrated at the strut tower and the spring twists as steering moves, the top bearing becomes much more convincing. If bumps or body movement reproduce the same sound, suspension joints and bushes move higher up the list.

Identify the System

Hydraulic PAS, EPS and other assistance designs require different diagnostic paths.

Reproduce the Exact Condition

Stationary, moving, cold, warm, partial lock and full lock are different tests.

Localise Physically

Pump, rack, strut tower and lower suspension noises should not be treated as interchangeable.

Confirm Before Replacing

Expensive pumps, racks and EPS assemblies should have supporting diagnostic evidence before replacement.

Master mechanic shortcut

First identify whether the vehicle has hydraulic or electric power steering. Then reproduce the groan stationary and moving, locate where it is strongest, compare steering angle and temperature, and pay close attention to steering feel. Those observations usually narrow the investigation far more effectively than replacing the component most commonly associated with the word “groan”.

Complete Cause Map

What Can Make a Car Groan When Turning?

A turning groan can come from the steering-assistance system, steering gear, suspension or tyre-to-road interaction. The most reliable way to diagnose it is to follow the load path from the driver's steering input through the assistance system and steering linkage, then outward into the suspension and tyre contact patch.

Hydraulic and electric power steering require different fault maps. A hydraulic vehicle can develop fluid, pump, hose and rack faults that simply do not exist on a pure EPS vehicle.

Hydraulic

Fluid & Pump Load

Low fluid, air, pump wear, hydraulic restriction and rack load can all create groaning.

Electric

EPS Assistance

Motor, reduction gear, control faults and mechanical resistance must be considered instead of hydraulic components.

Steering

Rack & Linkage

Steering-rack resistance, track-rod joints and column or shaft stiffness can transmit groaning through the system.

Suspension

Mounts, Joints & Bushes

Top mounts, strut bearings, ball joints and bushes can groan as steering and suspension loads change.

Load Path

How Steering Load Creates a Groaning Noise

At parking speeds the tyres resist turning against the road surface. Steering assistance must overcome that resistance while the rack, track rods, steering knuckles and suspension joints all articulate.

1. Steering Input

The driver turns the steering wheel and applies torque through the column.

2. Assistance System

Hydraulic pressure or electric motor torque reduces steering effort.

3. Steering Rack

The rack moves laterally and transmits force to the track rods.

4. Suspension & Tyres

Top mounts, ball joints, bushes and tyres resist or accommodate the steering movement.

More steering load does not automatically mean the pump has failed

A hydraulic pump may become louder because another component is creating excessive resistance. Diagnose the entire load path before replacing the assistance unit.

Hydraulic PAS

Hydraulic Power-Steering Causes of Groaning

On a hydraulic steering system, groaning can develop when the pump is struggling to supply smooth hydraulic assistance or when the steering gear requires more pressure than expected.

Low Fluid

Low reservoir level can allow air into the system and reduce consistent hydraulic assistance.

Aerated Fluid

Air bubbles can create groaning, foaming and inconsistent pump behaviour.

Pump Wear

Internal wear can produce groaning or whining under steering load.

Hydraulic Restriction

Restricted hoses or internal faults can increase pressure demand and alter steering noise.

Fluid Condition

Low, Aerated or Incorrect Power-Steering Fluid

Hydraulic steering fluid transfers pressure and lubricates the pump and steering gear. Low level, contamination, aeration or incorrect fluid can all affect system behaviour.

Low Level

Indicates fluid has been lost or the system has not been filled correctly.

Foaming

Aeration can make the fluid appear bubbly or frothy in the reservoir.

Discoloured Fluid

Age, contamination or overheating may alter fluid condition.

Wrong Specification

Incorrect fluid can change seal compatibility and hydraulic performance.

Low fluid is evidence of a problem

Do not repeatedly top up the reservoir without finding why the level is dropping. A leak, poor previous repair or another hydraulic fault still needs correction.

Hydraulic Noise

Aeration and Cavitation in the PAS System

Air entering the hydraulic circuit can disrupt smooth fluid flow and create groaning or whining as the pump tries to generate assistance. Cavitation-like noise can also occur when the pump cannot receive fluid cleanly enough on its inlet side.

Foamy Reservoir

Visible bubbles can support an aeration diagnosis.

Noise Changes With Steering

The groan becomes stronger as hydraulic demand rises.

Inconsistent Assistance

Air in the system can sometimes make steering effort feel uneven.

Hydraulic Leaks

Power-Steering Fluid Leaks

Hydraulic fluid can escape from hoses, unions, pump seals, reservoir connections or the steering rack. Loss of fluid can introduce air and eventually reduce steering assistance.

Reservoir / Hose Joint

Wetness around hose connections can indicate leakage.

Pump Seal

Leakage around the pump body or shaft area requires closer inspection.

Pressure Pipe

High-pressure lines can leak at crimps, unions or corroded sections.

Hydraulic Rack

Internal or external rack leakage can reduce fluid level and contaminate surrounding components.

Power-Steering Pump

Worn or Overloaded Hydraulic PAS Pump

A worn hydraulic pump can groan or whine when the steering requires assistance, but the pump should not be condemned until fluid level, aeration, leaks, drive condition and steering resistance have been checked.

Noise Under Load

Groaning becomes stronger as steering effort rises.

Noise at Idle

Some pump faults become more noticeable during stationary steering at low engine speed.

Persistent Whine

A high-pitched hydraulic sound can accompany internal pump wear.

Assistance Change

Heavy or inconsistent steering increases concern about actual hydraulic performance.

Pump noise can be a symptom of another fault

A binding rack, top mount or suspension joint can increase steering effort and make a healthy pump work harder. Confirm the mechanical load before replacing the pump.

Belt-Driven Systems

Drive Belt and Pulley Problems Where Applicable

Some hydraulic PAS pumps are driven mechanically by the engine. On those vehicles, belt condition and pulley operation form part of the steering-noise diagnosis.

Loose / Worn Belt

A slipping belt can alter pump speed and create squeal or changing assistance.

Tensioner Problem

Poor belt control can create noise under sudden steering demand.

Pulley / Bearing Noise

Accessory-drive bearings can be mistaken for PAS pump noise.

Hydraulic Pipework

Pressure and Return Hose Restrictions

Hydraulic hoses must allow the correct fluid flow between reservoir, pump and steering gear. Internal collapse, restriction, poor routing or damaged connections can alter pressure and create noise.

Pressure Hose

Must withstand high steering-system pressure without leakage or restriction.

Return Hose

Restriction can interfere with normal fluid circulation.

Suction / Feed Side

Air ingress before the pump can create aeration without an obvious external fluid leak.

Steering Rack

Hydraulic Steering-Rack Groaning and Resistance

A hydraulic steering rack converts fluid pressure and steering input into movement of the track rods. Internal seal problems, abnormal resistance, leakage or mounting issues can create noise or uneven steering effort.

Internal Resistance

Binding inside the rack can increase pump load and produce groan.

Internal Leakage

Hydraulic pressure can bypass internally and affect assistance consistency.

External Leakage

Fluid loss around rack seals or gaiter areas can reduce reservoir level.

Rack Mountings

Abnormal housing movement can create creaking or clunking under steering load.

Rack replacement needs strong evidence

Top mounts, track rods, intermediate shafts and hydraulic supply faults can all produce symptoms around the same area. Confirm the rack itself before authorising a high-cost repair.

Electric Power Steering

EPS Motor, Reduction Gear and Control-System Noise

Pure electric power steering does not use a hydraulic pump, reservoir or PAS fluid. A groaning or mechanical assistance sound therefore requires a different fault path.

Electric Motor

High assistance demand at low speed can make motor operation more audible.

Reduction Gear

Wear or roughness can create abnormal mechanical steering noise.

Torque / Position Sensor

Faults can alter assistance behaviour and may trigger warning messages.

Electrical Supply

Low system voltage or control faults can affect steering assistance.

EPS noise and suspension groan can coexist

If the electric motor is audible but the strongest groan is at the strut tower or suspension joint, do not assume the assistance motor is the fault.

Mechanical Resistance

Mechanical Binding Can Make the Assistance System Groan

The steering-assistance system only knows how much force is required. If a joint, bearing or rack is binding, hydraulic pressure or electric motor torque must increase to overcome that resistance.

Binding Top Bearing

Resists strut rotation and increases steering effort.

Stiff Ball Joint

Adds wheel-end resistance as the knuckle turns.

Stiff Track-Rod Joint

Increases resistance through the steering linkage.

Binding Rack

Can create heavy steering and make assistance noise more obvious.

Steering Linkage

Inner and Outer Track-Rod Joints

Track rods connect the steering rack to the steering knuckles. Dry, worn or stiff joints can creak, groan or add resistance during steering.

Inner Track Rod

Noise close to the rack can be mistaken for an internal rack problem.

Outer Track-Rod End

Joint wear or stiffness can affect steering smoothness at the wheel end.

Top Mounts

Top Mount and Strut-Bearing Groaning

On many front suspension designs, the strut and coil spring rotate as the steering turns. The top bearing allows that rotation while the mount carries suspension load.

Stationary Groan

Top-mount noise can often be reproduced without the car moving.

Strut-Tower Source

The noise is normally clearer at the upper suspension than at the hydraulic pump.

Spring Wind-Up

A binding bearing can make the coil spring twist instead of rotating smoothly.

Groan Then Pop

Stored spring force can release suddenly as steering continues.

Strong top-mount evidence?

Continue into Bad Top Mount Symptoms for the dedicated upper-strut diagnosis.

Spring Movement

Coil-Spring Wind-Up and Release

A sticking strut bearing can force the spring to twist as steering input continues. The spring may groan under torsional load and then release suddenly with a pop.

Spring Twists

Steering input is stored as torsional spring movement.

Groan Builds

Friction increases as the spring resists rotation.

Spring Releases

A sudden jump or pop can follow the groaning sound.

Groan develops into a pop?

Compare with Car Pops When Turning for the dedicated spring-release diagnosis.

Suspension Joint

Ball-Joint Groaning During Steering

Ball joints allow steering and suspension movement while maintaining wheel location. Dryness, contamination, stiffness or wear can create creaking or groaning as the joint articulates.

Damaged Dust Boot

Can allow grease loss and contamination.

Dry Articulation

Friction can create a groan or creak during steering.

Excessive Play

Changes the problem from simple noise to a wheel-control concern.

Confirmed excessive ball-joint wear is safety-critical

Do not dismiss a worn suspension joint because the original symptom was only a groaning noise during parking.

Suspension Bushes

Lower-Arm and Wishbone Bush Groaning

Suspension bushes are designed to twist within a controlled range. Age, contamination, separation or abnormal loading can make them groan as the suspension and steering move.

Steering-Only Groan

Bush loading changes as tyre forces move the suspension arm.

Bump Groan Too

Suspension movement strengthening the same noise supports a bush or joint source.

Wet / Dry Change

Moisture can alter rubber-interface friction and change the symptom temporarily.

Anti-Roll Bar

Anti-Roll-Bar Bushes and Drop Links

Anti-roll-bar components are less likely to cause a flat-surface steering-only groan, but they become more relevant when turning is combined with body roll, driveway entry or uneven suspension movement.

ARB Bushes

Can creak or groan as the bar twists through its mountings.

Drop Links

Worn joints more often knock, but stiff or damaged joints can contribute to suspension noise.

Column Comparison

Steering-Column and Intermediate-Shaft Groans

Column bearings, intermediate-shaft universal joints and bulkhead interfaces can create friction noises that drivers sometimes describe as groaning rather than creaking.

Cabin-Localised

Noise is strongest behind the steering wheel or lower dashboard.

Pedal / Bulkhead Area

Intermediate-shaft joints and seals become stronger possibilities.

Notchy Steering Too

Binding U-joints or bearings require closer safety assessment.

Noise clearly inside the cabin?

Use Steering Wheel Creaks When Turning for the dedicated cabin steering-column diagnostic path.

Tyre & Surface Noise

Tyre Scrub Can Sound Like a Steering Groan

During slow tight turns, tyres can scrub against the road surface and create a low friction noise that is sometimes mistaken for steering or suspension groaning.

Surface Scrub

Tyre Against Road

Often changes with surface grip, tyre type and steering angle and may be normal during very tight manoeuvres.

Physical Contact

Tyre Against Vehicle

Rubbing against an arch liner, body or suspension component is a clearance fault and should not be treated as normal tyre scrub.

Secondary Mimics

Other Faults That Can Be Mistaken for Steering Groan

Accessory Bearing

Engine-bay bearing noise can change as the PAS pump is loaded.

Engine Mount Movement

Low-speed manoeuvring under drive can create movement mistaken for steering noise.

Brake Hardware

Pad or caliper movement can create noise during parking manoeuvres.

Wheel-Arch Liner

Loose trim can rub at large steering angles.

Body / Door Seal Creak

Body twist on driveways can mimic a chassis groan.

Subframe Movement

Abnormal movement can transmit noise through the rack and body.

Recent Repair

New noise after steering or suspension work should send diagnosis back to the disturbed area first.

Multiple Faults

An older vehicle can have hydraulic steering noise and a separate suspension groan simultaneously.

Professional Diagnosis

How a Mechanic Diagnoses a Car That Groans When Turning

A professional diagnosis should reproduce the groan before parts are removed or replaced. The technician first establishes exactly when the noise occurs, identifies the steering-assistance system fitted to the vehicle and then follows the steering load path from the steering wheel through the assistance system, rack, linkage, suspension and tyres.

This matters because a hydraulic pump can become noisy when another component is creating excessive steering resistance. Replacing the pump without finding a binding top mount, stiff joint or rack problem can leave the original fault unchanged.

Step 1

Reproduce

Confirm the exact steering angle, vehicle speed, temperature and load that produce the groan.

Step 2

Identify

Establish whether assistance is hydraulic, electro-hydraulic or electric.

Step 3

Localise

Determine whether the sound is strongest at the pump, rack, column, strut tower or wheel-end.

Step 4

Confirm

Prove the suspected component is creating the symptom before authorising replacement.

The diagnosis should explain both the noise and the steering load

Finding a noisy hydraulic pump is not enough if the pump is only complaining because a top bearing, joint or steering rack is unusually difficult to move.

Step 1

Driver Interview: Define the Groan Before Testing

A few precise questions can remove several fault paths before the vehicle reaches the workshop lift. The driver should describe the conditions that reproduce the noise rather than simply saying the power steering is noisy.

Stationary or Moving?

Establish whether wheel rotation is required.

Cold or Warm?

Ask whether the symptom changes after the car has been driven.

Left, Right or Both?

Record whether steering direction changes the noise.

Full Lock or Any Angle?

Determine exactly where in the steering sweep it begins.

Heavy Steering?

Ask about increased effort, intermittent assistance or sudden changes in steering weight.

Any Warning Light?

EPS or steering warnings materially change the diagnostic path.

Any Fluid Loss?

On hydraulic systems, ask about topping-up or fluid underneath the vehicle.

Recent Repairs?

Steering, suspension, belt, rack or subframe work can provide an important timing clue.

Step 2

Identify Hydraulic PAS, Electro-Hydraulic or EPS First

The assistance design must be confirmed before interpreting the noise. Different systems contain different components and therefore require different tests.

Hydraulic PAS

Engine-Driven Hydraulic Assistance

Typically includes hydraulic fluid, reservoir, pump, hoses and a hydraulically assisted steering gear. The pump may be driven by an engine accessory belt depending on design.

Electro-Hydraulic

Electric Pump + Hydraulic Circuit

Uses hydraulic fluid and a hydraulic steering gear, but pressure is generated by an electrically driven pump rather than a conventional engine-driven PAS pump.

EPS

Electric Motor Assistance

Uses an electric motor and control system to provide assistance. A pure EPS system has no conventional hydraulic PAS fluid circuit to inspect or bleed.

Do not diagnose from assumptions about the model

Steering systems can differ between engines, trim levels and model years. Confirm the equipment actually fitted to the vehicle.

Step 3

Stationary Steering Test

With the vehicle safely positioned, controlled steering movement can establish whether the groan occurs without wheel rotation. This is particularly useful because it removes road speed, wheel-bearing rotation and most drivetrain variables from the test.

Start Near Centre

Listen for noise during the first small steering movement.

Sweep Slowly

Turn progressively and record where the sound begins.

Compare Both Directions

Note differences between left and right steering load.

Watch Steering Feel

Identify heaviness, binding, pulsing or notchy movement.

Do not hold the steering hard against full lock

The purpose is to reproduce and localise the symptom, not to create maximum steering load for an extended period.

Step 4

Controlled Low-Speed Manoeuvring Test

The next comparison is performed while the vehicle is moving slowly in a safe area. Rolling reduces tyre scrub compared with stationary steering and introduces suspension and wheel movement.

Gentle Left Circle

Reproduce the symptom without immediately using maximum lock.

Gentle Right Circle

Compare sound, steering effort and steering angle.

Forward vs Reverse

A change can reveal tyre, suspension or drivetrain influence.

Flat vs Uneven Surface

Suspension-related groans may respond to body movement as well as steering.

Step 5

Compare Engine Speed and Steering Assistance Where Appropriate

On some conventional hydraulic systems, pump behaviour can vary with engine speed because the pump is mechanically driven. That comparison can provide evidence, but it must not be applied blindly to electro-hydraulic or pure EPS systems.

Noise Changes With RPM

On a belt-driven hydraulic system, pump or accessory-drive behaviour deserves closer investigation.

No Meaningful RPM Change

Mechanical steering or suspension causes remain fully possible.

EPS Vehicle

Diagnose electrical assistance and mechanical steering resistance rather than looking for an engine-driven PAS pump.

Step 6

Compare Left and Right Steering Sweeps

The technician should reproduce the same steering angle and similar load in both directions. A difference can help identify asymmetric mechanical resistance or a component that loads differently on one side.

Equal Both Ways

Central steering-assistance or rack-related causes remain plausible.

Much Worse One Way

Compare individual top mounts, joints and directional rack behaviour.

Different Steering Effort

Uneven assistance or mechanical binding requires further investigation.

Step 7

Compare Partial Steering Angle With Near Full Lock

Record whether the groan begins during ordinary steering movement or only when steering angle and assistance demand become high.

Groans Immediately

Look for components moving through the complete steering range.

Only Near Full Lock

High assistance load, tyre scrub, top-mount articulation and clearance issues become stronger possibilities.

Groan Then Pop

Watch carefully for strut-bearing binding and coil-spring wind-up/release.

Step 8

Reproduce the Groan Cold and Warm

If the driver reports a temperature-sensitive symptom, testing only after the car has spent an hour in the workshop can miss the condition completely.

Cold Start Test

Record hydraulic noise and steering effort before components have warmed.

Warm Comparison

Repeat the same steering movement after normal operating conditions are reached.

Record the Difference

A temperature response supports diagnosis but does not identify a component by itself.

Step 9

Inspect Hydraulic PAS Fluid and Reservoir

Where hydraulic assistance is fitted, the reservoir and fluid provide important evidence. Checks should follow the vehicle manufacturer's procedure because level-reading conditions and fluid specifications vary.

Fluid Level

Confirm whether the system is genuinely low.

Fluid Condition

Look for abnormal contamination, discolouration or foaming.

Reservoir Condition

Inspect connections and surrounding areas for leakage.

Correct Specification

Confirm that the correct fluid has been used where this can be established.

Step 10

Check for Aeration and Air Entry

Foaming or persistent bubbles in a hydraulic reservoir can support the presence of air in the system. The next job is to establish why air entered rather than simply bleeding the system and returning the vehicle.

Low Fluid

A low reservoir can allow the pump to draw air.

Suction-Side Air Entry

A poor connection can sometimes admit air without producing a dramatic external leak.

Recent Hydraulic Repair

Air may remain if the correct bleeding procedure was not completed.

Step 11

Trace the Hydraulic Leak Instead of Guessing

A low PAS reservoir requires systematic inspection. Fluid can travel across surrounding components, so the wettest visible area is not always the original leak point.

Reservoir & Feed

Check the reservoir body, cap area, hose and connections.

Pump

Inspect the pump body, connections and relevant seal areas.

Pressure / Return Lines

Check unions, crimps, flexible sections and corroded pipework.

Steering Rack

Inspect rack connections, housing and gaiter areas where appropriate.

Step 12

Confirm Whether the Groan Is Actually Coming From the PAS Pump

The technician should listen for where the noise physically originates while steering demand changes. Engine-bay sound transmission can make accessory bearings, belt components and hydraulic noise difficult to separate from the driver's seat.

Sound at Pump

Strong localisation supports pump or hydraulic-flow investigation.

Sound at Strut Tower

Move attention towards the top mount, bearing and spring.

Sound at Rack / Bulkhead

Investigate rack, steering input and intermediate-shaft areas.

A noisy pump is not automatically a worn pump

Check fluid supply, aeration, restrictions and excessive mechanical steering load before condemning the pump internally.

Step 13

Check the Drive Belt, Tensioner and Pulley Where Applicable

If the hydraulic pump is belt-driven, the accessory drive becomes part of the diagnosis. Steering demand can expose a marginal belt, tensioner or pulley problem.

Belt Condition

Inspect for deterioration, contamination and abnormal wear.

Belt Tension

Incorrect control can allow slip as pump load increases.

Tensioner Behaviour

Abnormal movement can reveal an accessory-drive problem.

Pulley / Bearing

Bearing noise should be distinguished from internal pump noise.

Step 14

Check Hydraulic Hoses for Restriction, Damage and Air Entry

A hose can create a hydraulic problem even when it is not visibly pouring fluid onto the ground. Inspection should consider routing, deterioration, kinking, internal restriction and connection integrity.

Feed Side

Ensure the pump can receive fluid without restriction or air entry.

Pressure Side

Look for damaged, leaking or incorrectly routed pressure lines.

Return Side

Confirm normal fluid return without obvious restriction or collapse.

Step 15

Determine Whether the Steering Rack Is Creating Resistance

The rack becomes a stronger suspect when steering is uneven, notchy or abnormally heavy and the symptom localises around the rack rather than the pump or upper suspension.

Rough Through Sweep

Look for resistance that changes as rack position changes.

Directional Difference

Compare effort and noise turning left and right.

Leak Evidence

On hydraulic racks, inspect for external evidence of fluid loss.

Mounting Movement

Confirm the rack housing remains properly located under load.

Do not condemn the rack until external resistance has been excluded

Track-rod joints, ball joints, top bearings and steering-shaft components can create resistance that feels as though it is inside the rack.

Step 16

EPS Scan and Mechanical Diagnosis

Electric power steering requires both electronic and mechanical diagnosis. A diagnostic trouble code can identify an electrical or control problem, but mechanical resistance elsewhere can also make the EPS system work harder than expected.

Warning Indicators

Record EPS or steering warnings before clearing any faults.

Diagnostic Codes

Read relevant steering-control fault information where supported.

Electrical Supply

Check battery and charging-system condition where relevant to the reported EPS problem.

Mechanical Load

Exclude binding rack, top mounts and steering or suspension joints.

A fault code is evidence, not permission to skip mechanical checks

The complete diagnosis should establish whether an EPS fault caused the abnormal steering behaviour or whether excessive mechanical resistance contributed to the problem.

Step 17

Observe the Top Mount, Strut Bearing and Coil Spring

With suitable safety precautions and the suspension appropriately loaded, steering movement can be observed at the upper strut and coil spring. The assembly should rotate as designed rather than storing steering movement as excessive spring twist.

Smooth Rotation

The strut and spring move progressively with steering input.

Spring Winds Up

A sticking bearing becomes a strong possibility.

Groan at Tower

Strong localisation supports upper-strut investigation.

Sudden Release

A jump or pop can indicate stored spring torque releasing.

Step 18

Isolate Ball Joints and Lower-Arm Bushes

If the groan localises lower down, suspension joints and bushes should be assessed in the conditions in which they normally operate. Unloaded movement alone may not reproduce a fault that appears with vehicle weight on the suspension.

Ball-Joint Boot

Look for splitting, contamination or grease loss.

Ball-Joint Movement

Check for roughness, stiffness or excessive play.

Bush Condition

Look for tearing, separation or abnormal displacement.

Loaded Reproduction

Compare the suspect component while the original groan is being reproduced safely.

Step 19

Compare the Steering Column and Intermediate Shaft

If the sound is strongest around the cabin, pedals or bulkhead, trace steering input through the upper column, lower column and intermediate shaft rather than assuming the rack is transmitting the noise.

Upper Column

Listen for bearing or friction noise behind the steering wheel.

Lower Column / Bulkhead

Check where the steering shaft passes towards the engine bay.

Intermediate Shaft

Check universal joints and sliding sections for abnormal stiffness or movement.

Step 20

Separate Normal Tyre Scrub From Physical Tyre Contact

Tight low-speed steering can make tyres scrub across a high-grip surface. That should not be confused with the tyre physically rubbing an arch liner, suspension component or bodywork.

Changes With Road Surface

Supports tyre-to-road scrub rather than an internal steering fault.

Visible Rub Marks

Indicates actual contact requiring a clearance investigation.

Modified Wheel / Tyre Setup

Check wheel offset, tyre size and suspension changes where relevant.

Diagnostic Mistakes

Why Groaning Steering Gets Misdiagnosed

Groaning steering is particularly vulnerable to parts-swapping because several components are loaded simultaneously when the wheel is turned.

“It Must Be the Pump”

Pump noise may be caused by low fluid, aeration or excessive mechanical steering resistance.

“It Needs PAS Fluid”

Pure EPS vehicles have no conventional hydraulic PAS fluid to top up.

“The Rack Is Noisy”

Track rods, top mounts and intermediate shafts can transmit sound into the rack area.

“The Top Mount Looks Fine”

A bearing can bind under load without presenting an obvious visual defect from above.

Testing Only on a Lift

Removing suspension load can make the original symptom disappear.

Ignoring Temperature

A cold-only groan may be absent by the time workshop inspection begins.

Ignoring Recent Repairs

A symptom beginning immediately after work deserves inspection of the disturbed components first.

Replacing Before Confirming

Expensive pumps, racks and EPS units require stronger evidence than a similar noise description.

Do not lubricate random steering or suspension components simply to make the noise disappear

Temporary noise reduction does not prove that a component is safe, and inappropriate lubricant can damage some rubber components or conceal the symptom without correcting wear.

Final Diagnostic Step

Verify the Repair Under the Original Conditions

A repair is not confirmed merely because a new component has been fitted. The original test conditions should be repeated and the steering system checked for correct operation.

Repeat Stationary Test

Use the same steering sweep that originally reproduced the groan.

Repeat Road Test

Recheck low-speed left and right manoeuvres.

Confirm Steering Feel

Steering should be smooth, predictable and appropriately assisted.

Recheck for Leaks

Hydraulic repairs should remain dry and maintain the correct fluid level.

Confirm Fluid Condition

Check that persistent aeration has not returned.

Check Warning Status

EPS or steering warnings should be investigated rather than simply cleared.

Check Steering Centre

Confirm steering-wheel position and geometry where relevant to the repair performed.

Listen for Second Fault

Removing one loud groan can expose a quieter independent steering or suspension noise.

Severity Guide

How Serious Is a Groaning Noise When Turning?

Severity should be judged from steering operation and the confirmed defect, not from how loud the groan sounds.

Light Stable Groan

Steering remains smooth, predictable and normally assisted with no obvious leakage or warning.

Priority: Book Diagnosis

Groan Getting Worse

Noise is becoming more frequent or louder, but steering remains controllable and consistent.

Priority: Inspect Soon

Groan + Heavy / Notchy Steering

Steering effort or smoothness has changed, suggesting assistance loss or mechanical binding.

Priority: Urgent

Steering Control Affected

Severe binding, unpredictable assistance, major leakage or significant steering irregularity is present.

Priority: Stop & Assess

Safe-To-Drive Decision

Is It Safe to Drive a Car That Groans When Turning?

A groaning noise does not have one universal safe-to-drive answer. Some cars develop a relatively minor noise from a bush, top mount or tyre scrub while steering remains completely normal. Others groan because hydraulic fluid is being lost, steering assistance is deteriorating or a steering or suspension joint is binding.

The decision should therefore be based on steering behaviour, fluid loss, warning lights, the rate at which the symptom is changing and any confirmed mechanical wear — not simply on how loud the groan sounds.

Lower Immediate Risk

Light, Stable Groan

Steering remains smooth and predictable, assistance feels normal, there is no significant fluid loss and the symptom is not rapidly worsening.

Book Diagnosis
Needs Attention

Groan Becoming Worse

The noise is increasing, occurring through more of the steering range or is now accompanied by minor changes in steering feel.

Inspect Soon
High Concern

Heavy or Notchy Steering

Steering effort has increased, movement feels jerky or notchy, or assistance appears inconsistent.

Urgent Diagnosis
Stop & Assess

Steering Control Is Affected

Severe binding, sudden assistance loss, substantial hydraulic leakage, abnormal free play or unpredictable steering behaviour requires immediate assessment.

Do Not Continue Normally
Stop driving normally if steering operation has materially changed

A steering noise accompanied by sudden heaviness, severe notching, unpredictable assistance, significant looseness or obvious steering-component damage should not be treated as an ordinary nuisance noise.

If You Ignore It

What Happens If You Ignore a Groaning Steering Noise?

The outcome depends entirely on the cause. Tyre scrub on a suitable vehicle during a tight manoeuvre may require no repair, while a hydraulic leak, binding joint or deteriorating steering component can progress into a much more expensive or safety-relevant problem.

Fluid Level Falls

An unresolved hydraulic leak can continue lowering the PAS fluid level and allow air into the system.

Pump Works Harder

Poor fluid supply, aeration or excessive steering resistance can increase hydraulic pump stress.

Assistance Can Deteriorate

Hydraulic or electric assistance faults can eventually affect steering effort or consistency.

Joint Wear Can Progress

A dry, contaminated or worn steering or suspension joint may deteriorate further with continued use.

Bush Damage Can Increase

Deteriorating suspension bushes can permit increasing movement and affect wheel control or geometry.

Tyres May Wear Unevenly

Steering or suspension wear that alters geometry can contribute to abnormal tyre wear.

Repair Costs Can Rise

A relatively small leak, boot defect or worn joint can become a larger repair if deterioration continues.

MOT Problems Can Develop

Excessive wear, insecure components, significant leakage or steering defects can become relevant during an MOT inspection.

Hydraulic Consequences

Why Hydraulic PAS Fluid Loss Should Not Be Ignored

Hydraulic power steering relies on an adequate supply of the correct fluid. If a leak continues, the system can become aerated and the pump may operate with an inadequate or inconsistent fluid supply.

Stage 1 — Leak Begins

Fluid escapes through a hose, connection, pump or steering-rack fault.

Stage 2 — Air Enters

Falling fluid level or suction-side problems can introduce air and increase groaning.

Stage 3 — Assistance Suffers

Continued deterioration can affect hydraulic performance and steering effort.

Repeatedly topping up PAS fluid is not a completed repair

If the fluid level falls again, the cause of the loss still needs to be found. Correct diagnosis should identify the leak or air-entry point and then confirm that the system operates normally after repair.

Mechanical Consequences

What Happens If a Binding Steering or Suspension Part Is Ignored?

Mechanical resistance is particularly important because it can both create the groaning noise and increase the workload placed on the steering-assistance system.

Top Bearing Binds

The coil spring may wind up rather than rotating smoothly with the strut.

Joint Becomes Stiffer

A deteriorating ball joint or steering joint can increase steering resistance.

Wear Becomes Looseness

Some joints progress from stiffness or noise into excessive movement as deterioration continues.

Geometry Can Change

Worn bushes and joints can affect wheel position and tyre wear.

Repair Priorities

Which Groaning-Steering Repairs Need Priority?

Hydraulic

PAS Fluid, Hoses & Pump

Correct the reason for fluid loss or aeration first. A pump should only be replaced once supply problems and excessive mechanical load have been excluded.

Steering

Rack, Track Rods & Shaft

Binding, excessive play or insecure steering components require greater priority than a harmless friction noise.

Suspension

Top Mounts, Ball Joints & Arms

Judge urgency from the confirmed wear, movement and effect on wheel control rather than noise volume alone.

Wheel Alignment

Will the Car Need Wheel Alignment After Repair?

It depends on which components were disturbed or replaced. Track-rod, steering-rack and some suspension repairs can affect wheel geometry, so alignment should be checked where the repair procedure or measured geometry requires it.

Track-Rod Work

Toe setting can change directly when steering-linkage length is altered.

Rack / Suspension Work

Geometry should be assessed where component position has been disturbed.

Tyre Wear Already Present

Existing abnormal wear provides another reason to inspect geometry after mechanical faults are corrected.

Alignment is not a repair for worn steering or suspension parts

Mechanical defects should be corrected first. Adjusting geometry around excessive joint or bush movement can produce misleading measurements and does not remove the underlying fault.

UK MOT

Can a Car Fail Its MOT for Groaning When Turning?

The noise itself is not the diagnosis. What matters is whether the underlying steering or suspension condition falls within an MOT inspection item and is defective to the relevant level.

Steering operation, steering components, suspension components and relevant fluid leaks can therefore matter if the groan is being caused by an actual defect.

Steering Free Play

Excessive wear or movement in steering components can become an MOT concern.

Binding / Operation

A steering defect affecting safe or proper operation requires attention regardless of noise.

Suspension Wear

Excessively worn or insecure joints, bushes and mountings can be relevant to the inspection.

Fluid Leakage

The location and severity of a leak determine its significance; do not assume every trace of fluid has the same outcome.

Diagnose the defect, not the sound

Two cars can make a similar groaning noise but have completely different MOT implications. One may have ordinary tyre scrub while another has excessive steering-joint wear.

Used-Car Buying Risk

Should You Buy a Used Car That Groans When Turning?

Treat an unexplained steering groan as a diagnostic clue, not as proof of either a cheap or expensive repair. The important question is whether the seller can demonstrate the cause.

Light Tyre Scrub

May be normal on a tight manoeuvre if there is no physical tyre contact and steering operation is normal.

Buying Risk: Low

Top-Mount / Bush Noise

Potentially manageable, but obtain a confirmed diagnosis and repair estimate.

Buying Risk: Medium

Hydraulic Groan + Leak

Do not assume the repair is only a fluid top-up; pump, hose or rack costs may be involved.

Buying Risk: High

Heavy / Notchy Steering

Requires proper diagnosis before purchase because steering assistance or mechanical binding may be involved.

Buying Risk: High
“They all do that” is not a diagnosis

If a seller dismisses a steering groan, reproduce it yourself and obtain independent evidence. A noise that seems minor during a five-minute viewing can conceal a hydraulic leak, top-mount problem, steering-joint fault or expensive rack issue.

MOT-History Clues

What to Look for in the MOT History

MOT history cannot diagnose the current groan, but repeated steering, suspension and tyre observations can provide useful context when assessing a used car.

Steering-Joint Wear

Repeated observations involving steering components deserve closer inspection.

Suspension Bush / Joint Wear

Previous deterioration may relate to the current noise if repairs cannot be demonstrated.

Fluid-Leak History

Relevant leak observations can justify a more detailed underside inspection.

Repeated Tyre Wear

Recurring abnormal wear can support checking suspension condition and wheel geometry.

Use MOT history as evidence, not as a substitute for inspection

An old advisory may already have been repaired, while a current fault may not appear anywhere in the previous MOT record.

Prevention

Can Groaning Steering Problems Be Prevented?

Normal component ageing cannot be eliminated, but early attention to leaks, damaged protective boots, abnormal tyre wear and changing steering feel can prevent smaller defects from being ignored until they become larger repairs.

Investigate Fluid Loss

Do not allow a hydraulic system to remain repeatedly low.

Repair Damaged Boots

Protective boots help keep contamination away from steering and suspension joints.

Act on Steering Changes

New heaviness, notching or inconsistent assistance deserves investigation.

Watch Tyre Wear

Uneven wear can reveal developing geometry or suspension issues.

Use Correct Fluids

Hydraulic steering systems should use the specified fluid and service procedure.

Inspect After Impacts

Pothole or kerb impacts can affect steering and suspension components.

Verify Repairs

A noise appearing immediately after work should be investigated rather than accepted as normal.

Diagnose Before Replacing

Accurate fault confirmation prevents unnecessary pumps, racks and suspension parts being fitted.

Guide Summary

Car Groans When Turning: Key Takeaways

Identify the Steering System First

Hydraulic PAS, electro-hydraulic assistance and EPS require different diagnostic paths.

Do Not Blame the Pump Too Early

Low fluid, aeration, hose restriction or mechanical binding can make a hydraulic pump groan under load.

Check Where the Noise Lives

Pump, rack, cabin, strut tower and wheel-end localisation can separate very different causes.

Top Mounts Are a Major Mimic

A binding strut bearing can groan during steering and make the assistance system work harder.

Steering Feel Determines Urgency

Heavy, notchy or unpredictable steering is more concerning than a light stable noise with otherwise normal operation.

Fluid Loss Needs a Cause

Repeatedly topping up hydraulic PAS fluid does not repair the leak or air-entry problem.

Mechanical Wear Matters

Ball joints, track rods, lower-arm bushes and steering components can create noise while also affecting wheel control.

MOT Depends on the Defect

The groan itself is not the key issue; the condition of the underlying steering or suspension component is what matters.

Prove the Fault Before Buying Parts

Pumps, steering racks and EPS assemblies can be expensive. Evidence-based diagnosis helps prevent unnecessary replacement.

The best diagnosis connects the sound, steering behaviour and physical evidence

Do not stop at “the car groans when turning”. Establish whether the noise is hydraulic, electric-assistance related, steering mechanical, suspension related or simply tyre interaction — and confirm the responsible component before repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Car Groans When Turning: FAQs

Answers to common questions about hydraulic power steering, electric power steering, steering racks, top mounts, suspension joints and groaning noises when turning.

Why does my car groan when I turn the steering wheel?

A car can groan when turning because of low or aerated power steering fluid, a worn hydraulic power steering pump, steering rack problems, dry or worn suspension joints, a binding top mount or strut bearing, or excessive load within the steering system. The exact cause depends on where the noise comes from and whether the vehicle uses hydraulic or electric power steering.

Can low power steering fluid cause a groaning noise?

Yes. On vehicles with hydraulic power steering, low fluid can allow air into the system and cause the pump to groan or whine as the steering wheel is turned. Low fluid should not simply be topped up repeatedly because the system may have a leak that needs to be found and repaired.

Why does my car groan when turning at low speed?

Groaning at parking or low speeds often occurs because steering load is highest when the tyres are moving slowly against the road surface. Hydraulic power steering pump problems, low fluid, steering rack resistance, top mount bearings and suspension joints should all be considered.

Why does my steering groan when the car is stationary?

A groan while stationary can point towards the power steering assistance system, steering rack, top mounts or other components that move as the steering is turned. Because tyre resistance is high while stationary, existing steering faults can become much easier to hear.

Why does my car groan on full lock?

Steering load increases near full lock, particularly on hydraulic power steering systems. A brief change in pump noise may occur on some vehicles, but loud, persistent or worsening groaning can indicate low fluid, pump wear, air in the hydraulic system, excessive steering resistance or another developing fault.

Can a bad power steering pump cause groaning?

Yes. A worn hydraulic power steering pump can groan or whine when steering assistance is demanded. Before replacing the pump, a mechanic should check fluid level and condition, leaks, trapped air, drive-belt condition where applicable and whether another steering fault is placing excessive load on the pump.

Can a steering rack cause a groaning noise?

Yes. Internal steering rack wear, hydraulic leakage, mounting problems or abnormal resistance can contribute to groaning or other steering noises. Rack faults are more likely when the noise is accompanied by heavy, uneven, notchy or inconsistent steering.

Can bad top mounts cause groaning when turning?

Yes. On many front suspension designs the strut and spring rotate as the steering turns. A worn, dry or seized top mount bearing can resist this movement and produce groaning, creaking, knocking or spring wind-up during steering.

Can ball joints cause a groaning noise when turning?

Worn, dry or damaged ball joints can produce creaking or groaning as the suspension and steering move. Torn dust boots can allow lubricant to escape and contamination to enter, accelerating joint wear.

Can suspension bushes groan when steering?

Yes. Rubber suspension bushes can groan or creak as they twist under steering and suspension load. Bush noise may change with temperature, moisture, vehicle movement or suspension position, so it should be distinguished from a power steering or steering rack fault.

Can electric power steering cause a groaning noise?

Electric power steering can produce motor or reduction-gear noises, but it does not use hydraulic power steering fluid. A vehicle with EPS therefore requires a different diagnostic approach that includes the electric motor, steering column or rack assembly, electrical supply, fault codes and mechanical steering resistance.

Why does the steering groan more when the engine is cold?

Cold conditions can make hydraulic fluid more resistant to flow and can change the behaviour of rubber bushes, seals and bearings. A fault that is marginal when warm may therefore become more noticeable during the first few steering movements after a cold start.

Why does my steering groan after rain?

Moisture can temporarily change friction between rubber bushes, suspension components and their contact surfaces. If the noise changes significantly between wet and dry conditions, suspension bushes, top mounts and other mechanical joints should be considered alongside the steering system.

Is it safe to drive with a groaning steering noise?

A mild groan does not identify the severity of the fault by itself. If the steering remains smooth and normal, the vehicle should still be inspected before the problem worsens. If steering becomes heavy, jerky, notchy or inconsistent, a warning light appears, fluid is leaking or steering control feels affected, further driving should be avoided until the fault has been assessed.

Can groaning steering fail an MOT?

The noise itself is not normally the reason for an MOT failure, but the defect causing it can be. Excessive roughness, worn steering joints, insecure components, power steering malfunction, significant fluid leakage or an electronic power steering malfunction can result in an MOT defect.

How does a mechanic diagnose groaning when turning?

A mechanic first confirms when the noise occurs and where it is strongest, then identifies whether the vehicle uses hydraulic or electric power steering. Checks can include fluid condition and leaks, steering assistance, pump noise, steering rack operation, track rods, ball joints, top mounts, springs, suspension bushes and electronic fault codes where applicable.

How much does it cost to repair a groaning steering noise?

Repair cost depends entirely on the cause. A minor fluid or bleeding issue can be relatively inexpensive, while replacement of a hydraulic pump, steering rack, EPS assembly, top mount or suspension component can cost substantially more. Accurate diagnosis should therefore come before replacing parts.

Should I buy a used car that groans when turning?

A used car that groans when turning should be diagnosed before purchase rather than assuming the noise is harmless. The cause may be relatively minor, but steering rack, power steering, EPS or suspension repairs can be expensive. Heavy steering, warning lights, fluid leaks or obvious steering irregularities should be treated as significant buying risks.
