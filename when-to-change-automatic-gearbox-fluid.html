Most drivers remember to change their engine oil, but automatic gearbox fluid is often overlooked. This UK guide explains when automatic gearbox fluid should be changed, the warning signs to watch for, what manufacturers recommend, whether a flush is necessary and how much servicing typically costs.

Quick answer

When should automatic gearbox fluid be changed?

Automatic gearbox fluid should be changed according to the manufacturer's schedule for your exact vehicle and transmission. There is no single mileage interval that applies to every automatic car. Some manufacturers specify routine changes, while others describe the transmission as sealed for life or do not list a normal-service fluid replacement interval.

As a general maintenance discussion point, some transmission specialists recommend considering a fluid service at around 40,000–60,000 miles on suitable gearboxes, particularly with heavy stop-start driving or towing. This is not a universal manufacturer recommendation. Certain DSG, CVT and other automatic transmissions require different intervals, fluids and procedures.

When to change it

Check the manufacturer's mileage and time limits first. Review driving conditions, service history and gearbox type before choosing a maintenance interval.

Signs to investigate

Jerky shifts, delayed engagement, slipping, unusual noises, a burning smell or a transmission warning light deserve diagnosis. They do not automatically mean the fluid is the only problem.

What matters most

Use the exact approved fluid and correct filling procedure. Fluid level, temperature and transmission design can all affect whether the service is carried out properly.

Mechanic insight:

A healthy automatic gearbox should not need to develop symptoms before its scheduled fluid service. Preventive maintenance is usually more sensible than waiting for harsh gear changes. However, if the gearbox is already slipping badly, overheating or making serious mechanical noises, arrange a diagnosis before authorising a routine fluid change or machine flush. Fresh fluid cannot repair worn internal components.

Before booking a gearbox fluid service

Ask the garage to confirm the gearbox identification, approved fluid specification, manufacturer servicing procedure, whether a filter or sump replacement is required and the total price. A quote for a basic drain-and-refill may not include all the work needed for your transmission.

Understanding gearbox maintenance

Why Automatic Gearbox Fluid Maintenance Matters

Automatic gearbox fluid is one of the most important maintenance items in an automatic transmission. Unlike engine oil, which most drivers know needs regular replacement, gearbox fluid is often overlooked because some manufacturers describe their transmissions as sealed for life or do not include a routine fluid change in the normal servicing schedule.

However, automatic gearbox fluid operates in demanding conditions. It is exposed to heat, pressure, friction material, internal component wear and repeated changes in transmission load. Over time, its properties can deteriorate, particularly when the vehicle is used for frequent short journeys, heavy traffic, towing or prolonged high-load driving.

The important point is that not every automatic gearbox has the same servicing requirements. A conventional torque-converter automatic, a dual-clutch transmission and a continuously variable transmission (CVT) may require different fluids, maintenance intervals and filling procedures.

Conventional automatic

Many conventional automatic gearboxes use a torque converter, hydraulic control system and internal clutch packs. Their automatic transmission fluid must provide the correct hydraulic, lubrication and friction characteristics.

Dual-clutch gearbox

DSG and other dual-clutch gearboxes use two clutch systems to prepare and select gears. Wet-clutch and dry-clutch designs have different lubrication arrangements and servicing requirements.

CVT gearbox

Many CVTs use a belt or chain operating between variable-diameter pulleys. Their fluid requires carefully controlled friction characteristics and must meet the correct CVT specification.

Why gearbox fluid is different from engine oil

Engine oil mainly lubricates, cools and protects engine components while carrying contamination towards the filtration system. Automatic gearbox fluid can perform several additional functions, including transferring hydraulic pressure, controlling clutch engagement and providing the correct friction behaviour for internal transmission components.

This is why using an incorrect gearbox fluid can be particularly damaging. Two transmission fluids may look similar but have different additive packages, viscosity characteristics and friction properties. The wrong product can affect gear engagement, clutch operation, lubrication and long-term reliability.

Important fluid compatibility warning

Never choose automatic gearbox fluid by colour alone. Red, amber, green and other fluid colours are not reliable indicators of compatibility. Always identify the exact transmission and use fluid meeting the manufacturer's specified approval.

Does every automatic gearbox need a fluid change?

Not every manufacturer specifies a routine fluid replacement for every automatic transmission. Some gearboxes have defined mileage or time-based service intervals, while others are described as lifetime-filled under particular operating conditions.

That does not mean all automatic gearbox fluid remains in perfect condition indefinitely. It means the servicing decision must be based on the actual transmission, the manufacturer's maintenance policy, vehicle use, service history and mechanical condition.

For a vehicle with a clear scheduled service interval, follow that schedule. For a lifetime-fill transmission, consult the manufacturer or a competent transmission specialist before deciding whether preventive servicing is appropriate.

Mechanic insight:

When checking a used automatic car, do not assume that a stamped engine service book proves the gearbox has been serviced. Ask specifically for invoices showing the transmission fluid specification, date, mileage and work completed.

Transmission fluid functions

What Does Automatic Gearbox Fluid Actually Do?

Automatic gearbox fluid does much more than simply lubricate moving parts. Depending on the transmission design, it can transfer hydraulic pressure, help operate clutches, remove heat, protect bearings and gears, and control friction between internal components.

Understanding these functions helps explain why fluid condition, level and specification are so important to gearbox reliability.

1. Lubrication

Fluid creates a protective film between moving components such as gears, bearings, shafts and bushes, helping reduce friction and mechanical wear.

2. Hydraulic pressure

In many conventional automatics, fluid pressure is used to apply internal clutch packs and control gear selection. Incorrect pressure can affect engagement and shift quality.

3. Cooling

Transmission fluid absorbs heat from internal components and carries it towards the gearbox casing or cooling system, depending on the design.

4. Friction control

Special additives help provide the intended friction characteristics for clutch materials, torque-converter lock-up systems and other transmission components.

5. Component protection

Additives help limit corrosion, oxidation, foaming and wear, protecting internal components during normal transmission operation.

6. Contamination management

Fluid carries small amounts of wear material through the transmission. Filters, strainers and magnets may help capture debris, depending on gearbox design.

What happens as gearbox fluid ages?

Heat and repeated operating cycles can gradually change the fluid's properties. Additives may become depleted, oxidation products can accumulate and friction characteristics may change. Wear particles can also build up within the transmission.

In severe cases, deteriorated fluid may contribute to poor lubrication, overheating, hydraulic control problems or abnormal clutch operation. However, symptoms such as harsh shifting or slipping can also result from faulty solenoids, worn clutches, mechanical damage, software issues or incorrect fluid level.

A fluid change may be appropriate maintenance, but it should not be treated as a guaranteed repair for an existing gearbox fault.

What does healthy automatic gearbox fluid look like?

Fluid appearance varies between manufacturers and transmission types. Some fluids are red when new, while others may be amber, green or another colour. Normal darkening can occur during use, so colour alone cannot reliably establish whether a fluid change is due.

Normal service observations

Fluid may gradually darken with age while the transmission continues to operate normally. Maintenance decisions should still follow the correct service schedule and inspection procedure.

Signs requiring investigation

A strong burnt smell, obvious metallic particles, significant contamination, emulsified fluid or evidence of overheating can indicate a problem that needs diagnosis.

Do not diagnose a gearbox by fluid colour alone

Dark fluid does not automatically prove that the transmission is failing, and clean-looking fluid does not guarantee that the gearbox is healthy. Consider the service history, operating symptoms, fluid level, fault codes and manufacturer inspection requirements together.

Recommended servicing intervals

How Often Should Automatic Gearbox Fluid Be Changed in the UK?

The correct automatic gearbox fluid change interval depends on the exact transmission, vehicle manufacturer, driving conditions and maintenance schedule. There is no single mileage figure that is suitable for every automatic car.

Some gearboxes require scheduled servicing at specific mileage or time intervals. Others are described as sealed for life, while certain manufacturers specify additional servicing under severe operating conditions.

Is 40,000 to 60,000 miles a sensible interval?

Around 40,000–60,000 miles is sometimes discussed as a preventive servicing range by transmission specialists for suitable gearboxes. However, this is not a universal automatic gearbox service interval and should never override the manufacturer's instructions.

A transmission requiring a service at a shorter interval should not be left until 60,000 miles. Equally, a gearbox with a different manufacturer policy should not automatically receive a fluid change simply because it has reached 40,000 miles.

Driving conditions that may affect fluid life

Heavy stop-start traffic

Frequent low-speed operation and repeated gear engagement can increase heat and operating demands in some transmission designs.

Towing and heavy loads

Towing, carrying heavy loads and climbing long gradients can place additional thermal and mechanical demands on the transmission.

High-mileage use

Frequent long-distance driving increases operating hours and fluid exposure to repeated heating and cooling cycles.

Frequent short journeys

Repeated short journeys may prevent some transmissions from reaching stable operating conditions for long periods.

Performance driving

Hard acceleration, repeated high-load operation and demanding driving can increase transmission temperatures.

Previous overheating

A history of transmission overheating may justify additional inspection and specialist advice rather than simply waiting for the next scheduled service.

2. Check the schedule

Review the official maintenance schedule for the vehicle, model year, engine and gearbox combination.

3. Review service history

Look for gearbox-specific invoices showing the fluid, filter, mileage and service procedure used.

4. Confirm with a specialist

If the schedule is unclear, ask a competent transmission specialist to confirm the correct service requirements.

Should you change gearbox fluid if the service history is unknown?

An unknown gearbox service history deserves careful assessment, particularly on a high-mileage automatic car. The right decision depends on whether the gearbox operates normally, whether servicing is overdue and whether there is evidence of existing damage.

If the gearbox is operating correctly and the manufacturer specifies a service, a correctly performed fluid change may be appropriate. If the transmission already slips severely, has delayed engagement, overheats or contains significant debris, it should be diagnosed before deciding on a fluid service.

Mechanic insight:

Before authorising a gearbox fluid change, ask the garage whether the quote includes the correct approved fluid, filter or strainer where required, sump gasket or replacement sump if applicable, and the correct temperature-controlled level-setting procedure. The cheapest quote is not always the most complete service.

Important maintenance reminder

Do not apply a generic mileage interval or DSG servicing procedure to every automatic gearbox. The exact transmission identification and manufacturer specification must determine the maintenance plan.

Gearbox warning signs

Signs Your Automatic Gearbox Fluid May Need Changing

Automatic gearbox fluid can deteriorate gradually through heat, oxidation and normal transmission operation. However, an automatic gearbox may also develop symptoms because of low fluid level, incorrect fluid, worn clutch packs, faulty solenoids, valve-body problems or internal mechanical damage.

The important distinction is that a gearbox symptom is a reason to investigate, not proof that a fluid change will fix the problem. A scheduled fluid service helps maintain a healthy transmission, while an existing fault may require separate diagnosis and repair.

1. Jerky gear changes

Harsh or sudden gear changes can occur when hydraulic pressure, clutch control or transmission operation is abnormal. Low or incorrect fluid may contribute, but electronic and mechanical faults must also be considered.

Vibration or shudder under load can be associated with torque-converter lock-up operation, clutch behaviour or other transmission and drivetrain problems.

6. Unusual whining noises

Whining noises may involve pumps, bearings, gears or other components. Low fluid can sometimes contribute, but noise alone does not establish the cause.

Burnt-smelling or contaminated gearbox fluid

A strong burnt smell can suggest that transmission fluid has been exposed to excessive heat. Significant metallic debris, abnormal contamination or evidence of coolant mixing with transmission fluid can also indicate a problem requiring investigation.

Some transmission fluids naturally darken during service, and their original colours vary. Dark fluid alone is not a reliable diagnosis of internal gearbox damage or proof that a service is overdue.

Fluid observations that need checking

Strong burnt odour, unusual contamination, obvious metallic particles or fluid that appears emulsified should prompt a specialist inspection. The significance depends on the transmission design and operating history.

Fluid observations that are inconclusive

Gradual colour changes and normal fine material collected by transmission magnets do not automatically mean the gearbox is failing. They must be interpreted alongside the service history and symptoms.

What if the gearbox warning light comes on?

A gearbox warning light or transmission fault message can indicate an electronic, hydraulic, temperature-related or mechanical problem. It does not automatically mean the fluid needs changing.

Some vehicles may enter a protective operating mode, restrict gear selection or display a transmission temperature warning. Fault codes and live diagnostic information can help identify the system reporting the problem.

Risks of overdue maintenance

What Happens If You Do Not Change Automatic Gearbox Fluid?

If a manufacturer specifies regular automatic gearbox fluid replacement and that maintenance is repeatedly missed, the fluid may remain in service beyond its intended operating period. Over time, heat, oxidation, contamination and additive deterioration can reduce its ability to perform as designed.

The consequences depend on the transmission, operating conditions and existing mechanical condition. Some gearboxes continue operating normally for considerable mileages, while others may develop wear or performance problems sooner.

How neglected gearbox fluid can affect the transmission

Reduced lubrication protection

Deteriorated fluid may provide less effective protection under demanding conditions, increasing the risk of wear to bearings, gears and other moving parts.

Higher operating temperatures

Fluid deterioration and transmission faults can contribute to thermal stress. Excessive heat can accelerate further fluid degradation and damage sensitive components.

Changes in shift quality

Changes in fluid friction characteristics or hydraulic performance may affect gear engagement and shift behaviour in some transmission designs.

Contamination build-up

Wear material and deposits can accumulate over time. Depending on the gearbox, filters, strainers and magnets may capture some of this contamination.

Hydraulic control problems

Contamination or abnormal fluid properties may contribute to problems affecting valves, solenoids and hydraulic passages.

Expensive internal repairs

Serious transmission damage can require clutch-pack repairs, valve-body work, rebuilding or complete gearbox replacement.

Does old gearbox fluid always cause transmission failure?

No. Automatic gearbox failure can occur for many reasons, including design-related problems, component wear, cooling faults, electrical failures, incorrect servicing and manufacturing defects.

Equally, a gearbox that has missed fluid services does not automatically need replacing. The appropriate response is to assess its condition and follow the correct maintenance or repair procedure.

Can changing old gearbox fluid cause problems?

A correctly performed fluid service should not normally damage a healthy gearbox. However, changing fluid in a transmission that is already badly worn may not resolve its symptoms, and the gearbox may continue to deteriorate afterwards.

Problems can also arise if the wrong fluid is used, the level is set incorrectly, contamination is introduced or an unsuitable flushing procedure is carried out.

On a high-mileage gearbox with unknown maintenance history, especially one already showing slipping or delayed engagement, a specialist should assess the transmission before recommending a service.

Healthy gearbox with overdue servicing

Confirm the manufacturer schedule, correct fluid and service procedure. Preventive servicing may be appropriate where recommended for that transmission.

Gearbox already showing serious faults

Diagnose the fault before authorising a fluid change or flush. Fresh fluid cannot restore damaged clutches, bearings or other failed internal components.

Why a gearbox fluid change is not a repair for worn components

Automatic gearbox fluid is essential to transmission operation, but it cannot reverse physical wear. If internal clutch friction material has deteriorated, bearings are damaged or hydraulic components have failed, servicing the fluid alone will not repair those faults.

This is particularly important when a vehicle has persistent slipping, severe shuddering, loss of drive or repeated overheating. In these situations, diagnosis should come before maintenance work.

Mechanic insight:

The best time to service a gearbox is usually before problems develop, according to its maintenance requirements. Waiting until the transmission slips or overheats can turn a straightforward servicing decision into a much more expensive diagnostic and repair process.

Fluid level and topping up

Can You Just Top Up Automatic Gearbox Fluid Instead of Changing It?

Topping up automatic gearbox fluid and changing it are two different procedures. A top-up corrects a confirmed low fluid level, while a fluid change removes a quantity of used fluid and replaces it with the correct new fluid.

If the gearbox is due for a scheduled fluid service, simply adding a small amount of new fluid does not replace the existing fluid or complete the required maintenance.

When is topping up appropriate?

Topping up may be appropriate when the fluid level has been checked using the manufacturer's procedure and found to be below specification. The reason for the low level should also be investigated.

Many modern automatic transmissions do not have a conventional dipstick. Their fluid levels may need to be checked with the vehicle level, the gearbox at a specified temperature and the transmission in a particular operating state.

Some procedures also require diagnostic equipment to monitor transmission fluid temperature accurately.

Why automatic gearbox fluid level matters

Too little fluid

An insufficient fluid level can affect lubrication, cooling and hydraulic operation. Depending on the gearbox, this may cause abnormal engagement or damage.

Too much fluid

Overfilling can also cause problems. In some transmissions, excess fluid may foam or interfere with correct operation.

Incorrect fluid

Using fluid that does not meet the required specification can affect friction behaviour, hydraulic performance and internal component protection.

What causes automatic gearbox fluid to become low?

Automatic gearbox fluid normally operates within a contained system. A falling fluid level may indicate a leak, previous incorrect filling or another problem requiring investigation.

Possible leak locations include transmission sump seals, shaft seals, cooler pipes, connections and other gearbox components. The exact leak sources depend on the transmission design.

If fluid is repeatedly being added, the underlying cause needs to be identified. Continuing to top up without repairing a leak can leave the gearbox vulnerable to further fluid loss.

Never guess the gearbox fluid level

Do not add automatic gearbox fluid simply because the transmission feels jerky or slow to engage. Both underfilling and overfilling can cause problems. Always confirm the correct fluid specification and level-setting procedure before adding fluid.

UK gearbox servicing costs

How Much Does an Automatic Gearbox Fluid Change Cost in the UK?

An automatic gearbox fluid change at an independent UK garage commonly costs approximately £150–£350 for many routine services. More involved transmission services, specialist procedures and main dealer work can cost approximately £250–£500 or more.

These are indicative UK budgeting ranges, not fixed quotations. The final price depends on the exact gearbox, approved fluid specification, quantity of fluid required, filter arrangement, labour time and whether additional parts or diagnostic work are needed.

A basic drain-and-refill is not necessarily equivalent to a complete manufacturer-specified gearbox service. Some transmissions require a replacement filter, integrated sump assembly, new seals or a particular fluid-level setting procedure.

Is an automatic gearbox fluid change worth the money?

When a gearbox has a specified fluid service interval, carrying out that maintenance correctly is generally a sensible way to support transmission reliability. The service cost is usually substantially lower than the cost of major internal gearbox repairs.

However, servicing should not be sold as a guaranteed way to prevent every gearbox failure. Component wear, electrical faults, cooling problems and other defects can still occur even when the fluid has been maintained correctly.

If the transmission is already slipping, overheating or losing drive, spend money on an accurate diagnosis before assuming that a routine fluid service is the appropriate repair.

Beware of incomplete or unsuitable services

Avoid booking a gearbox fluid change solely because a garage advertises a low headline price. Confirm that the service is suitable for your exact transmission and includes the manufacturer's required procedure. Incorrect fluid or fluid level can cause expensive damage.

Choosing the correct service

Automatic Gearbox Fluid Change vs Flush — Which Is Better?

Drivers often hear the terms gearbox fluid change, drain-and-refill and gearbox flush used interchangeably. They are not necessarily the same procedure.

A conventional drain-and-refill removes the fluid that can drain from the transmission through its normal service points. A machine-assisted fluid exchange may replace a greater proportion of the transmission fluid by circulating new fluid through a suitable system.

Neither method is automatically better for every gearbox. The correct choice depends on the manufacturer's instructions, transmission design, service history and mechanical condition.

What is a gearbox drain-and-refill?

During a drain-and-refill, the technician removes the accessible used transmission fluid and replaces it with the correct approved product. Depending on the gearbox, the procedure may also include replacing a filter, sump assembly, gasket or other service components.

Some fluid can remain in the torque converter, cooling system or internal passages after draining. This means a routine drain-and-refill does not necessarily replace every litre of fluid inside the transmission.

What is a machine-assisted gearbox fluid exchange?

A machine-assisted exchange uses equipment designed to replace transmission fluid through an appropriate connection or circulation procedure. Where compatible with the gearbox, it may exchange a greater proportion of the existing fluid than a simple drain.

However, not every gearbox is suitable for this procedure, and an exchange machine does not automatically replace a required filter or sump. Some manufacturers specify particular servicing methods that should take priority over a garage's preferred equipment.

Is flushing an old automatic gearbox dangerous?

The concern is not that every fluid exchange is harmful. Problems arise when an unsuitable procedure is used, incorrect fluid is introduced, contamination is disturbed or a transmission with serious existing wear is treated as though it only needs maintenance.

A high-mileage automatic gearbox with unknown servicing history should be assessed before any fluid change or exchange, particularly if it already slips, overheats or engages gears poorly.

Some specialist equipment performs controlled fluid exchange without aggressive cleaning, while other services marketed as flushing may involve different chemicals or procedures. Ask the garage exactly what it intends to do.

Do not authorise an aggressive flush without checking compatibility

Avoid chemical cleaning or pressure-flushing procedures unless they are specifically appropriate for the transmission and supported by reliable technical guidance. A machine-assisted exchange is not automatically approved for every automatic gearbox.

Summary and key takeaways

When to Change Automatic Gearbox Fluid — Key Takeaways

Automatic gearbox fluid maintenance should be based on the exact transmission, the manufacturer's servicing requirements and the condition of the vehicle. There is no single fluid change interval that applies to every automatic car in the UK.

Follow the correct interval

Some automatic gearboxes have scheduled fluid changes, while others have lifetime-fill policies. Identify the gearbox and check its official maintenance schedule before booking work.

Use approved fluid

Automatic transmission fluids are not interchangeable. Incorrect fluid can affect clutch operation, hydraulic control, lubrication and transmission reliability.

Do not wait for failure

Where a fluid service is required, carry it out on schedule. Waiting for slipping, overheating or harsh gear changes can allow an existing problem to become more expensive.

Investigate warning signs

Jerking, delayed engagement, slipping, shuddering and unusual noises can have several causes. A fluid change is not a guaranteed repair for an existing gearbox fault.

Budget for the full service

Many routine UK services may cost around £150–£350, while complex or specialist work can cost £250–£500 or more. Confirm what the quotation includes.

Choose the correct method

A drain-and-refill and machine-assisted exchange are not identical. Follow the manufacturer's procedure and obtain specialist advice where servicing history is unknown.

Final mechanic insight:

The most useful gearbox service record is an invoice showing when the work was carried out, the vehicle mileage, the exact approved fluid, the replacement parts and the procedure used. Keep that record with the vehicle's maintenance history.

Frequently Asked Questions

Automatic Gearbox Fluid Change FAQs

These are some of the most common questions UK drivers ask about automatic gearbox fluid changes, maintenance intervals, warning signs, flushing and servicing costs.

How often should automatic gearbox fluid be changed?

There is no universal interval. Some manufacturers specify scheduled fluid changes, while others use lifetime-fill policies. Certain gearboxes have service intervals around 40,000 miles, but others differ significantly. Always check the exact gearbox and manufacturer schedule.

What are the signs that automatic gearbox fluid needs changing?

Possible warning signs include jerky gear changes, delayed engagement, hesitation, slipping, overheating, unusual noises and burnt-smelling fluid. However, these symptoms can also indicate mechanical or electronic faults, so diagnosis is important before assuming a fluid change will solve the problem.

How much does an automatic gearbox fluid change cost in the UK?

Many routine automatic gearbox fluid services at independent UK garages may cost approximately £150–£350. More involved services and main dealer work can cost £250–£500 or more. The final price depends on gearbox type, fluid specification, filters, labour and the service procedure.

Does sealed for life mean gearbox fluid never needs changing?

Not necessarily. Lifetime-fill wording usually reflects a manufacturer's servicing policy and operating assumptions. Transmission fluid can still deteriorate through heat and use. Check the vehicle manufacturer's schedule and any relevant gearbox manufacturer guidance before deciding whether preventive servicing is appropriate.

Is it better to change or flush automatic gearbox fluid?

Neither method is automatically better. A drain-and-refill replaces the accessible fluid, while a suitable machine-assisted exchange can replace a greater proportion. The correct method depends on the gearbox design, manufacturer instructions, maintenance history and mechanical condition.

Can changing old gearbox fluid damage the transmission?

A correctly performed fluid change should not normally damage a healthy gearbox. However, a badly worn transmission may continue to have problems after servicing. Incorrect fluid, improper filling or unsuitable flushing procedures can also cause damage. High-mileage gearboxes with unknown history should be assessed first.

Can I just top up automatic gearbox fluid instead of changing it?

Topping up is appropriate only when the fluid level is confirmed low and the correct approved fluid is used. It does not replace scheduled maintenance or repair a leak. Many modern gearboxes require specialist equipment and a temperature-controlled filling procedure.

Should I stop driving if my automatic gearbox is slipping?

Persistent slipping, loss of drive, severe grinding or a transmission overheating warning should be treated seriously. Stop driving safely and arrange specialist assistance. Continuing to drive may worsen internal damage and increase repair costs.
