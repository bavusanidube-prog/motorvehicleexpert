Quick answer

What are the MOT retest rules in the UK?

If your vehicle fails its MOT, you do not automatically need to pay for another complete test. The cost and type of retest depend mainly on whether the vehicle stays at the original test centre, whether you take it away for repairs, how quickly it returns and which items caused the failure.

Leave it at the test centre

If the vehicle remains at the same test centre for repair and is retested before the end of the 10th working day following the original test, only a partial retest is needed and no retest fee can be charged.

Take it away and return

If you take the vehicle elsewhere for repairs and return to the same test centre within 10 working days, a partial retest is normally enough but a partial retest fee can be charged.

Return by next working day

Certain specified failure items qualify for a free partial retest if the vehicle is returned to the same test centre by the end of the next working day.

Miss the qualifying period

Outside the qualifying retest arrangements, the vehicle normally needs another full MOT and the full MOT fee can be charged again.

The key question is what happens to the vehicle after it fails

Before driving away, ask the test centre whether your failure qualifies for a free next-working-day retest, what the last qualifying retest date is and what fee will apply if repairs are carried out elsewhere.

Retest rules do not override roadworthiness law

A valid MOT or an available retest window does not give permission to drive an unsafe vehicle. Dangerous defects must be repaired before the vehicle is driven, and any vehicle used on the road must remain roadworthy.

MOT retest rules

MOT retest rules at a glance

The MOT retest system is designed so that a vehicle does not always need another complete test after repairs. The exact retest route depends on where the vehicle is repaired, how quickly it returns and which items caused the original failure.

For most drivers, there are four practical outcomes: a free partial retest because the vehicle stayed at the test centre, a free next-working-day retest for certain specified items, a paid partial retest within 10 working days, or a completely new MOT when the qualifying conditions are no longer met.

The original MOT test centre matters

The partial-retest arrangements apply when the vehicle returns to the same MOT test centre that carried out the original examination. Taking the vehicle to another authorised MOT centre is normally treated as booking another full MOT rather than continuing the original partial-retest process.

Free retest

When is an MOT retest completely free?

The clearest free-retest route is to leave the vehicle at the same MOT test centre after it fails, allow that centre to carry out the repairs and have the vehicle retested before the end of the 10th working day following the original test.

In that situation, only the relevant parts of the MOT need to be re-examined and no retest fee is charged.

Required

Repairs carried out there

The vehicle is not removed while the failed defects are being repaired.

Required
This can be the simplest option for straightforward repairs

If the test centre is competitively priced and the failure is simple, leaving the vehicle there can remove the need to arrange transport elsewhere and can preserve the free partial-retest entitlement.

Free retest does not mean free repairs

The MOT re-examination itself may be free, but you still pay for the parts, labour and any diagnostic work needed to correct the failed defects.

Next-working-day rule

Which MOT failures can qualify for a free next-working-day retest?

If you take the vehicle away for repairs, certain specified failure items can still qualify for a free partial retest when the vehicle is returned to the same MOT test centre before the end of the next working day.

This is particularly useful for straightforward defects such as a bulb, registration plate, horn, tyre or wiper fault that can be repaired quickly without leaving the vehicle at the test centre.

Headlamp aim is specifically excluded

Lamps are included in the next-working-day free-retest list, but headlamp aim itself is not. Do not assume that every lighting-related MOT failure automatically qualifies.

Always ask the test centre to confirm eligibility

The failure certificate identifies the exact failed item. Before leaving, ask whether that item qualifies for the free next-working-day partial retest and confirm the deadline with the MOT centre.

Deadline explained

How the MOT 10-working-day retest rule works

If the vehicle is removed from the MOT test centre for repairs, it can normally return to the same test centre for a partial retest before the end of the 10th working day following the day of the original test.

The original test day is therefore followed by the qualifying working days. The important point is not simply counting 10 calendar days. Weekends and public holidays can affect the practical deadline, so ask the test centre to confirm the exact final qualifying date shown by the MOT testing system.

Repair choice

Do you have to use the same garage for an MOT retest?

You do not have to use the original test centre for the repair work. You can have the failed defects repaired elsewhere, provided the vehicle can legally and safely be moved.

However, the original partial-retest entitlement is linked to returning the vehicle to the same MOT test centre that carried out the failed test. If another MOT centre examines the vehicle, that centre would normally carry out a new full MOT.

Can another garage repair the car?

Yes. The garage carrying out the repair does not have to be the MOT test centre. For example, you can fail at one authorised MOT station, take the vehicle to a specialist repairer and then return it to the original MOT station for the partial retest within the qualifying period.

Check whether the vehicle is legal to move first

Do not drive a vehicle with a dangerous MOT defect simply to obtain a cheaper repair quote. Arrange recovery or another safe transport method where necessary.

Keep the refusal certificate and repair invoice

The MOT result is stored electronically, but keeping your paperwork makes it easier to confirm what failed, what was repaired and whether any disputed work relates directly to the original MOT defects.

Failure severity

Dangerous vs major MOT defects: what changes after a failure?

Both major and dangerous defects cause an MOT failure, but they do not carry the same immediate driving risk. The distinction matters before you decide whether to leave the vehicle at the test centre, drive it to another repairer or arrange recovery.

Major defect

The vehicle fails its MOT

A major defect is serious enough to cause an MOT failure. Whether the vehicle can be driven away depends on whether its existing MOT remains valid, whether any dangerous defects are also present and whether the vehicle is still roadworthy.

MOT Result: Fail
Dangerous defect

Do not drive the vehicle

A dangerous defect represents a direct and immediate risk to road safety or a serious environmental impact. It must be repaired before the vehicle is driven.

Driving Risk: Critical
Minor defect

The vehicle can still pass

A minor defect does not cause an MOT failure by itself, but it is recorded and should still be corrected before it develops into a more serious problem.

A valid old MOT does not override a dangerous failure

A vehicle can still have time remaining on its previous MOT certificate and yet be unsafe to drive. If the new test records a dangerous defect, do not drive the vehicle until that defect has been repaired.

Read the defect wording before deciding what to do

Driving after failure

Can you drive your car after it fails an MOT?

A failed MOT does not automatically cancel an existing MOT certificate that still has time remaining. However, that does not mean every failed vehicle can legally be driven away.

You can take the vehicle away after a failed MOT when its current MOT is still valid and no dangerous defects have been recorded. The vehicle must also continue to meet the minimum standards of roadworthiness.

Before driving away from a failed MOT

  • Check whether your existing MOT certificate is still valid.
  • Read every defect recorded on the refusal certificate.
  • Confirm that no defect is categorised as dangerous.
  • Consider whether the vehicle remains roadworthy despite the failure.
  • Ask the MOT tester if you are unsure about the defect severity.
  • Check tyres, brakes, steering and lights before leaving.
  • Arrange recovery instead if safe operation is uncertain.
Roadworthiness applies every day, not just on MOT day

A valid MOT certificate confirms that the vehicle met the required standards when it was tested. It is not permission to continue driving after a serious safety fault develops or is identified.

Expired MOT

What if your MOT has expired when the vehicle fails?

If the previous MOT has already expired, you cannot normally use the vehicle for ordinary journeys after the failure. There are limited exceptions for travelling to specific MOT-related appointments or repairs, but the vehicle must still be roadworthy.

Driving to a pre-arranged MOT

A vehicle without a valid MOT can be driven to a pre-arranged MOT test appointment, provided it remains roadworthy.

Driving to have failed defects repaired

A vehicle may be driven to a place where the defects from a failed MOT are going to be repaired, provided the journey is lawful and the vehicle is roadworthy.

Ordinary journeys

Shopping, commuting, social journeys or general use are not covered simply because a repair or retest is planned later.

Do not use normally
A journey exemption does not make an unsafe car roadworthy

If the vehicle has dangerous brakes, unsafe tyres, steering faults or another defect that makes it unroadworthy, arrange recovery instead of relying on an MOT-related journey exemption.

If your MOT has already expired, read Driving With an Expired MOT: UK Rules and Fines before moving the vehicle.

Disputed MOT result

Can you appeal an MOT failure?

Yes. If you believe the vehicle should not have failed, first discuss the result with the MOT test centre. If the disagreement cannot be resolved, you can appeal the result to the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency.

An appeal against a failed MOT must normally be submitted within 14 working days of the test.

1. Read the refusal notice

Identify exactly which defect you believe has been assessed incorrectly.

2. Speak to the test centre

Ask the tester or authorised examiner to explain the failure and the inspection standard used.

3. Do not repair it yet

The disputed condition needs to remain available for inspection during the appeal process.

4. Submit the appeal

Send the required appeal information to DVSA within the applicable deadline.

What happens during a failed-MOT appeal?

DVSA can arrange for a vehicle examiner to carry out another complete MOT examination. An appeal fee is payable before the examination. The outcome determines whether the original test decision was correct.

Do not repair the disputed defect before the appeal inspection

Repairing the vehicle can remove the evidence DVSA needs to assess whether the original MOT result was correct. If safety requires the vehicle to be moved, discuss the situation with DVSA and arrange safe transport rather than changing the disputed component.

Appealing a fail is different from complaining about service

The MOT appeal process is about whether the technical test result was correct. Problems involving customer service, repair prices or other garage disputes may need to be dealt with separately.

Retest failed again

What happens if the vehicle fails its MOT retest?

A partial retest only checks the parts of the vehicle that need to be re-examined under the retest rules. If a failed defect has not been repaired correctly, remains outside the required standard or another relevant defect is identified, the vehicle will not receive an MOT pass certificate.

Do not assume that repeated partial retests can continue indefinitely. Only one partial re-examination is permitted for each full MOT examination, so another failure can lead to a new full test being required.

After an MOT failure

What should you do immediately after your car fails its MOT?

A failed MOT can feel urgent, but the best decision comes from separating four questions: is the vehicle safe to move, what exactly failed, where should the repairs be carried out and which retest route gives the best overall value?

1. Read every defect

Identify major, dangerous, minor and advisory items separately.

2. Decide whether it can move

Check current MOT validity, dangerous defects and overall roadworthiness before driving away.

3. Confirm retest eligibility

Ask whether a free next-working-day retest, free retained-vehicle retest or paid 10-day partial retest applies.

4. Get the repair price

Ask for an itemised quote for the failed defects rather than a vague total.

5. Compare repairers

Decide whether leaving the vehicle at the MOT centre or using a specialist elsewhere gives the best complete outcome.

6. Record the deadline

Write down the final qualifying partial-retest date before leaving the test centre.

7. Keep the paperwork

Keep the refusal notice, repair invoices, parts warranty and retest receipt together.

8. Recheck before the retest

Confirm the failed defects have genuinely been corrected before presenting the vehicle again.

Questions worth asking the MOT station

  • Which defects actually caused the MOT failure?
  • Are any of the defects categorised as dangerous?
  • Can the vehicle legally and safely be driven away?
  • Does any failure qualify for a free next-working-day retest?
  • What is the exact last date for the 10-working-day partial retest?
  • What will the partial retest fee be if I repair it elsewhere?
  • How much would you charge to repair the failed defects here?
  • Which advisories are likely to become future failures?
Use the failure as a maintenance snapshot

Do not focus only on the items needed to obtain a pass. Repeated advisories for tyres, brakes, suspension, corrosion or oil leaks can show where the next repair bill is likely to come from.

If the refusal notice contains advisory items as well as failures, read MOT Advisory Meaning Explained before deciding which additional repairs are worth doing at the same time.

Retest planning

Which MOT failures are usually quickest to retest?

The size of an MOT failure list does not always tell you how difficult the retest will be. A vehicle can fail on several straightforward items that are repaired quickly, while a single brake, suspension, corrosion or emissions defect can require diagnosis, parts ordering or specialist work that uses much more of the retest window.

For retest planning, the important question is not simply what failed. It is whether the defect can be confirmed, repaired and rechecked before the qualifying retest deadline.

Before returning

What should you check before taking the car back for its MOT retest?

The purpose of a pre-retest check is not to carry out another complete MOT at home. It is to make sure the original failed items have actually been addressed and that no obvious new problem has appeared while the vehicle was being repaired.

Match repairs to the refusal notice

Work through the failed defects one by one rather than relying on a verbal statement that the car is now ready.

Check obvious functions

Confirm straightforward items such as lamps, horn, washers, wipers and other driver-checkable equipment operate normally.

Inspect repaired tyres and wheels

Confirm the correct tyre or wheel work has actually been completed and there are no obvious new problems before travelling.

Check warning lights

Make sure no relevant warning light has appeared since the repair or as a result of disconnected electrical components.

Keep the repair invoice

The invoice helps you identify exactly what was repaired if the vehicle does not pass the retest.

Confirm the booking and deadline

Verify the appointment is with the original test centre and still falls within the applicable retest period.

Pre-retest checklist

  • Keep the original MOT refusal details available.
  • Confirm every failed item has been repaired or corrected.
  • Check that no dangerous defect remains.
  • Confirm obvious lights and electrical items operate correctly.
  • Check the windscreen, mirrors, wipers and washers visually.
  • Look for obvious tyre damage or very low pressure.
  • Check that dashboard warning lights behave normally.
  • Confirm fluid is not visibly leaking after the repair.
  • Make sure no parts or trim have been left insecure.
  • Bring the vehicle back before the confirmed retest deadline.
Do not turn this into an unsafe DIY inspection

Driver checks are useful for obvious items, but brake performance, steering play, suspension wear, structural condition and emissions should be assessed with the correct equipment and competent procedures.

Avoid paying twice

How to reduce the chance of failing the MOT retest

Most avoidable retest problems come from incomplete repairs, overlooked items on the original refusal notice or returning before the repair has actually solved the measured defect.

Repair the failure, not the symptom

If a component failure has an underlying cause, make sure the cause is corrected rather than simply clearing the immediate symptom.

Use the complete failure list

Multiple small defects are easy to overlook when different people carry out different repairs.

Verify the repair

Where possible, the repairer should confirm the repaired system now operates correctly before the vehicle leaves the workshop.

Do not ignore related damage

A failed component can sometimes damage or expose wear in nearby parts that also need attention.

Allow enough repair time

Booking the retest before the garage knows when parts will arrive can create unnecessary deadline pressure.

Check obvious items again

A bulb or tyre issue can develop while more substantial repair work is being completed, so make a final basic check before returning.

For a full pre-MOT inspection, use the dedicated preparation guide

This page is intentionally focused on the retest process. If you want the complete checks to carry out before a future full MOT, continue to How to Prepare for an MOT Test UK .

Do not chase a pass at the expense of a proper repair

The goal is not simply to remove an MOT failure from the database. The vehicle should leave the repair process safe, roadworthy and with the underlying defect correctly resolved.

Retest problems

Why can a car fail again after MOT repairs?

A second failure does not always mean the MOT tester has changed the standard. More commonly, the original repair was incomplete, the wrong component was diagnosed, the repaired system still does not meet the test requirement or another relevant problem has become apparent.

Wrong fault diagnosed

A part was replaced without proving that it caused the original MOT test result.

Repair incomplete

One part of the failed system was repaired but another defect still prevents it meeting the required standard.

Adjustment still incorrect

The replacement component is serviceable, but adjustment, alignment or setup remains outside the required range.

Related wear was missed

Another worn component affects the same system and becomes apparent during the re-examination.

New defect developed

A separate problem appeared between the original test and the vehicle's return.

Repair quality was poor

Incorrect installation, unsuitable parts or poor workmanship can leave the vehicle outside the required standard.

Ask for the exact retest failure wording

If the car fails again, compare the new result with the original refusal notice and the repair invoice. That tells you whether the same defect remains or a different issue now needs attention.

Final checklist

MOT retest checklist: from failure to pass

Use this as the practical sequence from the moment the vehicle fails until it is presented again.

1. Read the failure

Separate dangerous, major, minor and advisory defects.

2. Confirm movement

Decide whether the vehicle can legally and safely leave the test centre.

3. Confirm retest type

Establish whether the likely retest is free, reduced-fee or a new full MOT.

4. Record the deadline

Get the exact qualifying return date from the original test centre.

5. Diagnose properly

Do not order expensive components until the repairer has confirmed the cause where diagnosis is required.

6. Repair every failure

Work through the complete refusal notice rather than only the most expensive defect.

7. Verify the work

Check the invoice and confirm obvious repaired functions before returning.

8. Return on time

Present the vehicle to the original MOT test centre within the applicable retest period.

Think in this order: safety → diagnosis → repair → retest

That order prevents a small potential saving on the MOT fee from controlling a much more important repair or safety decision.

Frequently asked questions

MOT Retest Rules UK FAQs

These 18 visible answers match the FAQ structured data in the page head exactly.

How long do you have for an MOT retest?

If you return to the same MOT test centre before the end of the 10th working day following the original test, the vehicle can normally have a partial retest rather than another complete MOT.

When is an MOT retest free?

A partial retest is free when the vehicle remains at the same MOT test centre for repairs and is retested before the end of the 10th working day following the original test.

Is an MOT retest free if I take the car away?

Not normally. If you take the vehicle away for repairs and return it to the same test centre within 10 working days, only a partial retest is normally required but the test centre can charge a partial retest fee. Certain specified items qualify for a free retest if the vehicle returns by the end of the next working day.

How much can an MOT partial retest cost?

When a vehicle is taken away for repair and returned to the same test centre within 10 working days, the test centre can charge up to half the statutory maximum MOT test fee for the vehicle class.

What happens if I miss the 10 working day MOT retest deadline?

If the vehicle is not retested within the qualifying 10-working-day period, a full MOT is normally required and the full statutory test fee may be charged again.

Does the day of the failed MOT count as one of the 10 working days?

The qualifying period runs to the end of the 10th working day following the day of the original test, so the day of the failed MOT itself is not counted as the first working day.

Can I get an MOT retest at a different garage?

Another authorised MOT test centre can test the vehicle, but the original partial-retest arrangements apply to returning to the same test centre. At another centre you would normally be booking a new full MOT.

Can I take my car to another garage for repairs after an MOT failure?

Yes, provided the vehicle can legally and safely be driven. If you return to the original MOT test centre within the 10-working-day period, a partial retest can normally be carried out and a partial retest fee may be charged.

Can I drive my car after it fails an MOT?

You can take the vehicle away after a failure if its current MOT is still valid and no dangerous defects were recorded, but the vehicle must still meet minimum roadworthiness standards. A vehicle with a dangerous defect must be repaired before it is driven.

Can I drive a car with a dangerous MOT defect?

No. A vehicle that has failed because of a dangerous defect must not be driven until the dangerous defect has been repaired.

Can I drive after a major MOT failure?

A major defect causes an MOT failure. Whether the vehicle can legally be taken away depends on whether the existing MOT remains valid, whether any dangerous defects are present and whether the vehicle remains roadworthy.

Can I drive to repairs if my MOT has expired?

If the MOT has expired, you can drive the vehicle to have failed defects repaired or to a pre-arranged MOT test appointment, but the vehicle must remain roadworthy and legal to use.

Which MOT failures can qualify for a free next-working-day retest?

Specified items can qualify for a free partial retest when the vehicle is repaired away from the test centre and returned to the same centre by the end of the next working day. Examples include certain lamps, mirrors, registration plates, tyres, wheels, wipers, washers, horn, battery and other items listed in the official MOT retest rules.

Is a headlamp aim failure eligible for the free next-day retest?

Lamps can qualify for the free next-working-day retest, but headlamp aim itself is excluded from that particular free-retest list.

What happens if my car fails the MOT retest?

The vehicle still has not passed and the remaining defects need to be repaired. Only one partial re-examination is permitted per full MOT examination, so further testing requirements and fees depend on the circumstances and timing.

Can I appeal an MOT failure?

Yes. If you believe the MOT failure is wrong, discuss it with the test centre before repairs are carried out. An appeal against a failed MOT must normally be submitted to DVSA within 14 working days of the test.

Should I repair the car before appealing an MOT failure?

No. If you intend to appeal the MOT result, you should discuss the result with the test centre and avoid having repairs carried out until the appeal process has been completed.

What should I ask the garage after my car fails its MOT?

Ask whether the vehicle is safe and legal to drive, which defects caused the failure, whether any item qualifies for a free next-working-day retest, the exact 10-working-day deadline and what retest fee will apply if you take the vehicle away for repairs.
