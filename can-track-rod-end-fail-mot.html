UK Track Rod End MOT Guide

Can Track Rod End Fail MOT?

Yes. A track rod end can fail the MOT for excessive joint play, insecure steering linkage, loss of dust-cover protection, missing locking hardware or serious misalignment. If joint wear becomes severe enough that detachment is a serious risk, the defect can become Dangerous.

Steering-joint play deserves proper diagnosis before test day

A track rod end helps transfer steering movement from the rack to the road wheel. Excessive looseness can affect steering precision and, in advanced cases, joint security. If the steering feels unusually vague, knocks heavily or has obvious free movement, have the front steering linkage inspected promptly.

Quick Answer

Will a Worn Track Rod End Fail an MOT?

Yes. A track rod end can fail the MOT when its ball joint has excessive wear or free play, the steering linkage is insecure, a required retaining device is ineffective, the joint is seriously misaligned or the protective dust cover has stopped keeping contamination away from the joint.

The result is not always Major. A dust cover that is damaged or deteriorated but still protects the joint can be recorded as a Minor defect. At the other end of the scale, joint wear severe enough to create a serious risk of detachment can be Dangerous.

Minor

Dust Cover Deteriorated

Pass Possible

A damaged or deteriorated ball-joint dust cover can remain Minor while it still protects the steering joint from dirt and water.

Major

Excessive Joint Play

MOT Failure

Excessive wear or free play in the steering ball joint is a Major defect because the steering linkage is no longer in serviceable condition.

Dangerous

Serious Detachment Risk

Urgent Steering Fault

If wear has progressed to the point that the joint could detach, the defect moves into the Dangerous category.

A split dust boot and a worn steering joint are not automatically the same defect

The cover protects the joint, while the ball joint itself controls steering movement. A cover can deteriorate before excessive mechanical play develops, or a joint can already be worn even if the rubber cover still looks acceptable.

Do not ignore obvious steering looseness because the car still tracks straight

A vehicle can sometimes travel reasonably straight even while a steering joint has significant free play. Physical joint condition matters more than whether the driver can temporarily compensate at the steering wheel.

Current MOT Classifications

Track Rod End MOT Defects: Minor, Major and Dangerous

Track rod ends sit within the steering-linkage inspection. The clearest way to understand the MOT is to separate joint wear, dust-cover protection, component security, physical damage and alignment.

Condition Classification MOT Result Practical Meaning
Dust cover damaged or deteriorated but still protecting joint Minor Pass possible Repair before the cover deteriorates far enough to expose the joint.
Dust cover missing or no longer protecting joint Major Fail Joint protection has been lost and contamination can enter.
Steering ball joint with excessive wear or free play Major Fail Confirm the looseness is in the track rod end rather than another steering or suspension joint.
Steering ball joint with serious detachment risk Dangerous Fail Advanced wear has created a serious steering-security risk.
Relative movement where steering components should remain fixed Major Fail Fixings and linkage connections must remain secure.
Excessive movement or linkage likely to detach Dangerous Fail Movement has progressed into a serious steering-security condition.
Retaining or locking device missing or ineffective Major Fail The steering linkage must remain correctly retained after adjustment and assembly.
Track rod or drag-link end seriously misaligned Major Fail This concerns serious joint/linkage alignment rather than ordinary wheel-toe adjustment alone.
Steering linkage component fractured or deformed Major Fail Physical damage to a steering component needs proper repair or replacement.
Fracture or deformation severe enough to affect steering Dangerous Fail Damage is now affecting the ability of the steering system to operate safely.
Mechanic distinction: track rod misalignment is not the same as ordinary wheel alignment

A vehicle can have toe outside the ideal alignment specification without automatically having a seriously misaligned track rod end. The MOT defect refers to the physical steering-linkage condition, while wheel alignment is a separate geometry question.

Dangerous wear means joint security has become the central issue

Once the joint is at serious risk of detachment, the concern is no longer tyre wear or vague steering feel. The steering linkage itself may no longer remain safely connected.

Tester Checks

What Does the MOT Tester Check on a Track Rod End?

A track rod end is assessed as part of the steering linkage rather than as an isolated rubber-boot component. The tester is looking at free play, joint security, physical condition, retaining devices, dust-cover protection and linkage alignment.

Ball Joint

Is There Excessive Free Play?

The joint is checked for mechanical looseness beyond a serviceable condition.

Security

Are Components Moving Where They Should Be Fixed?

Steering-linkage connections should remain firmly secured rather than shifting relative to their mounting or adjustment points.

Detachment Risk

Could the Joint Separate?

Severe wear or movement is assessed more seriously when it creates a realistic risk of the linkage becoming detached.

Dust Cover

Is Joint Protection Still Effective?

The rubber cover is checked for damage, deterioration and whether it still keeps contamination away from the joint.

Locking

Is the Retaining Hardware Effective?

Steering adjustments and joints need their intended retaining or locking method to remain effective.

Alignment

Is the Track Rod End Seriously Misaligned?

The physical relationship of the steering linkage is assessed separately from a routine four-wheel-alignment reading.

Damage

Is the Linkage Bent or Fractured?

Impact damage, deformation or cracking can create a separate steering failure route.

Steering Operation

Does the Linkage Move Freely?

Steering operation should not be restricted or fouled by surrounding components through the normal steering range.

Wider Front End

Is the Movement Actually Elsewhere?

Ball joints, inner tie rods, wheel bearings and suspension components can all create movement at the road wheel, so the failed component should be isolated.

The MOT tester identifies the defect; the workshop still has to diagnose the cause

A failed steering-joint check tells you what is wrong at inspection level. The technician then needs to decide whether the repair is the outer track rod end, inner tie rod, steering rack, fixing hardware or another front-end component.

Tyre wear can support the diagnosis but does not replace a steering inspection

Inner or outer shoulder wear can point towards poor toe or steering geometry, but tyres cannot tell you whether the outer track rod end, inner tie rod, wheel alignment or another component is responsible.

Major MOT Defect

Will Excessive Track Rod End Play Fail an MOT?

Yes. A track rod end contains a steering ball joint, and excessive wear or free play in that joint is a Major MOT defect.

The important diagnostic step is confirming that the movement is actually inside the outer track rod end. Looseness felt at the road wheel can also come from the inner tie rod, steering rack, ball joint, wheel bearing or another suspension component.

Joint Wear

Ball Joint Becomes Loose

Wear between the ball and socket can allow movement that should no longer be present in a serviceable steering joint.

MOT Result

Excessive Free Play

Major

Once free play has become excessive, the steering joint no longer meets the required mechanical condition and the vehicle fails.

Confirmation

Isolate the Movement

Observe the outer joint directly while steering or wheel movement is applied so another component is not mistaken for the track rod end.

Small steering movement and excessive joint play are not the same thing

The steering system contains several moving joints by design. The concern is unwanted free movement inside a component that should transfer steering input accurately and securely.

Mechanic check: watch the stud and joint housing

When steering load is applied, excessive relative movement at the track rod end helps distinguish joint wear from movement elsewhere in the steering or suspension.

Dangerous MOT Defect

When Can Track Rod End Wear Become Dangerous?

There is a clear step beyond ordinary excessive play. Track rod end wear becomes far more serious when the joint has deteriorated enough to create a serious risk that the steering connection could detach.

At that point, the issue is no longer simply vague steering or uneven tyre wear. The physical connection between the steering linkage and road wheel may no longer remain reliably secured.

Advanced Wear

Movement Is Severe

The joint has progressed well beyond ordinary service wear and may show obvious looseness under steering load.

Security

Joint Retention at Risk

Wear can become serious enough that continued mechanical connection between the stud and joint assembly can no longer be relied upon.

MOT Severity

Serious Detachment Risk

Dangerous

Once the joint is at serious risk of separating, the defect reaches the highest MOT severity.

Do not continue normal driving with a steering joint at risk of detachment

A track rod end is part of the mechanical steering connection. If the joint is severely worn or insecure enough that separation is possible, arrange repair or appropriate recovery rather than treating it as a routine MOT preparation item.

Dangerous is about joint security, not how loud the knock sounds

Noise can help reveal a problem, but MOT severity is determined by the actual mechanical condition and detachment risk rather than cabin noise alone.

Minor MOT Defect

Can a Damaged Track Rod End Dust Cover Fail an MOT?

A damaged or deteriorated track rod end dust cover does not automatically mean a Major failure.

If the cover is worn, cracked or damaged but still performs its protective function, the defect can be recorded as Minor.

Ageing

Rubber Cracking

Heat, steering movement and age can harden the rubber and create surface cracks before the seal is fully lost.

Deterioration

Cover Still Intact

The boot can look aged yet still keep grease around the joint and road contamination outside.

MOT Result

Protection Still Effective

Minor

The damaged cover has not yet lost the protective function needed to move into the more serious category.

Dust-cover condition and ball-joint wear are separate checks

A damaged cover does not automatically mean the joint has excessive play. Likewise, a worn joint can have serious internal movement even if its rubber cover still looks acceptable.

Repair the protective fault before contamination accelerates wear

Once dirt and water reach the ball-and-socket surfaces, abrasive wear can increase and turn a relatively minor protective-cover problem into a steering-joint replacement.

Major MOT Defect

When Does a Track Rod End Dust Cover Become a Major MOT Defect?

The classification changes when the cover is missing or damaged enough that it no longer protects the steering joint from contamination.

At that point, the issue is no longer simple rubber deterioration. The joint has lost the barrier intended to keep road dirt, water and grit away from its working surfaces.

Missing

No Protective Cover

The ball joint is left without the intended barrier protecting its internal surfaces from contamination.

Split

Seal Has Opened

A tear can progress far enough that dirt and moisture can enter even though part of the rubber boot remains present.

MOT Result

Protective Function Lost

Major

Once the cover no longer keeps contamination away from the joint, the defect becomes a Major failure.

Do not simply fill a contaminated joint with fresh grease

If the cover has been open for some time, dirt may already be inside the joint. Inspect for free play and roughness before assuming that a boot repair alone is enough.

Look for grease around the joint and surrounding components

Escaping grease can provide useful evidence that the dust cover has split or moved, but the MOT classification still depends on the actual protective condition of the cover and the joint itself.

Steering Security

Can a Loose or Insecure Track Rod End Fail an MOT?

Yes. The track rod end must remain securely connected to the steering linkage and steering arm.

Movement between components that should remain fixed can be a Major defect, while movement severe enough to make detachment likely can become Dangerous.

Joint Fixing

Taper Connection Loose

The ball-joint stud should remain securely located in its steering-arm mounting rather than shifting under load.

Adjustment

Track Rod Adjustment Insecure

The threaded adjustment between steering components needs to remain properly secured after toe setting or component replacement.

Locking

Retaining Device Ineffective

Lock nuts, locking arrangements or other retaining hardware must perform their intended security function.

Security Fault MOT Significance Practical Repair
Relative movement where components should remain fixed Major Diagnose loose fixing, damaged taper or insecure adjustment
Retaining or locking device missing / ineffective Major Restore the correct retaining arrangement
Movement so severe that detachment is likely Dangerous Do not continue normal driving; repair steering security
A recently replaced track rod end can still fail if it was fitted insecurely

New parts do not compensate for poor installation. Correct taper seating, retaining hardware and adjustment locking all need to be confirmed after replacement.

Check security before blaming internal joint wear

Movement caused by a loose mounting or adjustment can feel like a worn joint from the wheel. Watch the complete assembly while steering load is applied.

Linkage Alignment

Can a Misaligned Track Rod End Fail an MOT?

Yes, where the track rod or drag-link end is seriously misaligned. That is a defined steering-linkage problem and should not be confused with an ordinary wheel-alignment reading being slightly outside specification.

The distinction is important because wheel alignment describes steering geometry, while serious track rod end misalignment concerns the physical position and operation of the linkage itself.

Joint Position

Ball Joint Operating at Bad Angle

Serious misalignment can place the joint outside the position intended for normal steering movement.

Linkage

Track Rod Relationship Incorrect

Bent components, incorrect assembly or unsuitable parts can disturb the physical relationship of the steering linkage.

MOT Result

Serious Misalignment

Major

Where the steering linkage itself is seriously misaligned, the defect causes an MOT failure.

Wheel alignment and track rod end alignment are related but not identical

Track rod adjustment changes wheel toe, but an MOT failure for serious track rod end misalignment is not simply another way of saying the wheel alignment needs setting.

Bent steering components should not be corrected with alignment adjustment alone

If an impact has bent the track rod, steering arm or another linkage component, adjusting toe may make the steering wheel appear straighter without restoring the damaged component.

Check component identity after previous steering repairs

Incorrect track rod ends, wrong-length replacement components or poor installation can create abnormal joint angles and limited adjustment. Confirm the fitted parts before repeated alignment attempts.

Steering Symptoms

What Are the Common Signs of a Worn Track Rod End?

A worn track rod end can affect steering accuracy, front-end movement and tyre wear, but no single symptom proves the outer steering joint is faulty.

The strongest diagnosis comes from combining what the driver feels with direct inspection of the steering linkage.

Noise

Knocking or Clunking

Free play can produce a knock as steering load changes, particularly over rough roads or during low-speed steering movements.

Steering Feel

Vague or Loose Steering

Excess movement in the linkage can reduce steering precision and make the car require more frequent correction.

Tyres

Uneven Edge Wear

Track rod wear or poor adjustment can alter toe and accelerate wear on the inner or outer shoulder of a tyre.

Pulling

Car Wanders or Pulls

Steering looseness can contribute to poor directional stability, though tyre pressure, wheel alignment and suspension faults can do the same.

Steering Wheel

Off-Centre Position

Previous adjustment, bent steering parts or incorrect toe can leave the steering wheel off-centre even without severe joint wear.

Inspection

Visible Joint Movement

Direct movement at the outer joint while steering load is applied is far stronger evidence than symptoms alone.

Do not diagnose a track rod end from tyre wear or knocking alone

Ball joints, lower-arm bushes, anti-roll-bar links, wheel bearings, inner tie rods and the steering rack can produce similar front-end symptoms. Confirm the actual moving component before replacing parts.

The useful pattern is symptom plus visible movement

Vague steering or knocking becomes much more persuasive when the outer track rod end can also be seen moving excessively under controlled load.

Front-End Noise

Can a Worn Track Rod End Cause Knocking or Clunking?

Yes. A loose outer steering joint can produce a knock when steering force changes direction or the wheel moves over uneven road surfaces.

The sound is caused by clearance inside the joint allowing parts to move before load is transferred fully.

Rough Roads

Knock Over Bumps

Suspension movement can load and unload the steering joint enough for free play to become audible.

Steering Input

Clunk When Direction Changes

A worn joint may knock when steering force changes from left to right or vice versa.

Low Speed

Parking-Speed Noise

Slow steering manoeuvres can make front-end looseness easier to hear because road and engine noise are lower.

Knocking is not unique to track rod ends

Anti-roll-bar links, ball joints, lower-arm bushes and steering-rack mounts can all knock in similar conditions. The sound should guide the inspection, not decide the part.

Watch the joint while steering load is applied

If a second person gently moves the steering while the vehicle is safely supported, visible delay or movement at the track rod end can help confirm whether the noise is coming from that joint.

Steering Precision

Can Worn Track Rod Ends Make Steering Feel Vague?

Yes. The track rod end transfers steering movement to the wheel, so free play can introduce a delay between steering-wheel input and road-wheel response.

The driver may describe the car as wandering, loose, imprecise or needing frequent correction.

Delay

Steering Input Feels Less Direct

Movement is taken up inside the worn joint before the road wheel responds fully.

Motorway

More Correction Needed

At higher road speeds, steering looseness can become more noticeable as the driver repeatedly corrects the vehicle's path.

Geometry

Toe Can Also Be Affected

Wear or poor adjustment around the track rod can change steering geometry and add to the vague feeling.

Vague steering is not automatically an outer track rod end

Inner tie rods, steering-rack wear, suspension bushes, tyre problems and wheel alignment can all produce similar symptoms. The complete steering system should be checked.

Check for free play before booking alignment alone

Alignment cannot remove mechanical looseness. Worn joints should be repaired first, then the geometry can be set accurately afterwards.

Steering Geometry

Can Track Rod End Wear Cause Uneven Tyre Wear?

Yes. Track rod ends are directly involved in toe adjustment, so wear, looseness or incorrect adjustment can allow the wheel to run at an undesirable steering angle.

That can contribute to inner-edge wear, outer-edge wear, feathering or accelerated tread loss.

Toe

Wheel Points Too Far In or Out

Incorrect toe makes the tyre scrub across the road rather than rolling cleanly in the direction of travel.

Joint Wear

Geometry Can Change Under Load

A loose joint can allow steering position to vary as road forces change.

Tyre Pattern

Wear Is Evidence, Not Proof

Uneven tyre wear can also come from pressure, camber, suspension damage or previous alignment settings.

Tyres can reveal the history of a steering fault

Even after a worn track rod end is replaced, an old tyre may continue to show the wear pattern created before the repair. Diagnose the current steering condition rather than assuming the old tread pattern proves the joint is still faulty.

Repair wear first, align second

Accurate wheel alignment depends on the steering joints being mechanically sound. Replacing a worn track rod end before alignment prevents the geometry reading from being set around unwanted joint movement.

Differential Diagnosis

Track Rod End vs Ball Joint: Which One Is Worn?

Both are ball-and-socket joints near the front wheel, and both can produce knocking and free movement, but they perform different jobs.

Track Rod End

Steering Linkage

The outer track rod end connects the steering linkage to the steering arm and transfers steering movement to the road wheel.

  • Play often appears during steering input.
  • Wear can contribute to vague steering.
  • Adjustment directly affects toe.
  • Dust-cover failure can expose the steering joint to contamination.
Ball Joint

Suspension Joint

The suspension ball joint allows the wheel hub or steering knuckle to move with suspension travel while maintaining its connection to the control arm.

  • Movement may appear more clearly under suspension load.
  • Wear can create knocking over bumps.
  • Severe wear can affect wheel position.
  • It has its own separate MOT criteria.
Do not replace both joints because movement is felt at the wheel

Observe each component directly while load is applied. The track rod end, ball joint and wheel bearing can all transmit movement through the wheel, but only the worn component needs replacement.

Steering Differential

Track Rod End vs Steering Rack: Where Is the Play Coming From?

Steering free play can exist at several points between the steering wheel and the road wheel. Replacing the outer track rod end will not cure looseness that actually originates further inboard.

Outer Track Rod End

Play at Wheel-Side Joint

Movement is visible between the outer ball joint and steering arm while steering load is applied.

Inner Tie Rod

Play Further Inboard

The outer joint may remain tight while movement exists in the inner articulation closer to the steering rack.

Steering Rack

Rack or Mounting Movement

Internal rack wear or loose rack mountings can create steering free play that feels similar from the road wheel.

Follow the movement from the wheel towards the rack

Watching each joint while a small steering input is applied can show where the movement begins. The first component moving without transferring that input accurately deserves closer inspection.

Repeated outer track rod replacement will not fix rack wear

If new outer joints are tight but steering looseness remains, inspect the inner tie rods, steering rack and rack mountings before fitting more outer joints.

Repair Confirmation

What Should Be Checked After Track Rod End Replacement?

Replacing the joint is only part of the repair. The steering linkage must also be secured correctly and the wheel geometry checked afterwards.

The final confirmation should cover joint security, free movement, toe adjustment, steering-wheel position and tyre behaviour.

Joint

No Excessive Free Play

Recheck the new outer joint and confirm that the original unwanted movement has been removed.

Fixing

Taper Correctly Secured

The ball-joint stud and intended retaining hardware must remain firmly located after installation.

Adjustment

Locking Hardware Tight

The track rod adjustment must remain secure after the steering geometry has been set.

Alignment

Toe Checked

Track rod end replacement can alter toe, so alignment should normally be checked rather than relying only on counting threads or turns.

Steering Wheel

Wheel Returns Correctly

The steering wheel should sit correctly during straight-line driving once alignment is complete.

Road Test

Knocking and Vagueness Resolved

Confirm the original steering complaint has disappeared rather than assuming the replaced part was the only fault.

Do not skip alignment because the new joint was installed to the old position

Counting exposed threads or matching the old joint position can get the geometry close enough for assembly, but manufacturing tolerances mean it does not guarantee correct toe.

Recheck the complete front end if symptoms remain

Persistent knocking, tyre wear or steering looseness after track rod end replacement should lead back to the inner tie rod, steering rack, ball joints, lower arms, wheel bearings and alignment rather than another automatic outer-joint replacement.

Pre-MOT Checks

What Can You Check Before the MOT if a Track Rod End May Be Worn?

An owner can collect useful evidence before the test, but steering-joint wear should ultimately be confirmed by physically inspecting the linkage.

Concentrate on steering feel, knocking, tyre wear, steering-wheel position and any recent change in how the vehicle tracks.

Check 1

Listen for Front-End Knocking

Note whether the noise appears over rough roads, during low-speed steering or as steering load changes direction.

Check 2

Note Vague Steering

Increased correction, wandering or a less direct steering response can justify a closer steering-linkage inspection.

Check 3

Inspect Both Front Tyres

Look for feathering, inner-edge wear, outer-edge wear or a large difference between the two front tyres.

Check 4

Check Steering-Wheel Position

An off-centre wheel can point towards alignment or previous steering adjustment, although it does not prove the track rod end is worn.

Check 5

Look at the Dust Cover

Where visible, check for obvious splitting, missing rubber or grease escaping around the outer steering joint.

Check 6

Review MOT History

Repeated steering or tyre advisories can show whether the front-end problem has been developing over more than one test.

Do not put hands around steering joints while someone is moving the vehicle

Physical free-play checks should be carried out with the vehicle safely positioned and supported. If you cannot inspect the steering linkage safely, use a workshop.

Describe the behaviour rather than ordering a part

Telling the technician that the car knocks over rough roads, wanders at speed and is wearing one front tyre is more useful than asking for a track rod end to be replaced before the joint has been confirmed.

Workshop Confirmation

What Is the Correct Track Rod End Diagnostic Process?

The diagnosis should answer two questions: where is the steering movement coming from, and has the outer track rod end itself failed?

Step 1

Confirm the Driver Complaint

Establish whether the main symptom is knocking, vague steering, pulling, tyre wear or an MOT advisory.

Step 2

Inspect the Tyres

Tyre wear can reveal geometry problems but should not be treated as proof that the outer joint has failed.

Step 3

Apply Steering Load

Use controlled steering movement while observing the outer track rod end and surrounding steering components.

Step 4

Isolate Outer Joint Play

Confirm unwanted movement between the ball stud and joint housing rather than movement elsewhere in the linkage.

Step 5

Check the Inner Tie Rod

If the outer joint remains tight, trace the movement further inboard towards the inner tie rod and steering rack.

Step 6

Check the Ball Joint

Suspension ball-joint movement can be transmitted through the same road wheel and mistaken for track rod wear.

Step 7

Inspect Dust-Cover Protection

Determine whether the cover is merely deteriorated or has actually lost its ability to keep contamination away from the joint.

Step 8

Check Security and Locking

Inspect the taper fixing, adjustment thread and intended locking arrangement for movement or incorrect assembly.

Step 9

Check Alignment After Repair

Once worn parts are replaced, measure and correct toe rather than assuming the old adjustment position remains accurate.

Do not align a front end that still has steering free play

Mechanical looseness can make alignment readings unstable. Repair worn or insecure steering components first, then set the geometry.

Diagnose from the outside in

Starting at the road wheel and following movement through the outer joint, inner tie rod and steering rack helps prevent an expensive rack being blamed for a simple outer-joint fault — or vice versa.

Minor dust-cover deterioration still deserves attention

After Replacement

Do You Need Wheel Alignment After Replacing a Track Rod End?

Yes — alignment should normally be checked after track rod end replacement.

The outer track rod end sits on the threaded adjustment that determines wheel toe. Removing and refitting the joint changes that relationship even when the new part appears to be installed in the same position.

Toe

Steering Geometry Changes

Turning the joint further onto or off the track rod changes how far the wheel points inward or outward.

Parts

New Joint Dimensions Vary

Replacement components can differ slightly in dimensions even when they are correct for the vehicle.

Tyres

Protect the Tread

Correct toe helps prevent the new steering repair being followed by accelerated inner- or outer-edge tyre wear.

Counting turns is an assembly aid, not a finished alignment

Matching the old thread position can help keep the geometry close enough for the journey to alignment, but it does not prove the toe setting is correct.

Alignment cannot repair a worn track rod end

If the joint still has free play, geometry adjustment only changes wheel position around an unresolved mechanical fault. Repair first and align afterwards.

MOT Retest

What Happens After a Track Rod End MOT Failure?

Once the failed steering component has been repaired and the steering geometry checked where necessary, the vehicle can return for the appropriate MOT retest.

Vehicle Stays

Repaired at the Test Centre

Free Partial Retest

If the vehicle remains at the same test centre for repair and is retested within 10 working days, only a partial retest is needed and there is no retest fee.

Vehicle Leaves

Return Within 10 Working Days

Partial Fee Can Apply

If the vehicle is taken elsewhere for repair and returned to the same test centre within 10 working days, a partial retest can still be carried out but a partial fee may be charged.

Outside the Window

Full MOT Required

Full Test Fee

Outside the qualifying retest arrangements, another full MOT test is normally required.

A track rod end is not one of the specifically listed next-working-day free-return items

Do not assume that taking the vehicle away, replacing the joint and returning the next working day automatically makes the retest free. The normal retest arrangements above apply to the steering-linkage defect.

Dangerous steering defects must be treated as roadworthiness problems

If the joint is at serious risk of detachment, the priority is safe repair or recovery. A previous MOT certificate does not make an unsafe steering connection suitable for normal driving.

Used-Car Buying

Should You Buy a Used Car With a Worn Track Rod End?

A worn outer track rod end is not automatically a reason to reject a used car. The bigger question is whether it is an isolated steering-joint repair or one sign of wider front-end neglect.

Steering Feel

Check for Vagueness

A car needing constant steering correction may have more than one worn steering or suspension component.

Noise

Listen for Knocking

Multiple front-end knocks can indicate ball joints, bushes or anti-roll bar links in addition to the track rod end.

Frequently Asked Questions

Track Rod End & MOT FAQs

Can a track rod end fail an MOT?

Yes. A track rod end can fail an MOT if the steering ball joint has excessive wear or free play, the linkage is insecure, a retaining device is ineffective, the track rod end is seriously misaligned or the dust cover no longer protects the joint from contamination.

Is excessive track rod end play an MOT failure?

Yes. Excessive wear or free play in a steering ball joint is a Major MOT defect. The movement should be isolated at the track rod end rather than assumed from looseness felt somewhere else in the steering or suspension.

When can a worn track rod end become Dangerous on an MOT?

A worn steering ball joint can become Dangerous when deterioration has progressed far enough to create a serious risk that the joint could detach. This is substantially more severe than ordinary excessive play.

Can a damaged track rod end dust cover fail an MOT?

A damaged or deteriorated steering ball-joint dust cover is currently a Minor defect while it still protects the joint. If the cover is missing or no longer keeps dirt away from the joint, the classification becomes Major.

Can a split track rod end boot fail an MOT?

A split dust cover does not automatically receive the same result in every case. If it is damaged but still keeps contamination out it can be Minor, while a split that has destroyed the protective seal and allows dirt into the joint can be Major.

Will a missing track rod end dust cover fail an MOT?

Yes. If the track rod end should have a dust cover and the cover is missing so the joint is no longer protected from contamination, the defect is Major.

Can a loose track rod end fail an MOT?

Yes. Relative movement between steering-linkage components that should remain fixed is a Major defect. If the movement becomes extreme enough that detachment is likely, the defect can become Dangerous.

Can a missing track rod end locking device fail an MOT?

Yes. A missing or ineffective steering-linkage retaining or locking device is a Major MOT defect because the joint or adjustment must remain securely retained.

Can a seriously misaligned track rod end fail an MOT?

Yes. Track rod or drag-link ends that are seriously misaligned are a Major MOT defect. This is different from ordinary wheel-alignment geometry such as toe being slightly out of specification.

Can a bent or damaged track rod end fail an MOT?

Yes. A steering-linkage component that is fractured or deformed can be a Major defect. If the damage has progressed far enough to affect steering operation, the severity can become Dangerous.

Can track rod end wear cause knocking?

Yes. Free play in a worn track rod end can produce knocking or clunking as steering load changes, although ball joints, lower-arm bushes, anti-roll-bar links and other front suspension parts can create similar noises.

Can worn track rod ends cause vague steering?

Yes. Excessive movement in a steering joint can reduce steering precision and make the car feel vague or require more correction. The source should still be confirmed because inner tie rods, the steering rack and suspension wear can produce similar symptoms.

Can track rod end wear cause uneven tyre wear?

Yes. Track rod end wear or incorrect adjustment can affect toe and contribute to uneven tyre wear. Tyre wear alone does not prove the joint is defective, so the steering component and wheel alignment should both be checked.

Do you need wheel alignment after replacing a track rod end?

Wheel alignment is normally advisable after track rod end replacement because the track rod directly affects toe adjustment. Even careful measurement during replacement may not restore the steering geometry accurately enough.

Can you drive with a worn track rod end?

A mildly developing fault should be inspected promptly, while confirmed excessive play needs repair rather than continued normal use. Obvious steering looseness, severe knocking or evidence that a joint could detach requires much more urgent action.

Should I replace a worn track rod end before the MOT?

Yes if inspection confirms excessive free play, insecurity, loss of dust-cover protection, serious misalignment or another defined steering-linkage defect. Repairing the confirmed fault before test also helps protect tyre wear and steering geometry.

How much does track rod end replacement cost in the UK?

Track rod end replacement cost varies with the vehicle, parts quality, labour access and whether adjustment threads are seized. Wheel alignment should also be allowed for because changing the outer steering joint can alter toe.

Should you buy a used car with a worn track rod end?

A worn track rod end is not automatically a reason to reject a used car, but the complete front steering and suspension should be checked. Uneven tyres, vague steering, knocking, repeated MOT advisories or seized adjustment hardware can increase the repair exposure.
