Quick Answer

Is a Shock Absorber Advisory Serious?

It can be, but the wording and actual condition matter. A shock absorber helps control suspension movement and keeps the tyre following the road surface. When damping deteriorates, the vehicle can become less stable during braking, cornering and travel over uneven surfaces.

The most important distinction is between slight fluid seepage and severe leakage or loss of damping. Current DVSA guidance specifically states that slight seepage causing a film of fluid is not a reason for MOT rejection. Severe leakage or a shock absorber that no longer functions correctly is a different matter.

Lower Immediate Risk

Slight fluid film

A light film or slight seepage does not automatically mean the shock absorber has failed. Damping performance, mounting security and the rest of the suspension corner still need to be considered.

Monitor condition
Higher Risk

Severe leakage

Heavy fluid loss can indicate seal failure and reduced damping. Under the current MOT rules, severe leakage or damage that stops the shock absorber functioning is a Major defect.

Inspect promptly
High Safety Risk

Negligible damping

If the damper has effectively lost its ability to control suspension movement, the vehicle can bounce excessively and lose consistent tyre contact. Negligible damping is a Major MOT defect.

Repair required
MOT advisory does not mean the part is condemned

An advisory records deterioration or a condition worth bringing to the driver's attention when it has not reached the applicable rejection threshold. It should be used as a maintenance warning, not treated automatically as proof that the shock absorber requires immediate replacement.

Do not diagnose a shock absorber from oil staining alone

A mechanic should determine whether the fluid is genuinely coming from the damper, whether it is only slight seepage or severe leakage, whether the unit still controls suspension movement and whether bushes, mountings or another suspension component are actually causing the symptoms.

Mechanic insight

Shock absorber diagnosis works best as a complete corner inspection. A knock may come from a top mount, bush, spring, drop link or ball joint rather than the damper itself, while irregular tyre wear can also involve wheel alignment or other suspension movement.

Advisory Meaning

What Does a Shock Absorber Advisory Mean?

A shock absorber advisory means the tester has identified deterioration or a condition worth recording, but the shock absorber had not reached the applicable MOT rejection threshold when the vehicle was tested.

That distinction matters. An advisory is not the same as saying the damper has failed, and it is not automatically an instruction to replace the part immediately. It is a warning that the condition should be understood and monitored or repaired according to its actual severity.

Advisory

Deterioration noticed

The tester has seen something worth recording, but the applicable Major or Dangerous rejection criteria were not met at the time of the inspection.

Investigate
Major Risk

Function or security affected

Severe leakage, ineffective damping, excessive bush wear or an insecure shock absorber can move the condition beyond advisory territory and into MOT failure.

Repair required
Dangerous Risk

Missing or likely to detach

A shock absorber that is missing or likely to become detached is a substantially more serious defect because suspension control and component security are directly affected.

Do not ignore

The advisory is a snapshot of condition

An MOT describes the condition found during the inspection. It does not guarantee that the shock absorber will remain in the same condition for the following twelve months.

A damper seal can deteriorate further, fluid loss can increase, bushes can develop more movement and damping performance can decline with mileage. For that reason, the useful question is not simply “Did the car pass?” but “What exactly was found and how likely is it to worsen?”

Advisory does not automatically mean replacement

Replacement should be based on the condition of the damper, its damping performance, leakage severity, mounting condition, related suspension wear and manufacturer repair guidance. A light fluid film should not be treated the same way as a heavily leaking or ineffective shock absorber.

Best first question to ask the garage

Ask whether the shock absorber is merely showing slight seepage or whether it has genuine severe leakage or weak damping. That answer usually tells you far more than the word “advisory” alone.

MOT Wording

How to Interpret Shock Absorber MOT Wording

Shock absorber wording can be confusing because drivers often use terms such as misting, seeping, leaking and failed shock interchangeably. They do not necessarily describe the same level of deterioration.

The most useful way to interpret the record is to separate appearance, security and damping performance.

Appearance

Is there only a light fluid film, or is the damper body visibly wet with substantial fluid loss?

Security

Are the upper and lower mounts secure, and are the associated bushes still capable of locating the damper correctly?

Function

Does the shock absorber still provide meaningful damping, or is the suspension effectively uncontrolled?

Why the exact wording matters

Wording or observation What it may indicate Best response
Slight seepage / light fluid film Minor seal seepage without clear evidence of severe fluid loss Inspect and monitor damping performance
Shock absorber leaking Fluid loss may be progressing beyond normal light seepage Determine whether leakage is severe and whether damping remains effective
Shock absorber bush worn Excessive movement may develop at the mounting point Inspect bush movement and mounting security
Poor or negligible damping Damper may no longer control suspension movement effectively Treat as a functional suspension defect
Shock absorber insecure Fixings, bushes or mountings may no longer locate the damper correctly Repair promptly
Do not rely on vague descriptions such as “shock leaking a bit”

Ask whether the mechanic means slight surface seepage, genuine severe leakage, or measurable loss of damping. Those are very different conditions with different repair priorities.

MOT wording and garage diagnosis are not identical jobs

The MOT determines whether the vehicle meets the inspection standard on the day. A workshop diagnosis goes further by deciding why the condition exists, how quickly it may progress and whether related suspension components should be repaired at the same time.

Key Difference

Shock Absorber Misting vs Severe Leakage

This is one of the most important distinctions on the entire page. A small amount of oil visible on a damper does not automatically mean the shock absorber has failed.

Current MOT guidance recognises that slight seepage producing a film of fluid is not itself a reason for rejection. Severe leakage or a damper that no longer functions properly is a different condition.

Slight Seepage

Thin oily film

A light film can form around the damper rod or body without the unit having lost enough fluid to stop functioning correctly.

Assess, do not condemn
Developing Leak

Noticeable wetness

Increasing wetness, oil spreading down the body or evidence that the leak is worsening deserves closer inspection even if the vehicle still feels normal.

Recheck soon
Severe Leakage

Significant fluid loss

Heavy leakage can reduce the damper's ability to resist suspension movement and may indicate internal seal failure or loss of damping effectiveness.

Repair priority

What a mechanic should look for

  • Whether the fluid is actually coming from the shock absorber.
  • Whether the surface is only lightly misted or clearly wet.
  • How far the fluid has travelled down the damper body.
  • Whether dirt has accumulated in wet oil, suggesting continuing leakage.
  • Whether one side is considerably wetter than the opposite side.
  • Whether the piston rod is damaged, corroded or contaminated.
  • Whether damping remains effective.
  • Whether the shock absorber bushes and mountings are secure.
  • Whether nearby suspension or steering components are contributing to the reported symptoms.
Oil on the suspension may come from somewhere else

Engine oil, gearbox oil, power-assisted steering fluid, driveshaft grease or another contaminant can sometimes reach suspension components. The source should be confirmed before a shock absorber is replaced.

Why a leaking damper can still feel normal initially

Deterioration is often progressive. A damper may lose some fluid and still retain enough internal resistance that the driver notices very little. As leakage or internal wear progresses, body movement and wheel control can become increasingly obvious.

Compare both sides

Comparing left and right dampers on the same axle can reveal useful differences in leakage, corrosion, bush condition and movement. However, two similarly worn dampers can both be weak, so visual symmetry alone does not prove that damping is healthy.

Suspension Function

How Shock Absorbers Control the Suspension

A shock absorber is a hydraulic damping device. Its job is not simply to make the car comfortable. It controls the movement created when the suspension spring compresses and rebounds.

Without effective damping, a spring can continue oscillating after the wheel passes over a bump. The shock absorber converts that suspension movement into heat by forcing hydraulic fluid through internal valves, reducing repeated bouncing and helping the tyre remain in more consistent contact with the road.

1. Wheel moves

A bump pushes the wheel upward and the suspension spring compresses.

2. Spring stores energy

The compressed spring naturally wants to extend again after the bump has passed.

3. Damper resists movement

Hydraulic resistance inside the shock absorber slows compression and rebound movement.

4. Wheel settles

Controlled damping helps the tyre return to stable road contact without repeated uncontrolled oscillation.

Spring and shock absorber do different jobs

Spring

Supports and absorbs movement

The spring supports vehicle weight and allows the wheel to move relative to the body when the road surface changes.

Shock Absorber

Controls that movement

The damper limits how quickly and how far the suspension continues moving after compression or rebound.

Why damping matters to safety

Tyres generate useful braking and cornering force when they maintain controlled contact with the road. Excessive wheel oscillation can make that contact less consistent, especially over poor surfaces or during rapid changes in vehicle load.

Braking

Controlled suspension movement helps keep the tyre loaded more consistently during braking.

Cornering

Effective damping helps control body movement when weight transfers from one side of the vehicle to the other.

Rough roads

Dampers control repeated wheel movement as the tyre follows bumps, dips and uneven surfaces.

Shock absorbers do not normally support vehicle ride height

On most conventional systems the spring carries the vehicle weight while the damper controls movement. A vehicle sitting noticeably low on one corner therefore often points toward a spring, air suspension or related ride-height problem rather than the shock absorber alone.

Strut and shock absorber are related but not always identical

Some vehicles use a MacPherson strut where the damper forms part of a load-bearing suspension assembly and provides structural wheel location. Other vehicles use a separate shock absorber alongside a separate coil spring. Repair procedures and labour times therefore vary considerably between vehicles.

Symptoms

Symptoms of Worn or Weak Shock Absorbers

Shock absorbers usually deteriorate progressively rather than failing instantly. Because drivers adapt to gradual changes, damping can become noticeably weaker before the vehicle owner realises how much handling quality has been lost.

Repeated bouncing

The body continues moving several times after a bump instead of settling promptly.

Floaty handling

The vehicle can feel loosely controlled or slow to settle after steering corrections and road undulations.

Excessive nose dive

Increased forward body movement under braking can accompany weak front damping, although springs and other suspension faults should also be considered.

Excessive body roll

A vehicle may feel less controlled during cornering if damping is poor, although anti-roll bars, springs, bushes and tyres can also affect body movement.

Knocking over bumps

A knock may involve the damper, top mount or damper bush, but ball joints, drop links, springs and suspension arms can produce similar noises.

Irregular tyre wear

Poor wheel control may contribute to uneven or cupped tread wear, but tyre pressure, geometry and other suspension wear must also be checked.

Increased sensitivity to bumps

The vehicle may feel unsettled when one wheel hits a pothole, expansion joint or rough road surface.

Visible fluid loss

A damper body that is heavily wet with hydraulic fluid deserves inspection for severe leakage.

Uneven behaviour left to right

One side may react differently over bumps if damping or suspension condition differs significantly across the axle.

Symptoms do not prove the shock absorber is faulty

Several suspension, steering and tyre problems can imitate weak dampers. A proper inspection should therefore identify the failed component rather than replacing a shock absorber simply because the vehicle feels unsettled.

Treat sudden handling changes differently from gradual wear

If the vehicle suddenly becomes unstable, one corner begins moving abnormally, a suspension component starts striking the body or a mounting appears loose, stop treating the problem as routine advisory maintenance and have the vehicle assessed promptly.

Compare symptoms with the MOT advisory location

If the MOT identifies the left rear damper but the noise comes from the right front suspension, do not assume one explains the other. Matching symptom location to the recorded defect can prevent unnecessary parts replacement.

Common Causes

What Causes Shock Absorbers to Deteriorate?

Shock absorbers work continuously whenever the suspension moves. Internal seals, hydraulic fluid, valves, bushes, piston rods and mounting points therefore experience repeated mechanical load throughout the vehicle's life.

Age and mileage are common reasons for gradual deterioration, but road conditions, corrosion, impact damage and faults elsewhere in the suspension can accelerate wear.

Age & Mileage

Internal wear

Seals and internal damping components wear gradually after repeated compression and rebound cycles.

Seal Wear

Hydraulic fluid leakage

Deteriorated seals can allow fluid to escape around the piston rod, progressing from slight seepage to more substantial leakage.

Road Damage

Pothole or impact loads

Severe impacts can damage the damper, piston rod, mountings or surrounding suspension components.

Corrosion

Damaged piston rod

Corrosion or surface damage on an exposed piston rod can damage the seal as the rod moves through it and lead to leakage.

Bush Wear

Worn mounting bushes

Rubber bushes can crack, soften, separate or develop excessive movement, creating knocking and reducing mounting control.

Mounting Wear

Top mount deterioration

On strut-equipped vehicles, worn top mounts or bearings can create noise and movement that may initially be blamed on the damper.

Contamination

Dirt around the seal

Damaged dust boots can expose the piston rod and seal area to dirt, water and road contamination.

Suspension Faults

Damaged spring or geometry

Broken springs, incorrect ride height, accident damage or other suspension defects can place abnormal loads on a damper.

Heavy Use

Repeated load and poor roads

Heavy vehicle loading, towing, rough roads and frequent pothole impacts can increase suspension workload and accelerate wear.

Why one shock absorber may deteriorate before the other

Dampers on the same axle operate under broadly similar conditions, but they do not always wear at identical rates. One side may encounter more pothole impacts, water contamination, corrosion or previous suspension damage.

This is why an advisory on one damper should trigger inspection of the opposite side rather than an automatic assumption that both units have failed.

Mileage alone cannot diagnose shock absorber condition

There is no single mileage at which every shock absorber becomes unserviceable. Vehicle weight, damper design, road conditions, loading, driving style and previous repairs can create very different wear rates between vehicles.

Do not replace a damper before checking the surrounding suspension

A leaking or worn shock absorber may exist alongside a broken spring, damaged bump stop, worn top mount, loose bush or corroded fixing. Replacing only the damper without inspecting the complete suspension corner can leave the original symptom unresolved.

Diagnosis before parts replacement saves money

Confirming the leak source, damping condition and associated suspension wear before ordering parts helps avoid replacing a good shock absorber because a nearby bush, mount or spring was actually responsible for the complaint.

Diagnostic Inspection

How a Mechanic Checks a Shock Absorber Advisory

A proper shock absorber inspection should do more than look for oil on the outside of the damper. The technician needs to establish whether there is genuine leakage, whether the unit is securely mounted, whether its bushes and surrounding suspension components are sound and whether damping performance is still effective.

The best diagnosis combines visual inspection, suspension movement, comparison across the axle and evidence from the way the vehicle behaves on the road.

1. Confirm the advisory location

Identify the exact corner recorded on the MOT history and confirm whether the affected component is a separate damper or part of a suspension strut assembly.

2. Inspect the damper body

Look for a light fluid film, substantial wetness, physical damage, corrosion, a bent piston rod or contamination around the seal.

3. Check mountings and bushes

Inspect the upper and lower fixing points for looseness, excessive bush movement, deterioration, corrosion and signs that the damper is no longer securely located.

4. Assess damping

Determine whether the shock absorber still controls suspension movement effectively and compare behaviour with the opposite side.

Visual checks around the shock absorber

Damper Body

Leakage and damage

  • Light fluid film or misting.
  • Heavy or spreading hydraulic fluid.
  • Damaged or corroded piston rod.
  • Bent or dented damper body.
  • Damaged dust cover or gaiter.
Mountings

Security and bushes

  • Loose or missing fixings.
  • Excessive bush movement.
  • Split or separated rubber bushes.
  • Corroded mounting points.
  • Abnormal movement during suspension loading.
Surrounding Parts

Whole suspension corner

  • Coil spring condition.
  • Top mount condition.
  • Bump stop and dust boot.
  • Suspension arms and bushes.
  • Ball joints and anti-roll bar links.
Fluid staining alone is not enough to condemn the damper

Current MOT guidance specifically distinguishes slight seepage from severe leakage. A mechanic should therefore establish the amount of fluid loss and whether damping function has been affected instead of recommending replacement from a light oily film alone.

Compare both sides of the axle

The opposite damper provides a useful comparison for leakage, corrosion, bush condition and suspension movement. However, two equally old dampers can deteriorate together, so identical appearance does not automatically mean both are healthy.

Mechanic insight

Before ordering a shock absorber, inspect the complete suspension corner. Replacing a damper will not cure a knock caused by a loose drop link, worn top mount, broken spring or excessive movement in a suspension arm bush.

Damping Assessment

How Weak Shock Absorber Damping Is Identified

The purpose of a shock absorber is to control suspension oscillation. A damper can therefore look reasonably clean externally and still be mechanically weak inside.

This is why leakage inspection and damping assessment should be treated as related but separate parts of the diagnosis.

Static Clue

Excessive continued movement

If suspension movement continues excessively instead of settling promptly, damping may be weak. This is an indication rather than a complete diagnosis.

Road Behaviour

Poor body control

Excessive pitching, bouncing or slow settling after undulations can indicate inadequate damping when other suspension faults have been ruled out.

Axle Comparison

Uneven response

A large difference in suspension control between left and right sides may help identify one weak damper or another fault at that corner.

Why the traditional bounce test has limitations

Pressing down on a vehicle corner and watching how quickly it settles can reveal an obviously ineffective shock absorber, but it should not be treated as a precision test of damper condition.

Modern suspension spring rates, anti-roll bars, vehicle weight, suspension geometry and damper design can make a manual bounce test difficult to interpret. A damper may also have lost some performance without being weak enough to produce dramatic bouncing while the car is stationary.

Do not diagnose by counting bounces alone

A commonly repeated rule such as “more than two bounces means the shock is failed” is too simplistic for modern vehicles. The result should be considered alongside leakage, road behaviour, mounting condition and the rest of the suspension.

Road-test clues a technician may consider

  • Excessive bouncing after a road depression or speed hump.
  • Repeated body movement after the initial suspension input.
  • Poor control over undulating roads.
  • Excessive pitching during acceleration or braking.
  • Unsettled behaviour when one wheel hits a bump.
  • Noticeable difference between left and right suspension response.
  • Irregular tyre wear consistent with poor wheel control.
Negligible damping is an MOT failure condition

The current DVSA manual requires a shock absorber to be rejected if negligible damping effect becomes evident during the inspection. This is classified as a Major defect.

Leakage and weak damping do not always progress together

A damper may show visible leakage while retaining some useful damping, or it may have poor internal damping without dramatic external leakage. Both condition and function matter.

Fault Differentiation

What Else Can Feel Like a Worn Shock Absorber?

Bouncing, knocking, poor stability and uneven tyre wear are not unique to shock absorbers. Several faults around the same suspension corner can create similar symptoms.

This is why replacing a shock absorber solely because the car knocks or feels unsettled can lead to unnecessary expense.

Coil Spring

Broken or weakened spring

A fractured spring can change ride height, create metallic noises and alter suspension movement. The broken end may also move in the spring seat.

Top Mount

Worn strut mounting

A worn top mount can knock over bumps and may allow excessive movement at the top of a MacPherson strut.

Drop Link

Anti-roll bar link wear

Worn link joints commonly create rattles or knocking over broken road surfaces without the shock absorber itself being faulty.

Bushes

Suspension bush movement

Split or worn rubber bushes can allow suspension arms to move more than intended, causing knocks, instability or changes in wheel geometry.

Ball Joint

Excessive joint play

Wear in a suspension ball joint can produce knocking and unwanted wheel movement and may become a significant safety fault.

Tyre / Wheel

Tyre or wheel problem

Damaged tyres, wheel imbalance, distorted wheels or incorrect tyre pressures can create vibration or instability that may initially feel suspension related.

Wheel Geometry

Alignment problem

Incorrect toe, camber or other geometry can contribute to tyre wear and unstable handling without the shock absorber being the primary cause.

Wheel Bearing

Bearing wear

Excessive bearing play or roughness can affect wheel control and create noise that may be mistaken for suspension damage.

Structural Damage

Bent or damaged suspension parts

Pothole or accident damage can bend suspension arms, struts, subframes or mounting points and change how the vehicle behaves.

If a vehicle has a shock absorber advisory and also develops a suspension noise, the two may be connected, but they should not be assumed to have the same cause until the suspension is inspected.

Severity Guide

How Urgent Is a Shock Absorber Advisory?

There is no single urgency level that applies to every shock absorber advisory. A light film of fluid on a fully effective damper is very different from a severely leaking unit with poor damping or insecure mountings.

Use the actual condition and vehicle behaviour to decide priority.

Lower Immediate Risk

Slight seepage, no symptoms

The damper has only a light fluid film, remains secure and appears to control suspension movement normally.

Monitor and recheck
Medium Priority

Deterioration is progressing

Wetness is increasing, a bush is deteriorating, minor symptoms are developing or the same advisory keeps returning.

Plan inspection / repair
High Priority

Leakage or control affected

Severe leakage, obvious weak damping, excessive bush movement, insecure mounting or significant handling symptoms require prompt attention.

Repair promptly

There is no universal “safe mileage” after an advisory

A shock absorber advisory does not come with a guaranteed number of miles that can safely be driven. Progression depends on the nature of the defect, road conditions, vehicle load, mileage and the condition of the rest of the suspension.

Repeated advisories deserve more attention

If the same shock absorber appears repeatedly in MOT history, compare the wording year by year. Recurring deterioration without repair may indicate that a previously minor condition is progressing toward a rejectable defect.

Driving Safety

Can You Drive With a Shock Absorber Advisory?

A vehicle that has only received an MOT advisory has not failed the MOT for that condition. However, an MOT pass should not be interpreted as permission to ignore a developing suspension problem.

Whether continued driving is sensible depends on the actual damper condition, mounting security, damping performance and whether the vehicle remains stable and roadworthy.

Usually Lower Risk

Light seepage only

If inspection confirms only slight seepage, damping remains effective, the mountings are secure and the car behaves normally, continued use may be reasonable while the condition is monitored.

Arrange Inspection

Symptoms are developing

Increasing bouncing, knocking, body movement, tyre wear or visible leakage means the advisory should be reassessed rather than left until the next MOT.

Stop and Assess

Vehicle control affected

If the suspension feels seriously unstable, a damper appears loose, a component may detach or wheel control is substantially affected, normal driving should stop until the vehicle has been inspected.

Signs that should change your driving decision

  • Repeated uncontrolled bouncing after bumps.
  • The vehicle feels unusually floaty or difficult to settle.
  • One corner reacts very differently from the other side.
  • A shock absorber or mounting appears loose.
  • Hydraulic fluid loss becomes substantial.
  • Knocking becomes severe or is accompanied by visible movement.
  • Tyre wear is becoming rapid or irregular.
  • Steering or braking stability has noticeably deteriorated.
  • A suspension component appears damaged after a pothole or impact.
An MOT pass is not a twelve-month roadworthiness guarantee

The MOT records whether the vehicle met the applicable inspection requirements at the time of the test. Vehicle owners remain responsible for keeping the car roadworthy afterwards, and a shock absorber can deteriorate between MOT inspections.

Stop normal driving if component security is in doubt

A shock absorber that appears likely to detach, a badly damaged suspension mounting or a serious loss of vehicle control should not be treated as an ordinary advisory. Arrange professional assessment or recovery rather than continuing to use the vehicle normally.

Safety depends on the complete suspension corner

A car can have more than one developing suspension fault at the same time. Shock absorber condition should therefore be considered alongside springs, bushes, joints, tyres, wheel bearings and suspension mountings when deciding whether the vehicle is safe to continue using.

Current MOT Rules

Shock Absorber MOT Rules UK

Shock absorbers are inspected as part of the suspension section of the MOT. The tester is not simply looking for any trace of oil. The important questions are whether the damper is securely attached, whether leakage is severe, whether the bushes are excessively worn and whether meaningful damping effect remains.

The current DVSA manual also makes an important distinction that is often misunderstood: slight seepage that leaves only a film of fluid on the shock absorber is not, by itself, a reason for rejection.

Current DVSA clarification

The MOT inspection manual was updated on 1 June 2026 to make clear that slight seepage causing a film of fluid on a shock absorber is not a reason for rejection. This should not be confused with severe leakage or loss of damping performance.

Misting is not the same as severe leakage

Do not assume that any visible oil means an MOT failure. A tester must distinguish slight seepage from severe leakage and also consider whether the shock absorber still functions correctly.

An advisory is not a guaranteed future failure

A shock absorber advisory means the condition did not meet the applicable rejection threshold on the test day. It may remain stable, improve after repair or deteriorate into a Major or Dangerous defect before the next MOT.

Also remember that the MOT checks roadworthiness criteria at the time of inspection. A vehicle owner remains responsible for keeping the suspension safe between tests.

Repair Decision

Should You Replace a Shock Absorber After an Advisory?

Not every advisory requires immediate replacement. The correct decision depends on what the tester or mechanic actually found, how effective the damper remains and whether other suspension components are worn.

The three practical options are usually monitor, replace the affected unit or pair, or repair the complete suspension corner if additional faults are present.

Monitor

Slight seepage only

Monitoring may be reasonable where there is only a light fluid film, damping remains effective, the bushes are sound and the vehicle has no handling symptoms.

Recheck condition
Replace

Leakage or weak damping

Replacement becomes much more likely where leakage is substantial, damping is weak or the damper itself is physically damaged.

Repair likely
Inspect Complete Corner

Multiple suspension faults

If the spring, top mount, bump stop, bush or another suspension component is also worn, combining repairs can avoid paying labour twice.

Plan complete repair

One shock absorber or both sides?

Shock absorbers are commonly replaced in axle pairs because balanced damping from left to right helps maintain predictable suspension behaviour. If one damper is new and the opposite unit is substantially weaker, the vehicle may respond differently across the axle.

Pair replacement is a workshop recommendation rather than an MOT rule. The correct repair should follow the condition of both dampers and the vehicle manufacturer's repair information.

Replace one side

This may be reasonable in some circumstances, particularly where the opposite damper is relatively new, confirmed serviceable and manufacturer guidance allows individual replacement.

Replace the axle pair

Often preferred where both dampers are similar age and mileage, especially when one has failed through normal wear rather than isolated damage.

Parts worth inspecting while the suspension is apart

  • Opposite-side shock absorber.
  • Coil spring.
  • Strut top mount and bearing where fitted.
  • Shock absorber mounting bushes.
  • Dust boot and bump stop.
  • Spring seat.
  • Anti-roll bar link.
  • Suspension arm bushes and ball joints.
  • Tyre condition and wear pattern.
Do not replace both dampers blindly

Pair replacement is often sensible, but both sides should still be inspected. A relatively new opposite-side damper may not need replacement, while an older vehicle may require additional springs, mounts or bushes that matter more than simply fitting two dampers.

Consider labour overlap

On a MacPherson strut, replacing a worn top mount, bump stop or spring at the same time as the damper can be economical because much of the assembly is already being removed.

DIY vs Garage

Can You Replace Shock Absorbers Yourself?

The difficulty varies enormously with suspension design. Some separate rear dampers can be comparatively straightforward for an experienced DIY mechanic, while front strut assemblies can involve compressed coil springs, seized fasteners and safety-critical suspension geometry.

A repair should only be attempted if the correct tools, lifting equipment, torque information and mechanical experience are available.

Possible DIY

Separate rear shock absorber

Some vehicles use a simple separate shock absorber with accessible upper and lower mounting bolts and a coil spring that remains in place.

  • Correct axle support is essential.
  • Suspension may need supporting at ride position.
  • Fixings must be tightened to the correct specification.
  • Seized or corroded bolts can make the job much harder.
Garage Recommended

MacPherson strut assembly

A strut commonly requires removal of the complete suspension unit and may require controlled compression of a high-energy coil spring before the damper can be separated.

  • Spring compressors must be suitable and correctly positioned.
  • Incorrect spring handling can cause serious injury.
  • Hub-to-strut removal can affect wheel alignment.
  • Top mounts and bearings should be checked while dismantled.

Jobs best left to a specialist

Adaptive dampers

Electronically controlled dampers can require diagnostic checks, coding, calibration or model-specific procedures.

Seized suspension hardware

Corroded bolts, seized pinch bolts and damaged threads can turn a straightforward repair into a more involved suspension job.

Structural mounting damage

Corrosion or damage around the shock absorber or strut mounting should be assessed before a replacement component is fitted.

Coil spring safety

A compressed suspension spring stores a large amount of energy. Incorrect spring compressor use can cause severe injury. If you do not have appropriate equipment and experience, use a professional workshop for strut dismantling.

Alignment may be required afterwards

Depending on the suspension design and which fixings are disturbed, wheel alignment or geometry checks may be sensible after front strut replacement. This should be included when comparing repair quotes.

Next MOT Risk

Will a Shock Absorber Advisory Fail the Next MOT?

Not automatically. An advisory means the condition had not reached the applicable MOT rejection threshold when the vehicle was tested. What happens at the next MOT depends on whether the shock absorber remains stable, is repaired or deteriorates further.

Shock absorber deterioration can progress gradually. Slight seepage can develop into more substantial leakage, a mounting bush can develop excessive movement and an ageing damper can lose more of its damping effectiveness with continued use.

Lower Risk

Slight seepage remains stable

A light film of fluid with effective damping and secure mountings may remain below the MOT rejection threshold if the condition does not progress.

Continue monitoring
Rising Risk

Leakage or bush wear increases

Increasing fluid loss, developing bush movement or new handling symptoms indicate that the condition is moving in the wrong direction.

Inspect before MOT
Failure Risk

Major defect develops

Severe leakage, negligible damping, excessive shock absorber bush wear or insecure attachment can turn the previous advisory into an MOT failure.

Repair required

Signs the advisory may be getting worse

  • A light oily film develops into obvious wet leakage.
  • More fluid begins spreading down the damper body.
  • The vehicle takes longer to settle after bumps.
  • Body movement becomes more noticeable on uneven roads.
  • Knocking develops at the affected suspension corner.
  • A shock absorber bush develops visible or excessive movement.
  • Tyres start wearing irregularly.
  • The vehicle feels less settled during braking or cornering.
  • The same shock absorber appears again in the next MOT history entry.

How to reduce the chance of a surprise next MOT failure

Check the exact defect

Find out whether the original advisory involved slight seepage, bush deterioration, corrosion or another specific condition.

Reinspect before test day

If the advisory has been left unrepaired, have the suspension checked before the next MOT rather than discovering progression during the test.

Repair related faults

Address damaged springs, mounts, bushes or tyres at the same time where inspection shows they are contributing to the suspension problem.

Do not use the previous MOT pass as a condition guarantee

The previous MOT describes what the tester found at that inspection. It does not guarantee that a shock absorber will remain below the rejection threshold until the next test.

Repeated advisory wording is useful evidence

If you have several years of MOT history, compare the shock absorber entries. A condition that appears repeatedly without evidence of repair deserves closer inspection even if the vehicle continues to pass.

Used Car Buying

Should You Buy a Car With a Shock Absorber Advisory?

A shock absorber advisory does not automatically make a used car a bad purchase. The important questions are what the advisory actually means, whether the condition has progressed and how much it will cost to put the suspension into good condition.

A car with slight seepage and otherwise healthy suspension is a very different buying proposition from one with repeated damper advisories, uneven tyres, suspension knocks and unstable handling.

Lower Buying Risk

Minor condition confirmed

Slight seepage with effective damping, secure mountings, normal tyre wear and no related symptoms may only require monitoring.

Potentially acceptable
Negotiation Point

Replacement approaching

If the damper is clearly deteriorating but the repair is straightforward, obtain a quote and allow for the cost when negotiating the purchase price.

Price the repair
Higher Buying Risk

Multiple suspension problems

Repeated advisories combined with leaking dampers, worn tyres, knocking, damaged springs or poor handling can indicate a broader history of delayed suspension maintenance.

Inspect before buying

Shock absorber used-car inspection checklist

  • Read the exact wording of the latest MOT advisory.
  • Check whether the same damper appears in previous MOT history.
  • Ask whether the shock absorber has since been replaced.
  • Request invoices for suspension work where available.
  • Look for substantial oil leakage around each damper.
  • Compare the left and right suspension corners.
  • Check the tyres for irregular or uneven tread wear.
  • Listen for knocking over rough surfaces and speed humps.
  • Check whether the car settles normally after road undulations.
  • Watch for excessive body movement during braking.
  • Check that the car does not feel floaty or unstable at speed.
  • Inspect the springs and visible suspension mountings.
  • Ask whether replacement would involve standard or adaptive dampers.
  • Obtain a repair quote before agreeing the vehicle price.

Frequently Asked Questions

Shock Absorber Advisory FAQs

Straight answers to the most common questions UK drivers ask about shock absorber MOT advisories, seepage, leakage, damping, driving, repair costs and future MOT risk.

What does a shock absorber advisory mean?

A shock absorber advisory means the tester has identified deterioration or a condition worth bringing to the driver's attention, but it had not reached the applicable MOT rejection threshold at the time of the test.

Is a shock absorber advisory serious?

It can be. The seriousness depends on whether the shock absorber is only showing slight seepage, has severe leakage, has worn mountings or bushes, or has lost damping performance.

Does slight shock absorber seepage fail an MOT?

No. Current DVSA guidance says slight seepage causing a film of fluid on a shock absorber is not a reason for MOT rejection.

What is the difference between shock absorber misting and severe leakage?

Misting or slight seepage usually leaves a light film of fluid, while severe leakage involves much greater fluid loss and can indicate that the shock absorber is no longer functioning correctly.

Can a leaking shock absorber fail an MOT?

Yes. A shock absorber showing severe leakage, or damaged to the extent that it does not function, is a Major MOT defect.

Can weak damping fail an MOT?

Yes. A shock absorber with negligible damping effect is classified as a Major MOT defect.

Can a worn shock absorber bush fail an MOT?

Yes. An excessively worn shock absorber bush is classified as a Major MOT defect.

Can an insecure shock absorber fail an MOT?

Yes. A shock absorber insecurely attached to the chassis or axle is a Major defect, while one that is missing or likely to become detached is classified as Dangerous.

Can I drive with a shock absorber advisory?

Continued driving may be reasonable if the vehicle remains roadworthy, damping is effective and there are no serious handling symptoms, but the advisory should be investigated rather than ignored.

What are the symptoms of worn shock absorbers?

Possible symptoms include excessive bouncing after bumps, a floaty feeling, increased body movement, nose dive under braking, reduced stability, knocking noises and irregular tyre wear.

Can worn shock absorbers cause uneven tyre wear?

Yes. Poor damping can allow excessive wheel movement and contribute to irregular tyre contact and wear, although alignment and other suspension faults should also be checked.

Should shock absorbers be replaced in pairs?

Shock absorbers are commonly replaced in axle pairs to maintain balanced damping, although the correct repair decision should follow vehicle manufacturer guidance and inspection of both sides.

How much does shock absorber replacement cost in the UK?

Replacement cost varies with vehicle type, axle, suspension design, parts quality, labour time, seized fixings and whether the vehicle uses conventional or electronically controlled dampers.

Will a shock absorber advisory fail the next MOT?

Not automatically. The next MOT assesses the condition present on that day, but deterioration can progress into severe leakage, ineffective damping, excessive bush wear or another rejectable defect.

Can another suspension fault feel like a bad shock absorber?

Yes. Springs, top mounts, bushes, ball joints, suspension arms, anti-roll bar links, tyres and wheel alignment can produce symptoms that overlap with shock absorber faults.

Can I replace a shock absorber myself?

Some separate rear dampers can be mechanically straightforward, but strut assemblies may involve compressed coil springs, seized suspension fixings and alignment considerations, so professional repair is often the safer choice.

Should I buy a used car with a shock absorber advisory?

It may still be a reasonable purchase if the fault is properly assessed and the repair cost is reflected in the price. Repeated advisories, severe leakage, unstable handling or related tyre and suspension faults justify greater caution.

Does a valid MOT mean the shock absorbers are good for another year?

No. An MOT records the condition against the test requirements at the time of inspection. Shock absorbers can deteriorate afterwards, so the vehicle still has to remain roadworthy throughout the year.
