Most Common MOT Advisories Explained UK

An MOT advisory is an early warning that something on your vehicle is worn, corroded, leaking, deteriorating or close to becoming a defect. The vehicle may have passed today, but the advisory is telling you what could become unsafe, expensive or an MOT failure before the next test.

Plain English answer

What does an MOT advisory mean?

An MOT advisory means the tester has seen a problem that is worth recording, but the problem was not bad enough to fail the vehicle at the time of the MOT. It may be wear, corrosion, deterioration, leakage, damage, poor condition or a part that is close to becoming defective.

This is why many drivers get confused. The car has passed, but the MOT certificate still lists faults. That does not mean the car is perfect. It means the car met the minimum MOT standard on the day, while still having items that need watching, pricing or repairing.

The safest way to use advisories is to treat them as your early repair plan. They help you decide what to fix first, what to monitor, what to budget for and what could become a failure at the next MOT.

Important

Why MOT advisories matter

Some advisories are simple maintenance jobs. Others involve safety-critical systems such as tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, brake pipes and structural areas of the vehicle. Those advisories deserve more attention because they affect how the vehicle stops, steers, grips the road or protects occupants.

A tyre advisory can become an illegal tyre. A brake pipe corrosion advisory can become a brake fluid leak. A suspension advisory can turn into knocking, poor handling and uneven tyre wear. An oil leak can spread across the engine or underside. Structural corrosion can become welding work or make an older car uneconomical to repair.

For used car buyers, advisories are also very useful. They show whether the previous owner repaired issues or allowed the same defects to appear year after year.

Mechanic-style explanation

How To Read an MOT Advisory Like a Mechanic

Do not only read the advisory wording. Think about the system affected, whether it is safety-critical, whether symptoms are already present and whether the same problem appeared before.

Step 1

Identify the vehicle system

First decide what system the advisory belongs to. Brakes, steering, suspension, tyres and structure are higher priority than cosmetic or convenience items.

Step 2

Check if it affects safety

Ask whether the defect could affect braking, grip, steering, stability, visibility, emissions, fire risk or structural strength. If yes, it should move higher on your repair list.

Step 3

Look for symptoms

Knocking, grinding, pulling, vibration, burning smells, warning lights, oil spots, bouncing or longer stopping distance mean the advisory may already be affecting the car.

Step 4

Check previous MOT history

A one-off advisory may be normal wear. The same advisory appearing every year may suggest the owner ignored maintenance or the fault is getting worse.

Step 5

Estimate repair cost before buying

If you are buying a used car, get prices for the advisories before agreeing a final price. Brake pipes, corrosion and suspension work can change the real value of the car.

Step 6

Plan the next MOT early

Do not leave advisories until the next MOT week. Fix high-risk items early and use the next test as confirmation, not as the first time you deal with the problem.

Repair priority

How Soon Should You Fix an MOT Advisory?

There is no single time limit for every advisory. The right timing depends on what part is affected, whether the car has symptoms, how much you drive and how close the next MOT is.

Fix urgently

Do not leave these

Corroded brake pipes, low tyre tread, serious brake wear, steering play, structural corrosion, leaking shock absorbers and suspension looseness should be inspected quickly.

Fix soon

Plan these repairs

Brake disc corrosion, worn suspension bushes, minor oil leaks, exhaust corrosion and early shock absorber misting should be planned before they become bigger problems.

Monitor

Lower-risk advisories

Number plates, wipers, washer aim, cloudy lights and minor surface corrosion may be lower urgency, but they should still be repaired before becoming failures.

Symptoms that make an advisory urgent

  • Grinding, squealing, pulsing or weak brakes.
  • Vehicle pulling to one side when braking or driving.
  • Knocking, clunking or vibration from suspension.
  • Tyres wearing unevenly or close to the legal limit.
  • Oil spots under the vehicle or burning oil smell.
  • Rust holes, flaking metal or corrosion near structural areas.
  • Exhaust blowing, fumes, rattling or loose mountings.
  • Longer stopping distance, poor grip or unstable handling.

Used car MOT advisory checklist

  • Check whether the same advisory appears on previous MOT records.
  • Ask whether the advisory has already been repaired.
  • Look for invoices, parts receipts and garage inspection notes.
  • Compare advisories with mileage and service history.
  • Be cautious with brake pipes, structural corrosion and multiple suspension advisories.
  • Use advisories to negotiate fairly.
  • Do not assume a fresh MOT means the car has no upcoming repair costs.
Insurance question

Do MOT advisories affect insurance?

An MOT advisory does not automatically mean the vehicle is uninsured. However, drivers are expected to keep vehicles roadworthy. If a known safety-related defect is ignored and later contributes to an accident, it may create problems.

This matters most with tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, brake pipes and structural corrosion. These are not cosmetic items. They can affect stopping distance, stability, steering control and crash safety.

Driving question

How long can you drive with an MOT advisory?

There is no fixed time limit for every advisory. A worn wiper may be low priority, but a brake pipe, tyre close to the legal limit or structural corrosion advisory should be checked much sooner.

The more safety-critical the part is, the less time you should leave it. If symptoms are present, treat the advisory as urgent.

Frequently asked questions

FAQs About Common MOT Advisories

Straight answers to common UK driver questions about MOT advisories, repair urgency, safety and buying decisions.

What does an MOT advisory mean?

It means the tester has seen a defect, wear, corrosion, leak or deterioration that was not serious enough to fail the MOT at the time, but should be monitored or repaired.

Can I still drive with an MOT advisory?

Usually yes, but it depends on the advisory. Tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, brake pipes, oil leaks and corrosion should be treated more seriously than cosmetic advisories.

Will an MOT advisory fail next year?

It can. Tyres close to the limit, corroded brake pipes, worn brake discs, low brake pads, leaking shock absorbers, suspension wear and structural corrosion can become failures.

Are MOT advisories expensive to fix?

Some are cheap, such as wipers or number plates. Others can be expensive, especially brake pipes, structural corrosion, welding, suspension repairs and difficult oil leaks.

Do MOT advisories affect insurance?

An advisory does not automatically invalidate insurance, but knowingly ignoring a safety-related defect may create problems if the defect contributes to an accident.

Read insurance guide →

How long can you drive with an MOT advisory?

There is no single time limit. A worn wiper may be low priority, but a brake pipe, low tyre tread or structural corrosion advisory should be checked much sooner.

Read driving time guide →

Should I buy a car with advisories?

You can, but check the type of advisory, whether it repeats each year and whether repair costs are reflected in the price. Be careful with corrosion, brake pipes and multiple suspension advisories.

Read buying guide →

What is the most serious MOT advisory?

Brake pipe corrosion, structural corrosion, tyres close to the legal limit, brake defects and steering or suspension play are among the advisories that should be treated most seriously.
