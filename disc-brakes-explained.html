Disc Brakes Explained

Disc brakes are the most common braking system on modern cars. Understanding how they work, what wears out and what the warning signs mean helps you make better decisions about maintenance, repair costs and safety.

How disc brakes work

Disc brakes use hydraulic pressure to clamp two brake pads against a rotating metal disc (the rotor) that is bolted to the wheel hub. When you press the brake pedal, fluid travels from the master cylinder down the brake line and into the caliper. The caliper contains one or more pistons that are pushed outward by this pressure, squeezing the pads firmly against both faces of the disc.

The friction between the pads and the disc converts the vehicle's kinetic energy into heat, which slows the wheel. Because the disc is exposed to the air — unlike a drum — heat dissipates rapidly, giving disc brakes a significant advantage under repeated or heavy braking.

The moment you release the pedal, hydraulic pressure drops. Small return springs and the slight flex of the caliper seals pull the pistons back a fraction of a millimetre, allowing the disc to spin freely with minimal residual drag. This snap-back action is why a well-maintained disc brake feels progressive and consistent from the very first press of the pedal.

  • Self-cleaning: The wiping action of the pads on the disc removes water and light surface rust with each brake application, maintaining consistent bite in wet conditions.
  • Easy inspection: The disc and pad thickness can be measured visually or with a calliper, making wear assessment straightforward.
  • Fade resistance: Vented and cross-drilled discs channel away heat more aggressively, reducing brake fade during prolonged descents or track use.
Main parts

Disc brake components explained

Brake disc (rotor)

The cast-iron or composite disc is bolted to the wheel hub and rotates with the wheel. It is the surface the brake pads clamp against. Discs are measured for minimum thickness — once worn below that mark they must be replaced.

Brake pads

Pads consist of a steel backing plate bonded to a friction material compound. They sit inside the caliper on either side of the disc. As hydraulic pressure builds, the pads are forced against the disc to create the stopping force.

Brake caliper

The caliper is the hydraulic clamp that houses the brake pads and pistons. A floating caliper slides on guide pins to keep even pressure on both pad faces. A fixed caliper uses pistons on both sides for higher-performance applications.

Caliper bracket

The caliper bracket bolts to the stub axle or upright and holds the caliper in position. It carries the braking torque load and must be free of cracks, corrosion and stripped threads. Worn slide pin bores in the bracket cause uneven pad wear.

Brake hose and hydraulics

A flexible rubber or braided hose connects the hard brake pipe on the body to the caliper on the moving suspension. It must flex without cracking or restricting flow. A swollen or cracked hose can act as a one-way valve, causing a stuck caliper.

Wheel hub and bearing

The hub is the mounting point for both the disc and wheel. The wheel bearing allows the hub to rotate smoothly around the stub axle. Excessive bearing play causes disc run-out, which leads to pulsation under braking and uneven disc wear.

Types of disc brakes

Solid discs

Solid discs are a single flat plate of cast iron with no internal cooling channels. They are lighter and less expensive to manufacture, making them the standard fitment on rear axles and smaller, lighter vehicles where braking loads are lower. Because they have less thermal mass and no ventilation, they heat up more quickly under sustained braking. On the MOT, solid discs are assessed for minimum thickness, scoring, cracking and corrosion in the same way as any other disc type.

Vented discs

Vented discs have two parallel faces joined by a series of curved or straight internal fins, creating air channels through the centre of the disc. As the disc spins, air is drawn in at the hub and expelled at the outer edge, carrying heat away far more efficiently than a solid disc. They are fitted to front axles on almost all modern cars, and to all four corners on performance and heavier vehicles. Greater thermal capacity means longer fade-free braking. Minimum thickness limits are higher than for equivalent solid discs, so wear must be checked carefully.

Drilled and slotted discs

Drilled discs have holes through the disc face that allow hot gases and water to escape quickly, reducing fade and improving wet-weather bite. Slotted discs have machined grooves across the face that continuously clean and re-edge the pad surface, maintaining consistent friction. Both types are common on performance and modified cars. The trade-off is reduced thermal mass and, on cross-drilled discs, a greater risk of cracking around the drill holes under extreme heat cycles. An MOT tester will fail a disc with a crack that extends from a hole to the edge, so condition must be monitored closely.

Signs your disc brakes need attention

Squealing or squeaking

A high-pitched squeal when braking is usually the pad wear indicator — a small metal tab designed to contact the disc when pads are near their minimum thickness. It can also be caused by glazed pads, dust, or light surface rust after the car has been standing. Urgency: moderate. Have pad thickness checked within the next few hundred miles. Ignoring it risks a grinding failure.

Grinding noise

A harsh metallic grinding sound means the pad friction material has worn away completely and the steel backing plate is contacting the disc directly. The disc is being scored with every brake application and may already be beyond minimum thickness. Urgency: high. Stop driving and arrange inspection immediately. Continued use will damage the disc, caliper and could cause brake failure.

Pulsation or judder

A pulsing sensation through the brake pedal or steering wheel under braking points to disc thickness variation, warped discs, or uneven pad deposits on the disc surface. It can also indicate a loose wheel bearing. Urgency: moderate to high depending on severity. Book a brake inspection promptly — the symptom will worsen over time and affects braking consistency.

Longer stopping distance

If the car takes noticeably longer to stop, or the pedal feels spongy, this suggests glazed or heavily worn pads, a partially seized caliper, contaminated brake fluid, or air in the hydraulic system. Urgency: high. Increased stopping distances are a direct safety risk. Do not drive the vehicle until the braking system has been professionally inspected.

Pulling to one side

Brake pull occurs when braking force is uneven between the two front wheels. Common causes include a sticking caliper on one side, a seized slide pin, contaminated pads, or uneven pad wear. The vehicle will veer toward the side with more braking force. Urgency: high. This compromises steering control in an emergency stop. Have both front calipers inspected as soon as possible.

Brake warning light

The red brake warning light can indicate low brake fluid level, a triggered pad wear sensor, the handbrake being partially on, or a fault in the ABS or brake pressure system. Never ignore it. Urgency: high. Check the handbrake is fully released first. If the light remains on, check the fluid reservoir immediately. If fluid is low or the light is accompanied by any other symptom, do not drive — seek professional diagnosis.

Squealing and brake noise explained

Brake squeal is one of the most common reasons drivers seek a brake inspection, yet it has many different causes ranging from completely harmless to genuinely urgent. A brief, light squeak on the first few brake applications of the day — particularly after overnight rain or a period of standing — is normal. A thin layer of surface rust forms on the disc face when the car is stationary. The pads wipe it away within the first stop or two, and the noise disappears. No action is needed.

Persistent squealing that continues throughout a journey points to one of several causes. Pad wear indicators are the most important: a small steel tab is riveted to the pad backing plate at the minimum thickness point. When the pad wears down to that level, the tab contacts the disc and produces a consistent high-pitched squeal. This is a deliberate warning. Pads must be replaced promptly — ignoring the indicator leads to metal-on-metal contact and disc damage within a short distance. Glazed pads or discs occur when the braking surface overheats and hardens, reducing friction and causing a squealing or scraping sound. It is common after prolonged light braking on a long descent. Light glazing can sometimes be cleared by a series of firm, progressive stops from speed; severe glazing requires pad or disc replacement. Poor-quality pads with a hard or abrasive friction compound squeal more readily than OEM or premium alternatives, particularly when cold. Missing shims or anti-squeal clips are another frequent cause — these thin metal or rubber layers sit between the pad backing plate and caliper piston to dampen vibration. If they are omitted during a pad change or have deteriorated, resonance transmits directly through the caliper and produces a high-frequency squeal. Always fit new shims when replacing pads.

Mechanic note:

When diagnosing brake squeal, a mechanic will check pad thickness and condition, disc surface finish, the presence and condition of shims and anti-rattle clips, caliper slide pin freedom, and whether the correct pad specification has been fitted. Squeal that appears only on light pedal pressure — not under firm braking — often points to a resonance issue rather than wear, and may be resolved by applying brake paste to the back of the pad rather than replacing components. Any squeal accompanied by a longer stopping distance, brake pull, or warning light should be treated as urgent regardless of pad thickness.

Brake pulsation and judder

Brake pulsation is a rhythmic pushing back through the brake pedal that you feel every time you apply the brakes, typically becoming more pronounced at higher speeds. Steering wheel judder under braking — where the wheel shakes from side to side during a stop — is closely related and usually points to the same root causes at the front axle. Both symptoms indicate that the disc is not running perfectly true or that its faces are not perfectly parallel. Left untreated, the problem worsens progressively as the uneven contact continues to erode the disc. If the pulsation is severe enough to compromise control during emergency braking, the vehicle should not be driven until repaired.

Disc thickness variation (DTV)

DTV is the most common cause of pulsation. It occurs when the two parallel faces of the disc are no longer exactly the same distance apart all the way around. Even a variation of as little as 0.01–0.02 mm can be felt through the pedal. As the thicker section of the disc passes the pads it pushes them back, creating a pulse. DTV develops gradually through normal wear, or rapidly if pads drag against the disc due to a sticking caliper. A mechanic measures DTV using a dial test indicator (DTI) at multiple points around the disc circumference. Discs with DTV beyond the manufacturer's tolerance must be replaced.

Heat distortion

Repeated aggressive braking — particularly on long descents or when towing — heats the disc unevenly. If the vehicle then sits with the brakes applied while the disc is very hot, localised thermal stresses can cause the disc to distort slightly. Similarly, overtightened or unevenly torqued wheel bolts can pull the disc out of true on the hub. Once distorted, the disc runs with lateral run-out, causing the pads to be pushed back and forth with each revolution. Run-out is measured at the disc outer edge with a DTI; the limit is typically 0.05–0.10 mm depending on the vehicle.

Pad deposits

Under heavy or repeated braking, friction material from the pad can transfer onto the disc surface in an uneven pattern rather than as a smooth, consistent film. These raised deposits create high spots that the opposing pad contacts harder than the surrounding disc surface, generating a pulse and sometimes a groan or shudder at low speed. Deposits are most common when new pads are bedded in incorrectly, when cheap or incompatible pads are fitted, or when a caliper sticks and holds one pad against the disc while the vehicle is parked. In mild cases the deposits can be removed by a series of firm stops; in severe cases the disc must be replaced.

Loose wheel bearing

Wheel bearing play allows the hub — and the disc bolted to it — to shift slightly under braking load. This movement mimics the effect of disc run-out, producing a pulsation that can be felt through both the pedal and the steering wheel. Unlike disc-related causes, bearing play will also often produce a rumbling or growling noise when driving, which changes pitch on corners as load shifts between bearings. A mechanic checks for bearing play by rocking the wheel at the 12 and 6 o'clock positions with the vehicle raised. Any perceptible play is a fail point on the MOT and must be corrected before new discs are fitted — replacing discs on a worn bearing will simply reproduce the problem.

How long do disc brakes last?

Brake component lifespan varies enormously depending on driving style, vehicle weight, axle position and road environment. The figures below are realistic averages for UK driving — treat them as a starting point for maintenance planning, not a guarantee. Always inspect rather than assume.

Brake pads — typical range

Most brake pads last between 25,000 and 60,000 miles under normal driving conditions. Budget pads with harder compounds sit toward the top of that range but often sacrifice feel and fade resistance. Premium OEM-spec or performance pads wear faster but provide better modulation and heat tolerance. Rear pads on cars with front-biased braking systems frequently outlast front pads by a factor of two or more.

Brake discs — typical range

Discs generally last 50,000 to 120,000 miles, though this depends heavily on the minimum thickness limit set by the manufacturer. A disc that has never been allowed to overheat or corrode badly can reach the top of that range; a disc on a vehicle driven aggressively in stop-start traffic may need replacement at the same time as its second set of pads. Discs and pads are always replaced as a matched axle set.

Front vs rear wear

Front brakes do approximately 70–80% of the braking work on most petrol and diesel cars due to forward weight transfer under deceleration. As a result, front pads and discs wear noticeably faster than rears. It is normal for the front axle to need two sets of pads for every one set required at the rear. Always check all four corners at service — uneven wear rates mean rear discs can corrode heavily while remaining within thickness limits.

City and stop-start driving

Frequent low-speed braking in urban traffic is the most aggressive environment for brake pads. Each stop deposits and removes friction material, and the brakes never reach the temperatures needed to fully cure and stabilise the pad surface. City drivers can expect pad life toward the lower end of the range — sometimes as few as 20,000 miles. Disc corrosion is also worse in stop-start environments because the discs spend more time stationary and exposed to moisture.

Motorway driving

Long motorway journeys are the kindest environment for brake components. Braking events are infrequent, the pads and discs stay warm enough to resist moisture, and caliper pistons remain exercised. Drivers who cover predominantly motorway miles often reach 70,000 miles or more on a set of front pads. The trade-off is that infrequently used rear brakes can corrode and seize if the vehicle also sits unused for extended periods between trips.

Towing and heavy loads

Towing increases braking load significantly — a vehicle at its maximum towing capacity may need 40–60% more braking force than when unladen. This dramatically shortens brake component life. Frequent towing, particularly on hilly routes, can halve expected pad and disc lifespan. Vehicles with trailer stability programmes still impose the full deceleration load on the friction brakes. Inspect brakes annually or every 10,000 miles if the vehicle tows regularly.

Heavy vehicles and vans

Vans, SUVs and MPVs loaded near their gross vehicle weight carry more kinetic energy at any given speed, which must be absorbed by the brakes on every stop. Brake pads on a fully loaded large van can wear at twice the rate of the same brakes on an unladen vehicle. Commercial operators should treat 20,000–30,000 miles as a realistic inspection interval and factor brake replacement into vehicle running costs proactively rather than reactively.

Hybrid and EV regenerative braking

Battery-electric and strong-hybrid vehicles recover kinetic energy through the motor during deceleration, applying friction brakes only at higher deceleration rates or when the battery is full. This can extend brake pad life to 80,000–100,000 miles or beyond — significantly longer than an equivalent combustion-engine car. However, the reduced use of friction brakes means discs spend more time stationary and are more prone to surface corrosion and caliper seizure. EV and hybrid owners should have discs inspected for condition, not just thickness, at every service.

Low-mileage cars and disc corrosion

A car with 20,000 miles on the clock can still have badly corroded or deeply grooved discs. Cast iron discs rust rapidly when the vehicle sits unused — even for a few days in damp weather. If the car is driven only occasionally, the pads never fully clean the disc surface, and corrosion builds up in the areas the pad does not contact. Age is as important as mileage when assessing disc condition. A five-year-old car driven 15,000 miles may need discs before a three-year-old car at 40,000 miles.

When lifespan estimates should not be trusted: Any published mileage figure for brake life is a statistical average — it cannot account for your specific vehicle, load, route or braking habits. Do not use mileage estimates to delay an inspection if you have noticed squealing, grinding, brake pull, pedal pulsation or increased stopping distances. Similarly, do not assume brakes are fine simply because a recent service did not flag them — conditions can change rapidly, particularly after towing, a long descent, or a period of standing. The only reliable test of brake condition is a physical inspection by a competent technician.

What a mechanic checks on disc brakes

Pad thickness

A proper brake inspection starts with measuring the remaining friction material on both the inner and outer pads, not just glancing through the wheel spokes at the outer face. On many cars the inner pad wears faster if a caliper piston sticks or the slider pins begin to seize. A mechanic measures pad thickness in millimetres, compares it with the service limit, and checks that wear is even across the axle. Pads close to the minimum limit may still function today, but they should be replaced before they reach the backing plate and destroy the disc.

Disc thickness

Discs are checked with a micrometer or vernier caliper at multiple points around the braking surface, then compared with the minimum thickness cast into the disc hat or listed in manufacturer data. A disc can look usable yet already be below its safe service limit. Mechanics also check for thickness variation from one point to another, because uneven wear causes pedal pulsation even when the disc is technically above minimum thickness. If a disc is close to the limit, fitting new pads to it is false economy.

Disc surface condition

The disc faces are inspected for scoring, heat spots, cracking, heavy corrosion and an outer lip caused by wear. Light surface rust after standing is normal and usually clears quickly, but deep grooves, blue heat marks, pitting or flaking corrosion mean the braking surface is no longer healthy. A mechanic also checks whether the pads are contacting the full swept area. Rusty bands at the inner or outer edge often show the pads are not sitting squarely or the caliper is not sliding freely.

Caliper operation

The caliper must apply and release the pads evenly. A mechanic checks piston movement, dust boot condition, signs of fluid leakage and whether the caliper releases cleanly after the pedal is let off. A sticking piston can overheat one disc, wear one pad down to metal and make the vehicle pull under braking. On floating calipers, the body must centre itself correctly over the disc; if it cannot, one pad does most of the work while the opposite side barely contributes.

Brake fluid condition

Brake fluid is checked for level, age, contamination and moisture content. Because brake fluid is hygroscopic, it absorbs water from the air over time, which lowers its boiling point and increases the risk of fade under repeated heavy braking. Old fluid also promotes internal corrosion in calipers, ABS valves and brake pipes. A professional inspection looks beyond the reservoir level: the condition of the fluid, service history and any evidence of leaks all matter when deciding whether a fluid change is overdue.

Hoses and pipes

Flexible brake hoses are checked for cracks, bulges, twisting, chafing and signs that the inner liner may be collapsing. A hose can fail externally, but it can also fail internally and act like a one-way valve that holds pressure on a caliper. Rigid brake pipes are examined along their length for corrosion, dampness, poor repairs and insecure mounting clips. Surface rust on a pipe can quickly become a safety issue once the protective coating breaks down, especially on older vehicles driven through winter salt.

Slider pins

Slider pins are removed or at least checked for free movement, straightness and correct lubrication where the caliper design allows. These pins let a floating caliper move side to side so both pads clamp evenly. If they seize, one pad remains in hard contact with the disc while the other barely works, causing tapered pad wear, overheating and brake pull. A quick visual check often misses this fault because the caliper can look intact from the outside while the pins are dry or corroded inside their bores.

Wheel bearing and hub run-out

If there is pedal pulsation, uneven disc wear or repeated premature disc replacement, a mechanic checks wheel bearing play and hub run-out. Excessive bearing movement or a corroded hub face prevents the disc from running true, which creates vibration under braking and quickly produces thickness variation in a new disc. This is measured with a dial test indicator rather than guessed by eye. Replacing discs without correcting hub or bearing faults often means the same symptom returns within a few thousand miles.

Road test checks

A road test confirms how the system behaves under real braking loads. The mechanic listens for grinding or squeal, checks whether the car pulls to one side, feels for pedal pulsation, assesses pedal travel and firmness, and confirms the brakes release properly after a stop. They may also check ABS operation on a suitable surface if a fault is suspected. Some brake problems only appear when the discs are hot or when weight transfers forward, so a static workshop inspection alone is not always enough.

Why a professional inspection is better

A visual check through the wheel can only reveal obvious outer-pad wear or severe disc damage. A professional inspection is better because it measures component thickness accurately, checks inner pads and hidden surfaces, tests moving parts for seizure, evaluates hydraulic condition and links what is seen in the workshop with what is felt on the road. That combination of measurement, mechanical testing and road assessment is what catches the faults that most owners miss until braking performance has already deteriorated.

Disc brakes and the MOT

During an MOT, the tester assesses the braking system through a combination of visual inspection, pedal checks, warning-light checks and a brake performance test on calibrated rollers or a decelerometer where appropriate. The test is not a full strip-down inspection, but it is designed to identify wear, damage, leaks, imbalance and reduced braking performance that affect road safety. For used-car buyers, recent brake-related MOT advisories are valuable evidence that discs, pads, hoses or calipers may soon need money spending even if the car still passed.

Brake pad condition

Pads are checked for insecure mounting, contamination and whether friction material is worn dangerously low. An MOT tester will not normally dismantle the brakes to measure every pad precisely, but obvious wear, missing friction material or metal-to-metal contact is a fail. Uneven pad wear can also point to sticking calipers or seized slider pins, so a pass does not necessarily mean the brakes are wearing correctly.

Disc minimum thickness

A brake disc can fail the MOT if it is seriously weakened by wear or clearly below the manufacturer’s minimum thickness. Testers look for discs that are excessively worn, heavily lipped or visibly compromised, but exact thickness measurement depends on what is accessible without dismantling. If a disc is close to its service limit, it may earn an advisory rather than an immediate fail unless its condition is severe enough to affect safety.

Scoring, cracks and corrosion

Scored discs, heat cracks, heavy pitting and corrosion on the swept braking surface are all relevant to the MOT. Light surface rust after standing is not a problem if it clears in use, but deep grooves, cracking or corrosion that reduces the effective contact area can lead to failure. A disc that is rusted badly around the edges may still pass if the swept area is sound, while a badly corroded swept face or crack reaching toward the edge is a clear safety issue.

Braking efficiency and imbalance

The brake tester measures overall stopping effort and compares left-versus-right force on each axle. Poor overall efficiency can mean worn components, contamination, hydraulic faults or rear brakes contributing too little. Excessive imbalance is also important: if one wheel brakes much harder than the other, the car may pull sharply in an emergency stop. Imbalance often points to a sticking caliper, seized slider pins, contamination or a brake drum/disc issue on one side.

Hoses, pipes and calipers

Flexible hoses are checked for cracks, bulges, chafing, leaks and insecure routing. Metal brake pipes are inspected for corrosion, damage and poor repairs. Calipers must be securely mounted and free from leaks or obvious seizure. A damp caliper, heavily corroded pipe or damaged hose can all be MOT failures because hydraulic integrity is fundamental to safe braking.

Warning lights and pedal feel

Relevant brake warning lights — including ABS, ESC and red brake system warnings where applicable — must behave correctly and not indicate a fault. The tester also checks that the brake pedal does not feel excessively spongy or sink abnormally under pressure. A warning light on the dash may turn a marginal-looking brake system into a fail because it suggests the fault is electronic or hydraulic rather than cosmetic.

Advisories vs failures

An MOT advisory means wear or corrosion is present but has not yet crossed the legal fail threshold. A failure means the condition already affects safety, security or braking performance enough that the car is unroadworthy until repaired. For buyers, repeated advisories for discs, pads, pipes or imbalance show a maintenance pattern: even when the car passes, those notes often predict near-future brake costs better than the pass certificate alone.

DIY brake replacement vs using a garage

DIY brake work can be reasonable for competent owners with the correct tools, service data and a safe workspace, especially for straightforward pad or disc replacement on vehicles without corrosion, seized parts or complex electronic systems. The key test is whether you can complete the job to manufacturer standard, including torque settings, bleeding where required and post-repair checks. If you cannot, a professional garage is the safer option.

When DIY may be reasonable

DIY can be appropriate when the job is routine, you have axle stands and proper tools, and you can follow model-specific procedures exactly. You should be comfortable identifying pad wear limits, checking disc condition, cleaning hub faces, and torquing all fasteners correctly. You also need enough time to stop and fix issues properly rather than rushing the car back on the road.

When a garage is the safer choice

Use a garage when caliper pistons are seized, bleed nipples are corroded, brake pipes are weak, or the vehicle has electronic parking brakes that require a scan tool service mode. Garages are also safer for jobs involving brake fluid flushing, ABS-related diagnosis or recurring brake pull/pulsation where hub run-out and bearing checks are needed. If the car is your only transport and must be road-ready the same day, professional support reduces risk.

Safety-critical risks and caliper issues

Brakes are safety-critical: incorrect assembly can cause longer stopping distances, brake pull, wheel lock, overheating or total hydraulic loss. Common DIY mistakes include twisted hoses, contaminated pads, missing anti-rattle clips, and caliper carrier bolts not tightened to spec. A sticky caliper or seized slider pin can wear one pad to metal quickly and overheat the disc, so caliper movement and release must always be checked before road use.

Brake bleeding and fluid control

Any time the hydraulic system is opened, correct bleeding is essential to remove air and restore firm pedal feel. Incomplete bleeding leaves a long or spongy pedal and can severely reduce braking efficiency. Correct brake fluid grade, clean equipment and the right bleed sequence matter, and some systems require scan-tool activation of ABS valves for a full bleed. Brake fluid also damages paint, so spill control and clean-up are part of safe workmanship.

Electronic parking brakes and torque settings

Many modern rear calipers must be retracted electronically; forcing them back mechanically can damage the motor or screw mechanism and create expensive faults. Wheel bolts, caliper bolts and carrier bolts must be tightened to manufacturer torque values, not guessed by feel. Under-torque risks components loosening; over-torque risks stripped threads, warped discs and future seizure. Correct torque tools and procedures are non-negotiable on brake work.

Bedding-in and MOT/safety consequences

After fitting new pads or discs, bedding-in is required so friction surfaces mate evenly: repeated moderate stops with cooling periods, avoiding heavy emergency-style braking at first unless required for safety. Poor bedding-in can cause noise, uneven deposits and pulsation. Installation errors or poor bedding can also lead to MOT problems such as imbalance, low efficiency, leaks or warning lights — and in severe cases can produce genuinely dangerous braking on the road.
