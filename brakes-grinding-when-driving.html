Quick answer

Why Are My Brakes Grinding When Driving?

The most important possibility is brake pads worn far enough for metal to contact the brake disc. Once friction material has worn away, the steel pad backing plate can grind directly against the rotating disc, damaging the disc rapidly and potentially reducing braking performance.

Grinding does not always mean the pads are worn out. A seized caliper can keep a pad dragging against the disc, while a trapped stone, bent brake shield, heavy corrosion or loose brake hardware can create similar metallic noises. Some wheel bearing and hub faults can also be mistaken for brake grinding.

Most Serious Common Cause

Brake pads worn through their friction material can allow the steel backing plate to grind against the disc.

If It Grinds Without Braking

Check for a seized caliper, trapped debris, bent brake shield, loose hardware or another wheel-area fault.

When It Becomes Urgent

Grinding with poor braking, pulling, vibration, smoke, burning smell or excessive wheel heat needs prompt inspection.

Brake safety comes before diagnosis

If braking performance feels weak or unpredictable, the pedal becomes soft or unusually low, the vehicle pulls sharply, or smoke or a strong burning smell comes from a wheel, stop driving as soon as it is safe and arrange professional inspection or recovery.

Mechanic insight:

First establish whether the grinding happens only when the brake pedal is applied or continues while the vehicle is moving without braking. That single observation immediately changes which faults should be checked first.

Brake Noise Basics

How Disc Brakes Create a Grinding Noise

Disc brakes work by pressing friction material against a rotating brake disc. Under normal conditions the pads should contact the disc smoothly and quietly enough that only minor brake noise is heard.

Grinding develops when something harder, rougher or incorrectly positioned contacts the rotating disc or another wheel-end component. The sound can therefore come from worn friction material, metal contact, trapped debris, corrosion or a part being held against the disc when it should have released.

1. Disc Rotates

The brake disc turns with the wheel while the vehicle is moving.

2. Pads Clamp

When the brake pedal is pressed, the caliper pushes the pads against the disc.

3. Friction Slows the Wheel

The pad material creates controlled friction that converts vehicle movement into heat.

4. Abnormal Contact Creates Grinding

Metal contact, debris, corrosion or dragging components can produce a harsh scraping or grinding noise.

Grinding usually means abnormal contact

A normal brake system should not produce a harsh metallic grind. When that sound appears, the first job is to identify what is contacting the rotating disc or wheel assembly.

Mechanic Diagnosis

How Mechanics Diagnose Brake Grinding

A mechanic should not assume every grinding noise means worn brake pads. The diagnosis starts by matching the noise with the conditions in which it appears, then inspecting the relevant wheel-end components.

1. Confirm When It Happens

Establish whether the noise appears only during braking or continues while the car is rolling.

2. Identify Which Corner

Listen for whether the noise is strongest from the front, rear, left or right side.

3. Check Brake Behaviour

Note any pulling, vibration, reduced braking performance, pedal change or warning lights.

4. Inspect for Heat

Excess heat at one wheel can support a diagnosis of a dragging or seized brake.

5. Remove the Wheel

Inspect pads, discs, caliper, slider hardware, brake shield and nearby components.

6. Check for Debris

Look for trapped stones or foreign material around the disc and shield.

7. Compare With Bearing Noise

If the noise is speed-related rather than pedal-related, check wheel-bearing and hub condition.

8. Confirm the Root Cause

Repair the actual fault rather than simply replacing pads without finding out why the brake was grinding.

The noise pattern is the first diagnostic filter

Grinding only under braking points the diagnosis in a different direction from grinding that continues with the pedal released. That distinction should be established before parts are removed.

Noise Pattern

Grinding Only When Braking

Grinding that appears mainly or only when the brake pedal is pressed usually means the fault becomes active when the pads clamp the disc.

Worn Brake Pads

Pad friction material may be worn away enough for the steel backing plate to contact the disc.

Scored Brake Discs

Deep grooves, rough surfaces or severe corrosion can create harsh noise as the pads apply.

Damaged Pad Material

Cracked, broken or contaminated friction material can make abnormal noise under load.

Loose Brake Hardware

Clips, shims or retaining parts can shift when braking force is applied.

Severe Disc Corrosion

Heavy pitting or rust can make braking rough and noisy.

Uneven Caliper Operation

A sticking piston or slider can load one pad unevenly and accelerate wear.

Grinding under braking deserves prompt inspection

If the noise is caused by worn pads contacting the disc, continued driving can quickly turn a pad replacement into a pads-and-discs repair.

Noise Pattern

Grinding While Driving Without Braking

If the noise continues while the vehicle is rolling with the brake pedal released, the diagnosis broadens beyond simple pad wear.

Seized Caliper

A piston or slider may fail to release fully and keep the pad dragging against the disc.

Trapped Stone

Debris can become wedged between the rotating disc and the brake shield.

Bent Dust Shield

The metal shield behind the disc may touch the rotating surface continuously.

Loose Hardware

Brake components or nearby fittings may be contacting the disc or wheel.

Severe Rust

Corrosion can create rough rotating contact even before the pedal is pressed.

Wheel Bearing or Hub

Some bearing and hub noises are mistaken for brake grinding because they increase with road speed.

Brake Pad Wear

Worn Brake Pads and Metal-to-Metal Grinding

Brake pads contain friction material bonded to a steel backing plate. As the pad wears, the friction material becomes thinner. If replacement is delayed long enough, the steel backing plate can eventually contact the brake disc.

That metal-to-metal contact produces one of the most serious common grinding noises because it can damage the disc quickly.

Thin Friction Material

The pad has reached or passed its intended replacement point.

Backing Plate Contact

Once friction material is gone, steel can grind directly against the disc.

Urgent Repair

Disc Scoring

Metal contact can cut deep grooves into the disc face.

Reduced Braking Quality

Braking can become noisy, uneven and less predictable as the friction surfaces deteriorate.

Repair Cost Increases

A pads-only job can become pads and discs if driving continues after grinding begins.

Damaged Brake Discs and Grinding Noise

Brake discs can become scored, heavily corroded, pitted, overheated or worn below their intended service condition. Rough disc surfaces can create grinding, scraping and vibration when the pads apply.

Deep Scoring

Grooves can form after prolonged metal-to-metal pad contact.

Heavy Corrosion

Rust and pitting can reduce the quality of the friction surface.

Heat Damage

Severe overheating can change the disc surface and braking behaviour.

Uneven Wear

A seized caliper or poor pad movement can wear the disc unevenly.

Noise + Vibration

Rough disc condition may also contribute to brake judder or vibration.

Caliper Fault

Seized Brake Caliper Causing Grinding

A brake caliper must apply the pads when braking and then allow them to release sufficiently when the pedal is released. If a piston or slider seizes, the pad can remain in contact with the disc.

Constant Drag

The brake may continue rubbing while the car is moving.

Uneven Pad Wear

One pad can wear much faster than the other.

Hot Wheel

Continuous friction can make one wheel area much hotter than the others.

Disc Damage

A dragging brake can overheat and wear the disc rapidly.

Smoke or severe wheel heat means stop

If a wheel is smoking, producing a strong burning smell or becoming abnormally hot, do not continue driving normally. A dragging brake can become a serious safety fault.

Debris Contact

Trapped Stone or Debris Between the Disc and Shield

A small stone can become trapped around the brake disc, particularly between the disc and the thin metal shield behind it. Because the disc rotates continuously, even a small piece of debris can create a surprisingly loud scraping or grinding sound.

Sudden Onset

The noise may begin suddenly after driving over loose road material.

Noise While Coasting

Scraping may continue even with the brake pedal released.

Speed Related

The sound can change frequency as wheel speed changes.

Braking May Not Change It Much

Unlike pad wear, the noise can remain similar whether or not the brake is being applied.

Often Localised to One Wheel

The sound is usually strongest from the affected corner.

Inspect Before Replacing Parts

Confirm debris contact before assuming pads or discs need replacement.

Shield Contact

Bent Brake Dust Shield Scraping the Disc

The thin metal shield behind the brake disc sits close to the rotating surface. If it is bent during wheel, brake or suspension work, or damaged by road debris, it can touch the disc and create continuous metallic scraping.

Constant Light Scrape

Often heard continuously while the wheel rotates.

Changes With Steering

Contact may change slightly as suspension and steering geometry move.

After Recent Work

Especially worth checking after brake, hub, wheel or suspension repairs.

Disc May Look Healthy

Brake pads and disc friction surfaces can be serviceable even though the noise is metallic.

Visual Contact Mark

A shiny rubbed area on the shield or disc edge can confirm contact.

Usually Lower-Cost Repair

If no other brake damage is present, shield correction may be straightforward.

Brake Corrosion

Surface Rust vs Serious Brake Disc Corrosion

Brake discs are exposed bare metal and can develop surface rust when a vehicle sits in damp conditions. A thin film may produce noise during the first few brake applications and then clear.

Heavy pitting, deep rust, flaking or large areas of poor contact are different and may require brake repair rather than simply normal use.

Lower Concern

Light Surface Rust

  • ✓ Appears after rain or parking.
  • ✓ Noise is strongest at first use.
  • ✓ Noise quickly reduces with normal braking.
  • ✓ Braking remains smooth and normal.
Higher Concern

Heavy Corrosion

  • ! Grinding continues after several brake applications.
  • ! Disc faces are deeply pitted or heavily scored.
  • ! Braking feels rough or uneven.
  • ! Pad contact is poor across the disc surface.
Brake Hardware

Loose or Damaged Brake Hardware

Pads, calipers and carriers rely on clips, shims, pins, bolts and retaining hardware to stay correctly positioned. If anything becomes loose, damaged or incorrectly fitted, it can cause scraping, rattling or grinding.

Pad Clips

Incorrectly fitted or damaged clips can allow abnormal pad movement.

Anti-Rattle Shims

Missing or damaged shims can contribute to unwanted brake noise.

Slider Pins

Sticking or damaged sliders can create uneven pad loading.

Carrier Bolts

Brake carrier and caliper fasteners must be secure and correctly tightened.

Incorrect Pad Fitment

Pads that do not sit correctly in the carrier can bind, move or wear unevenly.

Recent Brake Work

New grinding immediately after brake work should prompt a fitting and security check.

Professional Diagnosis

Professional Diagnostic Workflow for Grinding Brakes

A proper brake-grinding diagnosis should identify exactly which wheel is affected, when the noise occurs, whether braking performance has changed and what physical contact is producing the sound.

Mechanics should not simply fit new pads because a customer reports grinding. The inspection needs to establish whether the root cause is worn friction material, disc damage, seized caliper movement, trapped debris, shield contact, corrosion or another wheel-end fault.

1. Confirm the Complaint

Establish whether the grinding occurs under braking, continuously while driving or only at certain speeds.

2. Road-Test the Vehicle

Reproduce the symptom safely and note braking response, pulling, vibration and noise location.

3. Compare Wheel Temperatures

Excess heat at one corner can indicate a dragging or seized brake.

4. Lift the Vehicle

Check wheel rotation, obvious drag, roughness and abnormal mechanical contact.

5. Remove the Wheel

Inspect the complete brake assembly rather than relying on what can be seen through the wheel.

6. Inspect Pads and Discs

Check remaining friction material, disc condition and uneven wear patterns.

7. Inspect Caliper Operation

Check piston movement, sliders, pad fitment and evidence of brake drag.

8. Confirm the Repair

Correct the root cause and verify that braking is smooth, balanced and free from abnormal noise afterwards.

Diagnose the complete corner, not just the noisy part

A badly worn pad may be the visible failure, but a seized slider or piston may be the reason it wore out. Replacing the pad without fixing the underlying fault can cause the same problem to return.

Road-Test Procedure

How Mechanics Road-Test a Grinding Brake Noise

The road test should be short, controlled and only carried out if braking remains safe enough to do so. Severe grinding, smoke, extreme wheel heat or poor braking performance may make a road test inappropriate.

Listen While Coasting

Check whether the grinding continues with the brake pedal completely released.

Apply Light Braking

Note whether the sound immediately becomes louder, quieter or changes character.

Apply Moderate Braking

Assess pedal feel, stopping response, vibration and vehicle stability.

Check for Pulling

A vehicle pulling left or right may indicate uneven braking force or a dragging brake.

Compare Road Speed

A noise that changes strongly with speed but little with braking can point away from the friction surfaces.

Check Cornering Load

A hum or rough grinding that changes through gentle corners can increase suspicion of a wheel bearing.

Workshop Inspection

Wheel-Off Brake Inspection

Removing the wheel gives the technician access to the pad, disc, caliper, carrier, shield and hub area. This is usually necessary before the cause of persistent grinding can be confirmed properly.

Inner Pad

The inner pad can wear very differently from the outer pad, especially when a piston or slider is sticking.

Outer Pad

Compare outer-pad thickness with the inner pad to identify uneven caliper operation.

Disc Faces

Inspect both faces where possible, because the inner disc surface can be worse than the visible outer face.

Caliper & Carrier

Check for secure mounting, free movement and evidence of abnormal contact.

Brake Shield

Look for shiny rub marks or areas bent towards the disc.

Debris

Check for trapped stones, corrosion flakes or foreign material around the rotating assembly.

Never judge pad condition from the outer pad alone

A seized piston or slider can wear the inner pad to metal while the outer pad still appears to have usable friction material.

Brake Pad Inspection

What Mechanics Check on the Brake Pads

Brake pad inspection is about more than remaining thickness. The technician should assess wear pattern, friction material, backing plates, contamination and whether both pads are moving correctly.

Remaining Friction Material

Check whether the pad is approaching or has passed its safe service limit.

Metal-to-Metal Contact

Exposed backing plate contacting the disc confirms an urgent friction-material failure.

Immediate Repair

Inner vs Outer Wear

Uneven thickness can indicate piston, slider or pad-binding problems.

Tapered Wear

One end of the pad wearing faster can indicate poor pad or caliper movement.

Cracked / Broken Material

Damaged friction material can cause noise and unreliable pad contact.

Oil or Grease Contamination

Contaminated pads may require replacement once the source of contamination is repaired.

Brake Disc Inspection

What Mechanics Check on the Brake Discs

The disc should provide a smooth, structurally sound friction surface and remain within the manufacturer's service limits. Grinding often leaves visible evidence on the disc.

Deep Scoring

Deep circular grooves can indicate prolonged metal-to-metal contact.

Minimum Thickness

Disc thickness should be measured and compared with the manufacturer's minimum specification.

Corrosion & Pitting

Heavy pitting can reduce effective pad contact and cause noise and rough braking.

Heat Marks

Blueing or abnormal heat patterns can indicate severe overheating or brake drag.

Cracks or Damage

Structural disc damage requires immediate attention.

Safety Critical

Inner Face Condition

The rear face can sometimes corrode or wear more severely than the visible outer face.

New pads do not repair a damaged disc

Fitting new pads against a deeply scored or badly corroded disc can result in poor bedding, noise and reduced friction contact. Disc condition should be assessed before deciding the repair scope.

Caliper Diagnosis

Caliper, Slider and Piston Checks

A healthy caliper must apply both pads correctly and release them when braking effort is removed. Caliper faults are a major reason one pad can wear much faster than the other.

Slider Movement

Floating-caliper sliders should move freely without stiffness, corrosion or excessive play.

Piston Operation

Check whether the piston applies and retracts correctly rather than holding pressure against the pad.

Piston Boot

Torn or damaged protective boots can allow contamination and corrosion into the piston area.

Pad Movement in Carrier

Pads should move correctly without binding because of rust, dirt or damaged hardware.

Uneven Pad Wear

One pad substantially thinner than its partner often gives useful evidence of a movement fault.

Brake Hose Influence

In some cases hydraulic pressure may fail to release correctly because of a brake hose or hydraulic fault rather than the caliper itself.

A seized brake must be repaired before new friction parts are relied on

New pads and discs can be damaged rapidly if a seized piston, slider or hydraulic fault continues holding the brake on.

Heat Diagnosis

What Brake Temperature Can Tell a Mechanic

Brakes naturally become warm during use, but one wheel being substantially hotter than the corresponding wheel on the same axle can support a diagnosis of brake drag.

Similar Temperatures

Similar wheel-end heat after normal braking makes severe single-corner drag less likely.

One Wheel Much Hotter

Investigate a seized caliper, slider, piston or hydraulic pressure not releasing correctly.

Dragging Brake Likely

Burning Smell

Strong odour from one wheel supports significant friction overheating.

Smoke

Smoke from a wheel area is an urgent indication of severe brake overheating or another serious fault.

Stop Driving

Heat After Short Journey

Excessive temperature after little braking increases suspicion of constant brake drag.

Compare Across the Axle

Comparing corresponding corners provides more useful evidence than judging one wheel in isolation.

Do not touch brake discs or calipers to check temperature

Brake components can become hot enough to cause serious burns. Temperature assessment should be carried out safely using appropriate workshop methods.

Because drivers may describe bearing roughness as grinding, the workshop should separate brake contact from hub and bearing noise before replacing parts.

Driving Safety

Is It Safe to Drive With Grinding Brakes?

Grinding brakes should be treated as a warning that something in the brake or wheel-end assembly needs inspection. The driving risk depends on whether braking performance remains normal, whether the grinding is caused by friction-part wear or simple contact such as a shield, and whether heat, pulling, smoke or pedal problems are present.

If the noise is caused by brake pads worn through to their steel backing plates, continued driving can damage the discs quickly and may reduce braking performance. A dragging caliper can also overheat the brake and create a more serious fault.

Lower Concern

Light Temporary Rust Noise

A brief scraping sound after the vehicle has been parked that disappears quickly and leaves braking completely normal may be lower risk.

Grinding + Safety Symptoms

Poor braking, a soft or low pedal, severe pulling, smoke, burning smell or extreme wheel heat can indicate a serious brake fault.

Driving Risk: Critical

A Short Journey May Be Lower Risk When...

  • ✓ Braking performance still feels completely normal.
  • ✓ The pedal remains firm and predictable.
  • ✓ There is no severe pulling under braking.
  • ✓ There is no smoke or burning smell.
  • ✓ No wheel appears abnormally hot.
  • ✓ The vehicle is going directly for inspection.

Stop and Arrange Recovery When...

  • ! The brake pedal feels soft, low or unpredictable.
  • ! The car pulls sharply when braking.
  • ! Stopping performance has noticeably reduced.
  • ! Smoke or a strong burning smell comes from a wheel.
  • ! One wheel is severely overheated.
  • ! The grinding is extremely loud or suddenly becomes worse.
Brake faults can become worse quickly

Do not keep driving simply because the vehicle still stops. A worn pad, seized caliper or damaged disc can deteriorate further and increase both the safety risk and the repair cost.

Failure Progression

What Happens If Grinding Brakes Are Ignored?

The consequences depend on what is creating the grinding. A trapped stone may cause little permanent damage if removed quickly, while metal-to-metal pad wear or a seized caliper can damage multiple brake components.

1. Friction Material Wears Out

Brake pad material becomes thinner until the steel backing plate approaches the disc.

2. Metal Contacts the Disc

Hard metal contact creates harsh grinding and rapidly marks the disc surface.

3. Heat and Damage Increase

The disc can become deeply scored or overheated and a dragging brake may create excessive wheel temperature.

4. Repair Cost Rises

What may have started as a pad replacement can become a pads-and-discs repair or require caliper work.

Disc Damage

Deep scoring or heat damage can mean the discs need replacement.

Severe Pad Wear

Friction material can disappear completely, leaving the backing plate against the disc.

Caliper Overheating

A dragging brake can overheat the pad, disc and surrounding components.

Uneven Braking

One brake working differently from the other side can affect vehicle stability under braking.

Higher Repair Bill

Delaying repairs can turn a relatively straightforward wear repair into a larger brake overhaul.

MOT Failure Risk

Excessively worn pads, severely worn discs, unsafe caliper faults or poor braking performance can affect the MOT result.

Early inspection usually protects the discs

Brake pads are designed to be replaced before the steel backing plate reaches the disc. Catching wear early can avoid unnecessary disc damage.

MOT Guidance

Can Grinding Brakes Fail an MOT?

Yes, if the condition causing the grinding meets an MOT defect threshold. The MOT does not fail a vehicle simply because a driver describes the brakes as “grinding”; the tester assesses the actual brake condition, operation and performance.

Under the current DVSA car MOT inspection manual, brake pads worn down to a wear indicator are a Major defect, while pads worn below 1.5 mm are classed as Dangerous. Significantly and obviously worn brake discs are a Major defect, while discs that are insecure, fractured or otherwise likely to fail are Dangerous.

Worn Brake Pads

Excessively worn brake pads can directly affect the MOT result.

Significantly and obviously worn discs can be recorded as a Major defect.

Unsafe Disc Damage

A fractured, insecure or otherwise failure-prone disc can be classed as Dangerous.

Contaminated Friction Parts

Oil or grease contamination affecting brake pads or discs can also result in an MOT defect.

Caliper Faults

Caliper condition and operation form part of the braking system inspection.

Brake Performance

The braking system must also meet the required performance and efficiency standards during the test.

Do not wait for the MOT to diagnose grinding brakes

A grinding brake should be inspected when the symptom starts. The MOT is a minimum roadworthiness inspection, not a substitute for diagnosis of a developing brake fault.

Prevention

How to Prevent Brake Grinding

Many serious grinding faults begin as ordinary brake wear that is left too long. Regular inspection and early attention to changes in brake noise can prevent larger repairs.

Inspect Pads Regularly

Replace pads before friction material wears through to the backing plate.

Check Both Pads

Inner and outer pads can wear at different rates.

Investigate Squealing Early

A developing brake noise may provide warning before grinding starts.

Service Caliper Sliders

Correct servicing helps prevent pad binding and uneven wear.

Check Disc Condition

Corrosion, scoring and wear should be assessed during brake servicing.

Do Not Ignore Brake Pull

Pulling can be an early clue that braking force is uneven.

Investigate Hot Wheels

Unusual heat can indicate a brake that is not releasing properly.

Check After Brake Work

New scraping or grinding immediately after repair should be inspected rather than assumed to be normal bedding-in.

Do Not Delay Advisories

Brake wear advisories can become expensive or unsafe if ignored until the next MOT.

Frequently Asked Questions

Brakes Grinding When Driving FAQs

These questions cover grinding under braking, continuous grinding, pad and disc wear, seized calipers, debris, wheel bearings, MOT implications, repair costs and used-car buying.

Why are my brakes grinding when driving?

Brakes can grind because of badly worn brake pads, damaged discs, trapped stones, a bent brake shield, seized caliper, heavy rust or loose brake hardware.

Is it safe to drive with grinding brakes?

It is not sensible to keep driving normally with grinding brakes. If the noise is loud, constant, comes with poor pedal feel, pulling, vibration, smoke or burning smell, stop safely and arrange inspection or recovery.

Do grinding brakes mean I need new discs?

Often yes if the pads have worn down to metal or the disc surface is scored, damaged, corroded or below safe condition. A garage should inspect the pads and discs before quoting.

Can grinding brakes fail an MOT?

Yes. Grinding brakes can fail an MOT if the brakes are excessively worn, damaged, unsafe, contaminated, incorrectly fitted or braking performance is affected.

Why do my brakes grind only when I press the pedal?

Grinding that appears mainly when the brake pedal is pressed commonly points to worn brake pads, scored discs, damaged friction material, contamination or another fault that becomes loaded when the pads clamp the disc.

Why do my brakes grind even when I am not braking?

Grinding while driving without pressing the brake pedal can be caused by a seized caliper, trapped stone, bent brake shield, loose brake hardware, severe corrosion or a wheel bearing or hub fault being mistaken for brake noise.

Can worn brake pads make a grinding noise?

Yes. If brake friction material wears away far enough, the steel backing plate can contact the brake disc and produce a harsh metallic grinding noise.

Can a seized brake caliper cause grinding?

Yes. A seized caliper piston or slider can keep a brake pad pressed against the disc, causing dragging, rapid wear, excessive heat, burning smells and grinding.

Can a stone trapped in the brakes cause grinding?

Yes. A small stone or road debris can become trapped between the disc and brake shield or around the brake assembly and create a sudden scraping or grinding noise.

Can a bent brake dust shield cause grinding?

Yes. A bent brake dust shield can touch the rotating disc and create a metallic scraping or grinding sound that may continue even when the brake pedal is not pressed.

Can rust cause brakes to grind?

Yes. Light surface rust after parking can cause temporary brake noise that may clear with normal braking, but heavy corrosion, pitting or persistent grinding needs inspection.

How can I tell brake grinding from wheel bearing noise?

Brake grinding often changes when the brake pedal is applied, while wheel bearing noise more commonly changes with road speed and cornering load. A workshop inspection is the safest way to confirm the source.

Can grinding brakes make the wheel hot?

Yes. A dragging pad or seized caliper can generate excessive heat at one wheel. Burning smell, smoke or an unusually hot wheel area should be treated as an urgent brake fault.

Can grinding brakes cause the car to pull to one side?

Yes. Uneven brake wear, a seized caliper or a brake that is dragging or not applying correctly can make the vehicle pull to one side, particularly during braking.

Can I just replace the brake pads if the brakes are grinding?

Not always. If grinding has scored, overheated or excessively worn the discs, the discs may also need replacement. The caliper and brake hardware should be checked for the reason the pads wore out.

How much does it cost to repair grinding brakes in the UK?

Repair cost depends on the cause. Pads may be the lower-cost repair, while pads and discs, seized calipers or additional brake damage can make the bill substantially higher.

Should I buy a used car with grinding brakes?

Treat grinding brakes as an unresolved safety and repair-cost risk. Have the braking system inspected and price pads, discs, calipers and any related faults before agreeing to buy the vehicle.

What is the best first check when brakes start grinding?

First establish whether the grinding happens only during braking or continuously while driving, then check for pulling, vibration, warning lights, burning smell, smoke or unusual wheel heat and arrange a proper brake inspection.
