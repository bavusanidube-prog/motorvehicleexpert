Corroded Brake Pipe Advisory Explained

A corroded brake pipe advisory means deterioration has been identified on part of the vehicle's rigid hydraulic brake pipework but the defect recorded at that MOT was not sufficient to cause failure. Because brake pipes carry hydraulic pressure every time you press the brake pedal, corrosion should be inspected rather than simply ignored until the next MOT.

Quick answer

Is a corroded brake pipe MOT advisory serious?

Yes. A brake pipe corrosion advisory should be treated as a safety-related warning rather than a cosmetic note. Rigid brake pipes carry pressurised brake fluid between the master cylinder, hydraulic control components and the wheel brakes. If corrosion progresses far enough to weaken or perforate the pipe, hydraulic pressure can be lost.

What does the advisory tell you?

Deterioration has been identified and recorded, but the condition assessed during that MOT did not meet the rejection threshold applied to the vehicle at that time.

When does it become an MOT failure?

Excessive corrosion or damage to a rigid brake pipe is a Major defect. A pipe at imminent risk of failure, or a leaking hydraulic brake pipe or connection, is categorised as Dangerous.

What should you do next?

Have the affected pipework inspected before corrosion progresses. The correct decision may be monitoring, corrosion protection or replacement depending on the remaining metal condition, location, access and evidence of pitting or leakage.

DVSA inspection point

Current MOT guidance states that chafing, corrosion or damage reducing a typical rigid hydraulic brake pipe's wall thickness by around one-third justifies rejection. Because that measurement is difficult to judge precisely during an MOT, testers are instructed to give the benefit of the doubt where they are uncertain.

Mechanic insight

Do not judge brake-pipe condition from the colour of the rust alone. Light oxidation across a broad area can look worse than a small deeply pitted section hidden behind a retaining clip. The important questions are how much sound metal remains, whether the protective coating has failed, whether the pipe is securely supported and whether there is any sign of leakage or imminent failure.

Advisory meaning

What Does a Corroded Brake Pipe Advisory Mean?

A corroded brake pipe advisory means deterioration has been identified on part of the vehicle's rigid brake pipework, but the condition recorded during that MOT did not reach the rejection threshold applied at the time of inspection. The important word is corrosion: the metal pipe has started deteriorating and its future condition should not be assumed simply because the vehicle passed its test.

Rigid brake pipes form part of the hydraulic braking system. They carry pressurised brake fluid from the master cylinder and hydraulic control system towards the brakes at each wheel. Their condition therefore matters far more than the appearance of ordinary cosmetic underbody rust.

Advisory recorded

Deterioration has been noticed and recorded for the driver's attention, but the tester did not classify the pipe as excessively corroded or otherwise defective enough to fail at that inspection.

Not a clean bill of health

An MOT advisory should not be interpreted as confirmation that the pipe will remain acceptable until the next annual test. Corrosion can continue developing after the MOT.

Inspection is the next step

The sensible next step is to identify exactly which pipe has been mentioned, inspect the complete accessible run and establish whether the deterioration is superficial or progressing into significant metal loss.

Advisory does not automatically mean replacement

Do not assume that every rusty-looking brake pipe needs immediate replacement. Condition matters more than appearance alone. Light deterioration may require monitoring, while deep pitting, excessive corrosion, damage or evidence that the pipe is becoming structurally weak changes the repair decision considerably.

Do not ignore repeated brake pipe advisories

If similar brake pipe corrosion appears repeatedly in the vehicle's MOT history, arrange a fresh inspection rather than assuming the condition has remained unchanged. A previous pass tells you how the pipe was assessed at that test; it does not tell you how much it has deteriorated since.

Hydraulic braking

What Do Brake Pipes Actually Do?

Brake pipes are rigid hydraulic lines that carry brake fluid through much of the vehicle's braking system. When the driver presses the brake pedal, the master cylinder generates hydraulic pressure. That pressure must travel reliably through the brake pipework and flexible hoses before it can operate the wheel brakes.

Unlike a body panel or cosmetic trim component, a brake pipe has to contain hydraulic pressure repeatedly throughout its service life. Its ability to remain sealed, securely supported and mechanically sound is therefore critical to braking safety.

1. Pedal applied

Pressing the brake pedal operates the master cylinder and begins creating hydraulic pressure in the braking circuit.

2. Pressure travels

Brake fluid carries that pressure through the rigid pipework routed around the vehicle.

3. Flexible hose moves

Near the wheels, flexible brake hoses allow for steering and suspension movement while maintaining the hydraulic connection.

4. Brakes apply

Hydraulic pressure reaches the wheel brake, allowing a caliper or wheel cylinder to apply braking force.

Rigid brake pipes

These fixed lines run through the engine bay and along the body or underside of the vehicle. Their route varies considerably between manufacturers and models.

Flexible brake hoses

Flexible hoses connect fixed hydraulic pipework to components that move with the steering or suspension. Hose deterioration is assessed separately from rigid brake-pipe corrosion.

Brake fluid

Brake fluid transfers hydraulic pressure through the system. Fluid escaping from a damaged rigid pipe is therefore not merely an underbody leak; it can affect the operation of the braking circuit.

Mechanic insight

A vehicle can contain several metres of rigid brake pipe, and the condition may not be identical along the whole run. A section in the engine bay can remain relatively clean while pipework exposed underneath the vehicle deteriorates much more heavily. That is why the location written on an MOT or identified during inspection is useful when planning the repair.

Corrosion causes

Why Do Brake Pipes Corrode?

Brake pipe corrosion develops when exposed metal reacts with moisture and oxygen. Protective coatings help slow this process, but years of road spray, winter contamination, dirt accumulation and physical damage can eventually expose the underlying pipe and allow corrosion to develop.

The rate of deterioration is not determined by vehicle age alone. Pipe material, protective coating, routing, local environment, maintenance history and how the vehicle has been used all influence the condition found underneath the car.

Water and road spray

Pipework routed underneath the vehicle is repeatedly exposed to rainwater, spray and wet road contamination. Areas that remain damp for longer can deteriorate faster than exposed sections that dry readily.

Winter road salt

Salt and de-icing contamination can accelerate corrosion when it remains on metal components. Cars regularly driven through UK winters may therefore show greater underbody deterioration than their mileage alone suggests.

Damaged protective coating

Stone impacts, abrasion, previous work or age-related coating deterioration can expose the metal underneath. Corrosion may then start locally rather than evenly across the complete pipe.

Dirt around retaining clips

Clips and mounting points can collect dirt and moisture around the pipe. These areas deserve careful inspection because deterioration may be more advanced than on the immediately visible pipework.

Long periods standing

Low mileage does not guarantee corrosion-free brake pipes. A car stored outdoors or standing for long periods in damp conditions can still develop significant underbody corrosion.

Age and previous repairs

Older pipework may have accumulated years of coating damage and environmental exposure. Previous replacement sections should also be inspected for sound routing, support and suitable connections.

Mileage can be misleading

A high-mileage motorway vehicle may have relatively sound pipework, while a lower-mileage vehicle that has spent years outside in damp or coastal conditions may be much more corroded. Physical inspection is more useful than mileage when assessing brake-pipe condition.

Inspection areas

Where Does Brake Pipe Corrosion Commonly Develop?

Brake pipe routing differs between vehicles, but deterioration is commonly found where rigid pipework is exposed to road contamination or where moisture and dirt can remain trapped. Some sections are easy to see on a ramp, while others disappear behind shields, tanks, subframes or other components.

Retaining clips

Dirt and moisture can accumulate around clips. The section hidden directly beneath a clip may deteriorate differently from the visible pipe on either side.

Underbody pipe runs

Long pipe sections beneath the vehicle can receive years of water, salt, grit and road contamination, making them important inspection areas.

Pipe unions

Connections and unions should be inspected for corrosion, deterioration and evidence of fluid leakage. Severe corrosion can also make later disassembly more difficult.

Rear brake pipework

Pipework routed towards the rear brakes can be heavily exposed to road spray and may pass through areas where dirt accumulates around suspension or body components.

Above covers or shields

Some sections run close to underbody covers, heat shields or other components. Accessibility can make both inspection and eventual replacement more labour-intensive.

Near bends and transitions

Changes in pipe direction and areas where the line passes between exposed and protected locations deserve attention, particularly where coating damage or trapped contamination is present.

Visible corrosion may not be the whole problem

Do not assume that the most visible rusty section is automatically the worst section. Clips, mounting points and partially obscured pipework can hide localised deterioration. A proper assessment should follow the accessible pipe run rather than examining only the area mentioned casually at the MOT.

Used car inspection tip

When an MOT history contains a brake pipe corrosion advisory, ask which section was affected and whether it was subsequently replaced. An invoice identifying the pipe or hydraulic line repaired is much stronger evidence than a seller simply saying that the previous advisory was "sorted".

Corrosion severity

Surface Rust vs Serious Brake Pipe Corrosion

Not all brake pipe corrosion represents the same level of deterioration. A pipe may show light surface oxidation while retaining sound metal underneath, or it may contain concentrated pitting and material loss that has significantly weakened the pipe wall. The difference cannot reliably be judged from rust colour alone.

The important factors are the depth and concentration of corrosion, the condition of the remaining pipe wall, whether the deterioration extends beneath retaining clips or coatings, whether the pipe remains securely supported and whether there is any evidence of hydraulic fluid leakage.

Light surface deterioration

Monitor

Light oxidation or early deterioration may be present without substantial loss of pipe-wall strength. The area still deserves inspection because corrosion can continue once the protective surface has deteriorated.

Localised pitting

Inspect carefully

Small concentrated areas of corrosion can matter more than broad discolouration. Pitting around clips, bends or exposed sections requires careful assessment because the remaining metal may be thinner locally.

Excessive corrosion

Major MOT defect

Where corrosion or damage has reduced the integrity of a rigid brake pipe sufficiently to meet the MOT rejection threshold, the defect is classified as Major and the vehicle fails the MOT.

Leak or imminent failure

Dangerous

A hydraulic brake pipe or connection that is leaking, or a brake pipe considered at imminent risk of failure or fracture, represents a much more serious condition and is classified as Dangerous.

How MOT severity is judged

Current MOT inspection guidance states that chafing, corrosion or damage reducing the wall thickness of a rigid brake pipe by around one-third justifies rejection. For a typical hydraulic brake pipe, the manual gives approximately 0.25 mm as an indication of that reduction, while recognising that accurately determining this during an MOT inspection is difficult.

The tester does not normally measure pipe-wall thickness

The MOT inspection is a visual and condition-based assessment rather than a laboratory measurement of every section of brake pipe. Where surface contamination prevents proper assessment, the tester may carefully remove dirt using an appropriate corrosion assessment tool. The pipe must not be damaged simply to determine its condition.

Do not scrape or aggressively clean a badly corroded pipe yourself

Heavy scraping, wire brushing or bending an already weakened brake pipe can disturb fragile material or expose a section that is close to perforation. If corrosion looks deeply pitted, heavily scaled or concentrated around a clip or union, professional inspection is the safer approach.

Warning signs

Warning Signs a Brake Pipe Problem May Be Getting Worse

Brake pipe corrosion often develops without an obvious change in how the car drives. That is one reason an MOT advisory is useful: it can identify deterioration before the driver experiences a hydraulic braking symptom.

Once a pipe begins leaking, however, the situation changes. Brake fluid is part of a sealed hydraulic system, so unexplained fluid loss, changes in pedal behaviour or visible wetness around brake pipework require prompt investigation.

Brake fluid level falling

An unexplained reduction in brake fluid level can indicate a leak somewhere in the hydraulic system. Do not assume the rigid brake pipes are responsible without locating the source.

Soft or sinking brake pedal

A pedal that suddenly feels unusually soft, travels further than normal or continues sinking under steady pressure can indicate a hydraulic fault and requires urgent diagnosis.

Visible fluid around pipework

Wetness around a rigid brake pipe, union or hydraulic connection should be investigated. Brake fluid leakage from hydraulic pipework is a serious defect.

Brake warning light

A brake-system warning light can have several causes, including low fluid level on vehicles that monitor it. Treat a new brake warning alongside abnormal braking behaviour as a priority.

Reduced braking performance

If the vehicle suddenly requires more pedal travel or braking performance has noticeably deteriorated, stop treating the issue as an advisory-only concern and have the braking system checked.

Corrosion visibly worsening

Heavy scaling, concentrated pitting or deterioration spreading along a previously advised section is a reason to arrange another inspection rather than waiting for the next MOT.

Do not wait for braking symptoms before acting

The absence of a soft pedal or fluid leak does not prove that a corroded brake pipe is in good condition. A weakened pipe can remain sealed until deterioration progresses further. If an advisory has identified corrosion, use the warning as an opportunity to inspect the pipe before hydraulic failure occurs.

Physical inspection

How Should a Corroded Brake Pipe Be Inspected?

A useful brake pipe inspection follows the affected hydraulic line rather than looking at one rusty patch in isolation. The technician needs suitable access, good lighting and enough visibility to compare exposed sections with areas around clips, bends, unions and mounting points.

Inspection should also consider whether the pipe is correctly routed and supported. Corrosion is not the only possible rigid-pipe defect: chafing, physical damage, poor support and unsuitable previous repairs can also affect safety and MOT condition.

1. Identify the pipe

Start with the MOT advisory wording or workshop findings and identify which section of rigid pipework is affected.

2. Follow the accessible run

Inspect beyond the obvious rusty patch. Compare neighbouring sections and check where the pipe passes through clips, bends and exposed underbody areas.

3. Assess deterioration

Look for coating failure, scaling, concentrated pitting, chafing, physical damage and evidence that corrosion has materially reduced the pipe's integrity.

4. Check for leakage

Inspect the pipe and its connections for hydraulic fluid leakage. Any suspected leak changes the urgency of the diagnosis.

Clips and supports

Check that the pipe remains adequately secured and that clips have not created areas where moisture, contamination or movement has damaged the line.

Unions and connections

Inspect connection points for corrosion and leakage. A heavily seized union can also affect how much surrounding pipework needs to be disturbed during replacement.

Previous repairs

Check replacement sections for correct routing, secure support and appropriate hydraulic connections. A recent-looking section does not automatically mean the complete brake pipe run is sound.

MOT inspection and workshop diagnosis are not identical

An MOT is a statutory inspection carried out to prescribed test standards. A workshop assessing whether to repair a vehicle can go further by using additional access, examining the wider pipe run and planning how deteriorated sections should be replaced. An advisory can therefore be the starting point for a more detailed mechanical inspection.

Ask the garage to show you the affected section

When practical, ask where the corrosion is located and whether the concern is general surface deterioration, concentrated pitting, poor support or a section approaching replacement condition. This makes the repair recommendation easier to understand and gives you useful information for future MOT history.

Mechanic diagnosis

How a Mechanic Assesses Corroded Brake Pipes

A mechanic should make the repair decision from the physical condition of the pipe and the surrounding hydraulic system, not simply from the presence of the word "corroded" on an MOT history. The objective is to establish whether the pipe remains serviceable, requires close monitoring or should be replaced.

Check 1

Extent of corrosion

Is deterioration limited to one exposed area, concentrated around a retaining point or present across several sections of the hydraulic pipe run?

Check 2

Depth and condition

Does the pipe show superficial oxidation, or are there signs of scaling, deep localised pitting or substantial material loss?

Check 3

Hydraulic integrity

Is the system dry and maintaining normal hydraulic operation, or is there evidence of fluid leakage or another braking-system fault?

Check 4

Pipe security

Is the rigid line properly clipped and supported, or can movement allow it to chafe against the body, suspension or another component?

Check 5

Condition of nearby sections

Replacing one short area makes little sense if neighbouring original pipework is also heavily deteriorated. The wider condition affects the sensible repair scope.

Check 6

Access and repair route

Pipe routing determines the labour involved. Some lines are easily accessible, while others pass above tanks, subframes, shields or tightly packaged components.

Mechanic decision point

The correct repair is not automatically "replace every brake pipe" and it is not automatically "clean the rust and leave it". The decision should reflect actual pipe condition, the extent of deterioration, accessibility, the condition of neighbouring lines and whether a safe durable repair can be achieved.

Be cautious with cosmetic treatment

Applying grease, paint, underbody coating or corrosion protection over a deteriorated pipe does not restore metal that has already been lost. Protection may be appropriate for sound pipework after proper assessment, but it should never be used to disguise significant corrosion or postpone necessary replacement.

Driving safety

Can You Keep Driving With a Corroded Brake Pipe Advisory?

A brake pipe corrosion advisory does not automatically mean the vehicle became prohibited from normal road use simply because the advisory was recorded. However, the vehicle must still be roadworthy, and an MOT pass does not guarantee that the condition of a corroded brake pipe will remain acceptable for the following year.

How urgently the vehicle should be inspected depends on the actual condition of the pipe. A recently recorded advisory with no hydraulic symptoms is very different from a pipe showing severe deterioration, visible leakage or signs that braking performance has changed.

Advisory only, brakes normal

Arrange inspection

If the vehicle has passed its MOT with an advisory and braking remains normal, arrange inspection of the affected pipe rather than simply waiting until the next annual test.

Corrosion visibly severe

Do not delay

Deep pitting, heavy deterioration or uncertainty about the structural condition of the pipe warrants prompt professional assessment before relying on the vehicle for continued use.

Leak or abnormal braking

Stop using vehicle

If brake fluid is leaking, the pedal becomes soft or sinking, or braking performance is substantially reduced, do not continue normal driving. Arrange safe recovery and professional diagnosis.

A valid MOT does not override roadworthiness

The vehicle keeper remains responsible for keeping the vehicle in a safe and roadworthy condition between MOT tests. A component can deteriorate after the test, so a previous pass should never be used to dismiss new braking symptoms, fluid leakage or obvious worsening corrosion.

Practical priority

Treat the advisory as advance warning. Having the pipe inspected while it is still an advisory gives you the opportunity to plan the repair before the condition progresses into an MOT failure or a hydraulic braking fault.

UK MOT rules

When Does a Corroded Brake Pipe Fail an MOT?

A brake pipe does not fail an MOT simply because some corrosion is visible. The tester assesses the condition of the rigid pipework and determines whether corrosion, damage, leakage or inadequate support reaches the relevant rejection criteria.

This distinction is important when interpreting an older MOT advisory. A previous advisory records deterioration that did not result in rejection at that test. If corrosion progresses, the same section can later become a Major defect. If the pipe becomes liable to imminent failure or a hydraulic leak develops, the classification becomes Dangerous.

Advisory territory

Corrosion noticed but not rejected

Deterioration may be recorded for the owner's attention where the tester identifies corrosion but does not consider the rigid pipe sufficiently deteriorated to justify rejection at that test.

Major

Damaged or excessively corroded

A rigid brake pipe that is damaged or excessively corroded is a Major defect. The vehicle fails the MOT and the affected pipework requires appropriate repair before the defect can be cleared.

Dangerous

Imminent risk of failure

If a brake pipe is considered at imminent risk of failure or fracture, the defect is Dangerous. This represents a substantially more serious condition than an ordinary corrosion advisory.

Dangerous

Hydraulic pipe leaking

Leakage from a hydraulic brake pipe or connection is categorised as Dangerous because the braking system relies on maintaining hydraulic fluid and pressure.

The MOT corrosion threshold is not simply "visible rust"

DVSA guidance states that chafing, corrosion or damage reducing the wall thickness of a rigid brake pipe by around one-third justifies rejection. For a typical hydraulic brake pipe, the guidance gives approximately 0.25 mm as an indication of that reduction. It also acknowledges that this is not easy to determine during an MOT and instructs testers to give the benefit of the doubt where they are unsure whether deterioration is sufficient to justify rejection.

An old advisory does not preserve the old MOT assessment

A brake pipe that received an advisory last year must be assessed in its current condition at the next MOT. Corrosion can progress from observable deterioration to excessive corrosion, leakage or imminent failure. Previous MOT wording does not prevent the tester from applying a more serious defect classification later.

Repair options

How Are Corroded Brake Pipes Repaired?

The correct repair depends on how much of the pipe is affected, where the deterioration is located and the condition of the remaining hydraulic line. Early surface deterioration and a deeply pitted pipe approaching failure are not the same repair job.

Before authorising replacement, a competent garage should establish where sound pipe begins and ends, whether nearby lines are also deteriorating and how the original pipe is routed. Access can have as much influence on the labour time as the length of replacement pipe itself.

Option 1

Inspect and monitor

Where deterioration is genuinely superficial and the pipe remains structurally sound, immediate replacement may not be necessary. The important point is to record the affected area and monitor its condition rather than forgetting the advisory.

Option 2

Protect sound pipework

Once a pipe has been properly assessed as serviceable, suitable corrosion protection may help reduce further environmental attack. Protection does not reverse pitting or restore metal already lost through corrosion.

Option 3

Replace the affected pipe

Significant deterioration requires replacement with suitable brake pipe material, correctly formed and routed, securely supported and connected using appropriate hydraulic fittings.

Partial section or complete pipe run?

It is not always necessary to replace every rigid brake pipe on the vehicle because one section has deteriorated. Equally, fitting a very short new section into extensively corroded surrounding pipework may provide poor value and leave another weak area close to the repair.

The technician should consider the condition of the complete accessible run, the number and position of connections required, access to the original unions and whether a longer replacement provides a cleaner, more durable repair.

Localised deterioration

A limited repair may be reasonable where the affected area is clearly defined and the remaining pipework is in good condition. Any hydraulic connections used must be appropriate for brake pressure lines.

Corrosion along the run

Where several sections of the same pipe are deteriorated, replacing a longer run may avoid joining new pipe onto metal that is already approaching the end of its useful service life.

Several pipes affected

If neighbouring original pipes have experienced the same years of exposure, inspect them before deciding the repair scope. Similar age does not automatically mean every pipe requires replacement, but their condition should be established.

Not every brake-pipe connector is acceptable

DVSA guidance specifically states that repairs to hydraulic pressure lines are unacceptable unless suitable connectors are used. Compression joints that use separate ferrules are not considered suitable for this purpose. A brake pipe repair must be designed for the hydraulic braking system rather than treated like an ordinary low-pressure plumbing repair.

Ask what is actually included in the quote

"Brake pipe replacement" can describe very different jobs. Ask whether the quote covers one local section, a complete front-to-rear line, multiple pipes, new unions, bleeding the hydraulic system and any additional labour required to gain access. Comparing quotes is difficult unless the repair scope is the same.

DIY vs garage

Should You Replace Corroded Brake Pipes Yourself?

Brake pipe replacement is possible for an experienced person with the correct tools and knowledge, but it is not a good first braking-system repair. The job involves more than cutting a piece of tube to length. Pipe specification, flare formation, fittings, routing, support, hydraulic sealing and correct bleeding all matter.

A poor repair can leak, chafe against another component, become damaged through suspension movement or introduce air into the hydraulic system. Because the consequences affect braking, uncertainty at any stage is a strong reason to use a competent garage.

Inspection and monitoring

Knowledge required

Owners can monitor an advisory and look for obvious changes, but judging whether corrosion has materially weakened a pipe requires experience. Do not aggressively scrape a questionable hydraulic line to find out how thin it has become.

Brake pipe fabrication

Advanced DIY

Replacement requires the correct pipe, compatible fittings, accurately formed flares and careful routing. Connections must seal correctly under hydraulic braking pressure.

Bleeding and final checks

Safety critical

Opening the hydraulic circuit requires the braking system to be correctly bled and checked afterwards. The finished system must maintain hydraulic integrity and normal pedal operation.

Do not improvise a hydraulic brake-line repair

Temporary-looking joints, unsuitable fittings or a repair that leaves the pipe unsupported can create another defect rather than solving the original corrosion problem. If you cannot positively identify the correct pipe, flare, connector and bleeding procedure for the vehicle, use a competent repairer.

Brake pipe replacement does not have one useful fixed price because labour varies enormously between vehicles. A short accessible section can be relatively straightforward, while a long pipe routed above a fuel tank, subframe or underbody components can turn inexpensive pipe material into a labour-intensive repair.

Best way to compare garage quotes

Ask each garage to identify the affected pipe, how much of it they intend to replace, whether access requires other components to be removed, whether brake bleeding is included and whether the price includes VAT. For a safety-critical hydraulic line, repair quality and the condition of the remaining system matter more than achieving the lowest possible invoice.

Future MOT risk

Will a Corroded Brake Pipe Advisory Fail the Next MOT?

A previous brake pipe advisory does not automatically mean the vehicle will fail its next MOT. The tester assesses the condition that exists on the day of the next test. If the pipe remains below the rejection threshold, it may still pass. If corrosion has progressed far enough to become excessive, the same area can become a Major defect.

The difficulty is that corrosion does not progress at a predictable annual rate. A section exposed to road salt, persistent moisture or trapped contamination around a retaining clip may deteriorate faster than another section of the same pipe. Waiting for the next MOT is therefore not a reliable inspection strategy.

Lower immediate risk

Light deterioration only

If a professional inspection confirms that corrosion is superficial and the pipe remains sound, the sensible approach may be monitoring and appropriate protection rather than immediate replacement.

Increasing risk

Repeated MOT advisory

The same brake pipe appearing repeatedly in MOT history deserves closer attention. Repeated wording does not prove that deterioration is unchanged, and progression may eventually move the pipe into failure territory.

High risk

Deep or spreading corrosion

Visible pitting, scaling or deterioration extending along the pipe makes future failure more likely and is a strong reason to obtain a repair assessment before presenting the vehicle for another MOT.

Use the advisory as repair-planning time

One advantage of identifying deterioration before it becomes an MOT failure is that you can inspect the pipe, obtain quotations and plan the repair rather than dealing with a failed vehicle unexpectedly on test day.

Do not rely on the next MOT to decide whether the pipe is safe

The MOT is not a substitute for maintenance between tests. If the corrosion has visibly worsened, brake fluid is being lost or braking behaviour changes, arrange inspection immediately rather than waiting for the next scheduled MOT.

Used car buying

Should You Buy a Car With a Corroded Brake Pipe Advisory?

A brake pipe corrosion advisory is not automatically a reason to reject a used car. It is, however, a reason to investigate the vehicle more carefully before agreeing a price. The most important questions are whether the corrosion still exists, whether it has progressed and whether other underbody components show similar deterioration.

A seller may say that an advisory was "only minor", but an old MOT result cannot tell you the present condition of the pipe. Where possible, inspect the vehicle underneath or arrange an independent pre-purchase inspection.

One old advisory, repaired

Lower buying risk

An older advisory followed by documented professional replacement is usually much less concerning, provided the repair and surrounding pipework are in good condition.

Recent advisory, no repair evidence

Inspect before buying

Treat the likely repair cost as unresolved. Ask where the affected pipe is located and obtain a proper inspection rather than assuming that another MOT pass guarantees future serviceability.

Repeated corrosion history

Investigate carefully

Repeated brake pipe advisories can indicate continuing deterioration or repairs that have not addressed the full affected area. Inspect both the identified pipe and neighbouring lines.

What else should you inspect?

Brake pipe corrosion can sometimes be part of a broader underbody condition rather than an isolated defect. A vehicle exposed to years of moisture and winter road contamination may also show deterioration around brake hose fittings, suspension mounting areas, exhaust components and structural seams.

Read the MOT history as a timeline

Do not look only at the latest result. Check whether brake pipe corrosion first appeared several years ago, whether its wording or location changed, whether it disappeared after documented repair and whether related braking or corrosion defects appeared later.

Be cautious if fresh coating hides the pipework

New underbody coating is not automatically suspicious, but it should not prevent you from establishing the condition of safety-critical components. Where an MOT history already mentions brake pipe corrosion, ask for repair invoices or inspection evidence rather than relying on a freshly coated appearance.

Buying decision

A properly repaired historic advisory is very different from active corrosion that has been repeatedly recorded and never investigated. Base the purchase decision on current condition, repair evidence and likely future costs rather than on the word "advisory" alone.

FAQs

Corroded Brake Pipe Advisory FAQs

These answers cover the most common questions UK drivers ask after finding brake pipe corrosion in an MOT history or receiving a new advisory.

What does a corroded brake pipe advisory mean?

A corroded brake pipe advisory means the MOT tester identified deterioration on rigid brake pipework, but the condition recorded at that test was not sufficient to justify rejection. It should be treated as a warning to inspect and monitor the affected pipe because corrosion can continue to progress.

Is a corroded brake pipe advisory serious?

Yes. Brake pipes carry pressurised brake fluid, so corrosion is safety-related rather than cosmetic. An advisory does not mean the pipe is about to fail, but it does mean deterioration has been identified and should not be ignored.

Can I drive with a corroded brake pipe advisory?

A vehicle that passed its MOT with a brake pipe advisory is not automatically prohibited from normal road use because of that advisory alone, but it must remain roadworthy. Arrange inspection of the affected pipe, and stop using the vehicle if there is brake fluid leakage, a soft or sinking pedal, substantially reduced braking or another sign of a hydraulic fault.

Can a car pass an MOT with brake pipe corrosion?

Yes. Visible corrosion does not automatically cause an MOT failure. A tester can record deterioration as an advisory where the condition does not meet the rejection criteria at the time of the test.

When does a corroded brake pipe fail an MOT?

A rigid brake pipe fails the MOT when it is damaged or excessively corroded to the point that it meets the prescribed rejection criteria. Current MOT guidance states that chafing, corrosion or damage reducing pipe wall thickness by around one-third justifies rejection.

Is an excessively corroded brake pipe a Major or Dangerous defect?

A rigid brake pipe that is damaged or excessively corroded is a Major defect. A brake pipe at imminent risk of failure or fracture is Dangerous, so the classification becomes more serious when failure is considered imminent.

What happens if a brake pipe starts leaking?

A leaking hydraulic brake pipe or connection is classified as a Dangerous MOT defect. Brake fluid loss can reduce hydraulic pressure and braking performance, so the vehicle should not continue in normal use and should be professionally repaired before being driven again.

How quickly can brake pipe corrosion get worse?

There is no reliable fixed timescale. Progression depends on pipe material, coating condition, road salt, moisture, trapped dirt, vehicle storage and the depth of existing corrosion. A pipe that received an advisory should therefore be assessed by condition rather than assumed safe until a particular date.

Will a brake pipe advisory fail the next MOT?

Not necessarily, but it can. If the corrosion remains below the rejection threshold the pipe may still pass, while deterioration that becomes excessive can result in a Major defect. Leakage or imminent risk of failure can result in a Dangerous defect.

Can light brake pipe corrosion be cleaned and protected?

Light deterioration on pipework that has been properly assessed as structurally sound may be suitable for careful cleaning and corrosion protection. Protective treatment cannot restore metal already lost through pitting or excessive corrosion and should never be used to disguise a weakened pipe.

Can you repair only one section of a brake pipe?

Sometimes. A local section can be replaced where the remaining pipework is sound and the repair uses suitable brake-line material, correct flares, secure routing and appropriate hydraulic connectors. The wider pipe run should be inspected before deciding that a short repair is sufficient.

Do both brake pipes need replacing at the same time?

No. Brake pipes do not automatically have to be replaced in pairs. Replacement should be based on the condition of each pipe, although neighbouring lines of similar age and exposure should be inspected because they may be deteriorating at the same time.

How much does brake pipe replacement cost in the UK?

An accessible local section may cost roughly £80–£180, while a longer pipe run can be around £150–£350 or more. Difficult access, component removal or several affected pipes can push the total into several hundred pounds, so a vehicle-specific quote is more useful than a single national price.

Why are brake pipe replacement jobs sometimes expensive?

The pipe material itself is often only a small part of the bill. Labour can include gaining access, releasing corroded unions, forming and routing new pipe, fitting secure supports, removing obstructing components, bleeding the hydraulic system and checking the finished repair for leaks and correct pedal operation.

Where do brake pipes usually corrode?

Common areas include underbody pipe runs, retaining clips, bends, unions, rear brake pipework and sections exposed to road spray, salt and trapped dirt. Partially hidden sections near covers, tanks, subframes or mounting points can also deteriorate.

How does a mechanic check a corroded brake pipe?

A mechanic follows the accessible pipe run and checks coating condition, scaling, pitting, chafing, physical damage, clips, supports, unions and evidence of hydraulic leakage. The technician also considers the condition of neighbouring pipework and whether any previous repair is correctly routed and connected.

Should I buy a used car with a brake pipe corrosion advisory?

Not automatically reject it, but investigate it before buying. Check whether the advisory is recent or repeated, ask for evidence of any repair, inspect the wider underbody condition and budget for replacement if the affected pipe has not been professionally assessed.

Does a valid MOT mean a corroded brake pipe is safe for a year?

No. An MOT records the vehicle's condition against the test requirements at the time of inspection; it does not guarantee that every component will remain roadworthy for the following year. The vehicle keeper remains responsible for maintaining a safe and roadworthy vehicle between tests.
