Quick answer

What should you check before buying a used car?

Before buying a used car in the UK, check the MOT history, service history, V5C document, VIN, mileage records, ownership details, dashboard warning lights, tyres, brakes, suspension, bodywork, accident-repair signs, fluid levels and leaks. Start the engine from cold where possible and take a proper test drive before agreeing a price.

Do not judge the car from one good sign. A recent MOT does not automatically mean the vehicle has been serviced properly, and low mileage does not guarantee that the clutch, gearbox, engine, cooling system or other expensive components are healthy. The advert, seller's answers, paperwork, MOT history, service records, mileage, physical condition, cold start, test drive and asking price should all make sense together.

Before you view

Check the advert carefully, compare the asking price with similar cars, review the MOT history and ask about service records, ownership, faults, warning lights, recent repairs and why the vehicle is being sold.

At the car

Check the V5C and VIN, tyres, brakes, bodywork, fluids, leaks, warning lights, interior wear and service evidence. Whenever possible, inspect the engine before it has already been warmed up for your arrival.

Before you pay

Complete a proper test drive, investigate suspicious noises or warning lights, estimate upcoming repair costs and make sure the paperwork, history, condition and final price still add up.

Mechanic insight:

Do not inspect a used car looking for reasons to buy it. Inspect it looking for reasons not to buy it. A genuinely good car should still make sense after you check its history, paperwork, condition and road behaviour properly.

A fresh MOT is not a full mechanical inspection

An MOT is important, but it does not prove that every mechanical component is healthy or that the vehicle has been maintained well. Clutch wear, gearbox problems, timing-chain noise, poor servicing, cooling-system problems and developing repair costs can still exist on a car with a valid MOT.

Used car buying priorities

1. Verify the history

Check whether the mileage, MOT records, service history, paperwork and seller's description tell the same story.

2. Inspect the car

Look beyond clean paint. Check tyres, brakes, fluids, leaks, corrosion, warning lights, accident-repair signs and general condition.

3. Drive it properly

Listen and feel for clutch, gearbox, steering, suspension, braking, vibration, overheating, smoke and performance problems.

4. Price the risk

Consider immediate maintenance, MOT advisories and likely repairs before deciding whether to buy, negotiate or walk away.

Before You Start Looking

Set Your Real Used Car Budget

Your buying budget should not be the same as the amount you have available to spend on the car. A £5,000 budget does not necessarily mean you should buy a £5,000 vehicle.

Leave room for insurance, vehicle tax where applicable, servicing, tyres, brakes and any maintenance the car needs soon after purchase. A cheaper car with several overdue jobs can cost more than a slightly more expensive example that has been maintained properly.

Purchase price

Compare the asking price with similar cars of the same age, engine, mileage, specification and condition. A suspiciously cheap car deserves more investigation, not less.

Insurance

Get an insurance quote for the vehicle before committing to it. This is especially important for new drivers because two similarly priced cars can have very different insurance costs.

Immediate maintenance

Allow for servicing, tyres, brakes, battery replacement, fluids and other maintenance that may be due shortly after you take ownership.

History & Paperwork

Check the Car's Identity and History Before You Buy

A used car should make sense on paper before you become interested in how it looks or drives. Check that the registration, VIN, V5C details, mileage, MOT records, service history and seller's description all fit together.

If one part of the story does not match another, do not explain the problem away for the seller. Investigate it before paying a deposit or handing over money.

V5C registration certificate

Check the V5C details against the vehicle in front of you. Look carefully at the registration number, make, model and other identifying information.

VIN

Check the vehicle identification number on the car against the paperwork. Be cautious if numbers are missing, damaged, altered or do not agree.

Mileage

Compare the displayed mileage with MOT records, service invoices and the general wear inside the vehicle. The numbers should form a believable progression.

Service history

Look for evidence of regular maintenance rather than simply accepting the phrase “full service history” in an advert.

Receipts and invoices

Repair invoices can help confirm what has actually been replaced and when. They may also reveal recurring faults or major work the seller has not mentioned.

Seller's story

Ask how long the seller has owned the car, why it is being sold, what has recently been repaired and whether any known faults remain.

The V5C is important, but it is not proof that the vehicle is problem-free.

Treat identity, ownership information, MOT records, service history, vehicle-history information and the physical car as separate checks. One document should not be used as a substitute for checking everything else.

Does the paperwork tell one consistent story?

Good signs

  • ✓VIN and registration details agree.
  • ✓Mileage rises consistently over time.
  • ✓Service records fit the age and mileage.
  • ✓Repair invoices support work the seller describes.
  • ✓The seller answers reasonable questions clearly.

Warning signs

  • ✓VIN or vehicle details do not match.
  • ✓Mileage history is difficult to explain.
  • ✓Long gaps appear in maintenance records.
  • ✓The seller cannot explain recent repairs or faults.
  • ✓You are pressured to pay before completing checks.
Check before travelling.

Ask the seller for the registration number and enough information to carry out your initial checks before making a long journey. Our questions to ask when buying a used car guide can help you screen the vehicle and seller before arranging a viewing.

Maintenance Evidence

What Should You Look for in a Used Car Service History?

A service book with several stamps is useful, but good maintenance evidence goes further than that. Look for dates, mileages, invoices and records showing what work was actually carried out.

The important question is not simply whether the car has a service history. It is whether that history suggests the vehicle has been maintained properly throughout its life.

Regular servicing

Check whether servicing appears reasonably consistent with the vehicle's age, mileage and manufacturer schedule.

Major maintenance

Where relevant, look for evidence of items such as timing-belt replacement, gearbox servicing, spark plugs, filters and other scheduled work.

Repair history

Receipts for brakes, suspension, cooling-system repairs, batteries and other components can show that previous problems were dealt with rather than ignored.

Missing history does not automatically prove a bad car.

But uncertainty has value. If maintenance cannot be verified, judge the car more cautiously and allow for the possibility that overdue servicing or preventative work may become your responsibility after purchase.

MOT History

How to Read a Used Car's MOT History Properly

MOT history is one of the most useful free sources of information available to a UK used car buyer. Do not look only at whether the car passed its latest test. Read the history across several years and look for patterns.

Previous MOT records can help you see recorded mileage, failures, advisories and recurring defects. They can also show whether the same type of problem appears repeatedly rather than being repaired properly.

Mileage progression

Recorded mileage should generally rise in a logical way from test to test. An unusual change deserves an explanation and further checking.

Repeated advisories

If the same tyre, brake, suspension, corrosion or leak issue appears repeatedly, ask whether the underlying problem was ever properly repaired.

Failure patterns

A long sequence of failures and neglected advisories may tell you something about how the vehicle has historically been maintained.

A clean MOT history is useful — but it is not a service history.

The MOT is a roadworthiness test against testable items at the time of inspection. It should not be treated as proof that the engine, clutch, gearbox, timing system, cooling system or every other mechanical component is in good condition.

Physical Inspection

How to Inspect a Used Car Before the Test Drive

Inspect the car in good daylight if possible and give yourself enough time. Do not let fresh polish, a clean interior or an enthusiastic seller rush you through the checks that actually matter.

Start outside, work around the vehicle systematically, check underneath where it is safe to do so, then inspect the engine bay and interior before starting the engine.

1. Bodywork and accident-repair signs

Panel gaps

Compare gaps around the bonnet, wings, doors and boot. Noticeable differences can justify a closer look for previous repair work.

Paint finish

Look along the panels from different angles for colour differences, overspray, unusual texture or areas that do not match surrounding paint.

Lights and glass

Check lamps for damage or moisture and inspect the windscreen and other glass for cracks, chips or obvious replacement issues.

Suspect previous body repairs?

Continue with our How to Spot Accident Repair guide before deciding whether previous damage is acceptable or whether the vehicle needs further investigation.

2. Tyres and wheels

Tread and damage

Check all four tyres, not just the easiest ones to see. Look for low tread, cuts, cracking, bulges and other obvious damage.

Uneven wear

Uneven tyre wear can be associated with pressure problems, alignment, suspension wear or other issues that deserve investigation.

Tyre quality

A mixture of very different tyre brands or bargain replacements is not automatically a fault, but it can provide clues about previous maintenance priorities.

3. Look underneath the vehicle

You do not need to crawl underneath an unsafe vehicle. From a safe position, look for obvious fluid on the ground, damaged undertrays, hanging exhaust parts and visible corrosion or damage.

Fresh fluid deserves investigation.

Do not accept “they all leak a bit” as a diagnosis. Identify what is leaking, where it appears to come from and how serious the repair could be before agreeing a price.

4. Check under the bonnet

Engine oil

Check the oil level where the vehicle design allows it and look for obvious signs of leakage, contamination or neglected maintenance.

Coolant

With the engine cold, inspect the coolant level and look around the expansion tank, hoses and engine bay for staining or evidence of leakage.

General condition

Look for loose wiring, damaged hoses, obvious bodged repairs, missing covers and fresh cleaning that may make leaks harder to identify.

Never remove a pressurised coolant cap from a hot engine.

Hot coolant can cause serious burns. Cooling-system checks around the expansion tank should be carried out only when it is safe to do so.

5. Inspect the interior before starting the engine

Wear vs mileage

Look at the steering wheel, pedals, driver's seat and frequently used controls. Heavy wear on a supposedly very low-mileage car deserves a closer look at the history.

Electrical equipment

Check windows, mirrors, locks, heating, air conditioning where fitted, infotainment, cameras, parking sensors and other important equipment.

Warning-light check

When the ignition is switched on, observe the dashboard warning lamps. After the engine starts, investigate important warning lights that remain illuminated instead of assuming they are harmless.

Cold-Start Check

Why You Should Start a Used Car From Cold

A cold start can reveal problems that are much harder to notice once the engine has already been warmed up. If possible, ask the seller not to start the car before you arrive.

Before starting it, carefully check whether the engine actually feels cold. If the bonnet, engine or cooling system is already warm when you expected a cold vehicle, ask why it has been run.

Arrived and the engine is already hot?

That does not automatically mean the seller is hiding something, but it removes one of your most useful inspection opportunities. Ask why the car was started and, if the cold-start condition matters to your decision, consider arranging another check rather than guessing.

Before you turn the key or press the start button

Check the dashboard

Switch the ignition on and observe the warning lamps. Important systems normally carry out a lamp check before the engine starts.

Listen before starting

Turn off the radio and climate fan if practical. A quiet cabin makes it much easier to hear unusual noises during the first few seconds after start-up.

Watch the exhaust

If it is safe, position yourself so the exhaust can be observed after the engine starts. Persistent or unusual smoke deserves investigation.

Engine & Mechanical Checks

Let the Engine Run and Look for Developing Problems

Once the engine has started, do not immediately rush into the test drive. Give yourself time to listen, watch the dashboard and see how the vehicle behaves as it begins to warm up.

You are not trying to diagnose every possible fault at the roadside. You are looking for symptoms that change the buying risk or justify a professional inspection before you commit.

Idle quality

The engine should not repeatedly stall, misfire heavily or shake abnormally. An unstable idle deserves investigation rather than being dismissed as normal for an older car.

Engine noises

Listen for persistent knocking, tapping, rattling, squealing or other unusual sounds. Consider whether the noise changes as the engine warms or when the engine speed changes.

Dashboard warnings

Important warning lamps should not simply be ignored because the car still drives. Find out what the light means and why it is illuminated.

Fluid leaks

Look again underneath and around the engine bay after the vehicle has been running. Fresh oil, coolant or other fluid may become easier to identify.

Cooling system

Watch the temperature indication during the inspection and later test drive. Overheating, coolant loss or abnormal cooling-system behaviour can turn a cheap purchase into a major repair.

Exhaust

Listen for excessive blowing or unusual exhaust noise and continue watching for persistent smoke once the engine has warmed.

Cooling-system problems deserve particular caution.

Do not assume topping up coolant will solve repeated coolant loss, bubbling or overheating. Those are symptoms that need a cause, not just more coolant.

Warning signs worth investigating

  • ✓Engine-management light remains on.
  • ✓Oil-pressure warning remains illuminated.
  • ✓Coolant-temperature warning appears.
  • ✓Engine runs unevenly or repeatedly stalls.
  • ✓Persistent smoke continues after warm-up.
  • ✓Fresh fluid appears underneath the car.

Do not diagnose from one clue

A warning light, noise or leak tells you that something needs checking; it does not automatically tell you which component has failed.

This matters when buying because a seller may describe a fault as a cheap sensor, simple seal or easy repair without having evidence to support that diagnosis.

Diagnostic Test Drive

How to Test Drive a Used Car Properly

A proper test drive is not a quick trip around the block with the radio playing. You need enough time and suitable road conditions to check starting, low-speed manoeuvring, clutch or automatic gearbox behaviour, steering, suspension, braking, acceleration, vibration, temperature and warning lights.

Drive legally and within your ability. The purpose is not to push the car hard. It is to see whether the vehicle behaves consistently under normal driving conditions.

Confirm you are insured before driving.

Never assume you are automatically covered to test drive somebody else's car. Make sure suitable insurance is actually in place before you drive on the road.

Use the test drive as a diagnostic process

1. Low speed

Check clutch take-up or automatic engagement, steering effort, low-speed noises and how smoothly the car moves away.

2. Normal driving

Check gear changes, acceleration, steering stability, suspension behaviour and whether the engine pulls smoothly.

3. Braking

On a safe road, assess whether braking feels smooth and controlled without excessive vibration, pulling or unusual noise.

4. Recheck afterwards

Leave the engine running briefly where appropriate, check the dashboard again and look for new smells, leaks or symptoms that appeared after the drive.

Clutch and manual gearbox

Clutch take-up

Notice whether pulling away is smooth. Repeated judder, vibration or difficulty moving away deserves investigation.

Clutch slip

If engine speed rises without the expected increase in road speed under normal load, clutch slip may be present and should not be ignored.

Gear selection

Check that gears select normally without persistent crunching, excessive resistance or jumping out of gear.

Clutch feels wrong?

If the vehicle judders when moving away, our Clutch Judder When Pulling Away guide explains the common causes and why the symptom matters when buying a used car.

Automatic gearbox

Selecting Drive

Pay attention to excessive delay, harsh engagement or a strong clunk when Drive is selected.

Selecting Reverse

Reverse should engage predictably. A noticeable delay, repeated failure to engage or severe jolt deserves investigation.

Changes on the road

Watch for slipping, shuddering, harsh changes, unexpected revving, warning messages or a gearbox that becomes stuck in one gear.

Do not buy an automatic gearbox fault on a seller's promise.

Statements such as “it only needs an oil change” or “it's probably just a sensor” should not replace diagnosis. Automatic gearbox repairs can be expensive, so establish the likely cause before pricing the risk.

Steering and suspension

Steering

Check whether the steering feels consistent and whether the vehicle tracks normally. Investigate excessive play, pulling, vibration or unusual steering noises.

Suspension

Listen for knocks, clunks and creaks over suitable road surfaces. Pay attention to excessive bouncing, instability or poor control.

Wheel-bearing noise

A humming or droning noise that changes with road speed can justify further investigation of wheel bearings, tyres and other rotating components.

Hear a noise that changes with speed?

Use our Wheel Bearing Noise When Speeding Up guide to understand why road-speed-related noise should be diagnosed rather than guessed.

Braking

On a suitable road and at a safe speed, the car should slow in a controlled way. Pay attention to the steering wheel, brake pedal, vehicle direction and any unusual noise.

Brake vibration

Vibration through the pedal, steering wheel or vehicle during braking deserves investigation before purchase.

Pulling under braking

If the vehicle consistently pulls to one side when braking, the cause needs to be identified rather than accepted as normal.

Brake noise

Grinding, severe scraping, repeated clunks or other abnormal noises can indicate wear or faults that need checking.

Car shakes when braking?

Continue with our Car Shakes When Braking guide before assuming the problem is simply worn brake pads.

Acceleration and engine performance

Under normal acceleration, the engine should respond consistently without severe hesitation, repeated misfiring, unexpected loss of power or warning lights appearing.

Slow acceleration

A vehicle that feels unusually weak may have an engine, turbo, fuelling, airflow, exhaust, transmission or other problem that requires diagnosis.

Stuttering or hesitation

Repeated hesitation under acceleration is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Do not assume a cheap ignition part will solve it.

Revs rise but speed does not

On a manual car this may point towards clutch slip; on other drivetrains it can indicate a different transmission-related problem.

Performance does not feel right?

Relevant Motor Vehicle Expert diagnostic guides include Car Feels Slow to Accelerate, Car Stutters When Accelerating and Car Revs But Won't Accelerate.

Temperature, warning lights and final checks

Continue watching the temperature and dashboard throughout the drive. Some faults only appear once the vehicle is warm or has been driven under load.

  • ✓Temperature remains normal.
  • ✓No new warning lights appear.
  • ✓No obvious loss of power develops.
  • ✓No new vibration appears as road speed increases.
  • ✓No strong burning or coolant smell develops.
  • ✓No fresh fluid is visible after the drive.

Buyer Risk Dashboard

How Risky Is the Used Car You're Looking At?

Used cars do not need to be perfect. Wear is normal, and many faults are repairable. The important question is whether the vehicle's condition, history and likely repair costs still make sense at the asking price.

Use the whole picture rather than judging one defect in isolation.

Low Risk

Generally reassuring

Strong history, believable mileage, no major warning symptoms, sensible MOT record and only normal age-related wear.

Medium Risk

Price the work

Tyres, brakes, servicing or other manageable maintenance is approaching, but the condition and price may still make sense.

High Risk

Diagnosis needed

Warning lights, poor history, repeated MOT problems, leaks, clutch or gearbox symptoms, overheating concerns or unexplained noises.

Walk Away

Do not ignore

Identity problems, suspicious history, serious safety concerns, severe overheating, major mechanical symptoms or a seller preventing reasonable checks.

Risk increases when problems appear together

One worn tyre can be a straightforward maintenance item. A worn tyre combined with suspension knocks, repeated MOT advisories, poor steering and missing service history is a different buying decision.

The same applies to an oil leak, warning light or gearbox symptom. The more uncertainty you have, the more important diagnosis and repair-cost planning become.

Separate the fault from the price.

A car with known maintenance needs can still be a reasonable purchase if the faults are understood, the vehicle is otherwise sound and the price genuinely reflects the work. An unknown expensive fault hidden behind a cheap asking price is much harder to justify.

Buying Decision

Should You Buy, Negotiate or Walk Away?

Finding a fault does not automatically mean you should reject the car. Used vehicles naturally have wear. What matters is the seriousness of the problem, how confidently it can be diagnosed, what it may cost to repair and whether the asking price reflects that risk.

What you find Typical decision Why
Strong history, sensible MOT record and no significant symptoms Consider buying The available evidence supports the vehicle, subject to the price and final checks.

Brake or suspension wear with a clear diagnosis Negotiate carefully The car may still make sense if repair costs are known and reflected in the price.

Clutch slip or automatic gearbox symptoms High caution Transmission repairs can materially change the value of the car.
Repeated overheating or unexplained coolant loss High caution / walk away The cause may be expensive and continued overheating can lead to further engine damage.

BUY

Evidence supports it

History, paperwork, inspection and test drive are consistent, and the price makes sense for the vehicle's age and condition.

NEGOTIATE

Known costs

The car has understandable maintenance or repair needs that can be reasonably priced and reflected in what you pay.

WALK AWAY

Too much uncertainty

Major symptoms, questionable history, safety concerns or seller behaviour leave too many expensive unanswered questions.

Do not let money already spent influence the decision.

Travelling to see the car, paying for a history check or spending an hour inspecting it does not mean you need to buy it. Walking away from the wrong vehicle can be the cheapest decision you make.

Repair-Cost Planning

Work Out What the Car Could Cost After You Buy It

The asking price is only part of the cost of buying a used car. Before you agree a deal, make a simple list of the maintenance and repairs the vehicle may need during the first few months of ownership.

This does not mean rejecting every car with worn parts. Tyres, brakes and servicing are normal ownership costs. The important thing is knowing what is due, how urgent it is and whether the asking price still makes sense once that work is included.

Immediate costs

Think about faults or maintenance that need attention straight away, including unsafe tyres, brake problems, warning lights, leaks or an overdue service.

Near-future costs

MOT advisories, worn tyres, brakes, suspension components, battery condition and scheduled maintenance may create costs shortly after purchase.

High-cost risks

Clutch, automatic gearbox, turbo, timing-system, DPF, cooling-system and serious engine problems can change whether the car is worth buying at all.

Price the car you are actually buying.

A £4,500 car that immediately needs £1,000 of work is not really a £4,500 purchase. Use known repair and maintenance needs when comparing it with cleaner examples.

Do not negotiate an unknown major fault as though it were a known repair.

A warning light, overheating problem, gearbox symptom or serious engine noise can have several possible causes. Diagnosis should come before deciding how much discount makes the risk acceptable.

First Car & New Drivers

Buying Your First Car? Choose for Ownership, Not Just Looks

Your first car does not need to be the cheapest vehicle you can find. It needs to be a car you can realistically insure, maintain, fuel and repair while you build experience on the road.

A slightly more expensive car with sensible insurance costs, good maintenance history and fewer immediate repairs can be a much better first purchase than a cheap car that quickly needs tyres, brakes, servicing and mechanical work.

Easy to insure

Get real insurance quotes before buying. Do not assume a small engine or low purchase price automatically means cheap insurance.

Easy to live with

Think about visibility, parking, driving position, fuel use, the type of journeys you make and whether you actually feel comfortable driving the car.

Easy to maintain

A first car with sensible maintenance requirements and widely available parts can make ownership easier than a cheap vehicle carrying expensive mechanical risks.

Scams & Hidden History

Check What the Seller May Not Be Telling You

Mechanical condition is only half of the used-car buying decision. A car can drive well and still have history or identity problems that make it a poor purchase.

Be particularly careful when the price looks unusually low, the seller wants to rush the transaction, the paperwork is incomplete or the story changes when you ask detailed questions.

Stolen or identity concerns

Where You Buy

Dealer vs Private Seller: What Changes?

You should inspect the car carefully whether you buy privately or from a motor trader. But the buying situation is not identical, so do not assume the same practical protections apply in every sale.

Buying from a dealer

A trader sale normally gives a consumer stronger legal protection than a private purchase, but that does not mean you should skip the history check, inspection or test drive.

  • ✓Check exactly who the selling business is.
  • ✓Read what is included in any warranty.
  • ✓Keep the advert and purchase paperwork.
  • ✓Still inspect and test drive the car properly.

Buying privately

Private purchases need particular care because your practical position can be very different from buying from a trader.

  • ✓Confirm who is actually selling the vehicle.
  • ✓Check the V5C and vehicle identity carefully.
  • ✓Do not rely on verbal promises about faults.
  • ✓Keep evidence of the advert, agreement and payment.
Watch for disguised traders.

If somebody appears to be regularly selling vehicles but presents the sale as private, be cautious. Ask clear questions about ownership, how long they have had the vehicle and why it is being sold.

If the service history is uncertain, consider establishing a known maintenance starting point rather than assuming everything is up to date.

Prioritise known tyre, brake, steering, suspension, lighting or other safety-related problems instead of leaving them until the next MOT.

Frequently Asked Questions

Used Car Buying FAQs

These are some of the most common questions UK drivers ask when buying a used car, checking vehicle history, inspecting condition and deciding whether a car is worth buying.

What should I check before buying a used car in the UK?

Check the vehicle's identity, V5C details, VIN, MOT history, mileage, service history, tyres, brakes, bodywork, fluids, warning lights and general mechanical condition. Start the engine from cold where possible and complete a proper test drive before deciding whether to buy.

What documents should I check when buying a used car?

Check the V5C registration certificate and compare its vehicle details with the car itself. Review available service records, maintenance invoices and other supporting paperwork. The VIN, registration, mileage history and seller's description should tell a consistent story.

How do I check a used car's MOT history?

Review the vehicle's previous MOT records rather than looking only at the latest pass. Check recorded mileage, previous failures, advisories and defects that appear repeatedly. Recurring tyre, brake, suspension, corrosion or leak issues can reveal areas that deserve closer inspection.

Does a fresh MOT mean a used car is good?

No. A fresh MOT is useful, but it is not a complete mechanical inspection or service history. A vehicle can have a valid MOT and still have clutch wear, gearbox problems, poor servicing, timing-system noise, cooling-system faults or other developing repair costs.

Should I start a used car from cold before buying it?

Yes, where practical. A cold start can reveal slow cranking, start-up rattles, rough idle, smoke and other symptoms that may become less obvious once the engine has warmed up. If the engine is already warm when you arrive, ask why it has been run.

What should I look for during a used car test drive?

Check clutch take-up or automatic gearbox engagement, normal gear changes, steering, suspension, braking, acceleration, vibration, engine temperature and dashboard warning lights. Listen for knocks, humming, grinding, rattling or other noises that change with road speed, engine speed or braking.

What mileage is too high for a used car?

Mileage should not be judged on its own. A higher-mileage car with strong maintenance history and good condition can be a better purchase than a lower-mileage vehicle that has been neglected. Consider age, servicing, use, condition, MOT history and upcoming repairs together.

Is it safe to buy a used car with MOT advisories?

An advisory does not automatically mean you should reject the vehicle. Find out whether the issue is still present, how serious it is now and what it may cost to repair. Repeated or worsening advisories deserve more attention than a minor issue that has already been repaired.

What are the biggest red flags when buying a used car?

Major red flags include vehicle identity or paperwork that does not match, unexplained mileage, serious overheating, major engine or gearbox symptoms, important warning lights with no diagnosis, severe corrosion, suspicious history and a seller who prevents reasonable inspection or test-drive checks.

When should I walk away from a used car?

Walk away when there is too much unresolved risk. Identity problems, suspicious history, serious safety defects, major overheating, severe engine or gearbox symptoms, unexplained warning lights or a seller who refuses reasonable checks are all good reasons to stop the purchase.

Final Thoughts

Buying the Right Used Car Is About Reducing Risk

The aim of a used-car inspection is not to find a perfect vehicle. Almost every used car will show some age, wear or maintenance needs. The important part is understanding what you are buying before the money changes hands.

Start with the vehicle's history and paperwork. Inspect the bodywork, tyres, engine bay and interior carefully. See the engine start from cold where possible. Test drive the vehicle properly and pay attention to the clutch or gearbox, steering, suspension, brakes, acceleration, temperature, vibration, warning lights and unusual noises.

Minor wear can often be negotiated into the price and known maintenance can be budgeted for. Be much more cautious when there is uncertainty around vehicle identity, serious safety problems, overheating, major engine or gearbox symptoms, suspicious history or a seller who prevents reasonable checks.
