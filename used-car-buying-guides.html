Start here

Start here before buying a used car

A good used car should make sense in every area: the advert, the seller’s answers, the MOT history, the service history, the mileage, the paperwork, the cold start, the test drive and the final price. If one part of the story does not add up, slow down before paying a deposit.

Many poor used car purchases happen because the buyer focuses on the wrong things. Clean paint, low mileage, a fresh valet and a recent MOT can make a car look safe, but they do not always show whether the car has been maintained properly.

Use this hub as your starting point. Begin with the inspection checklist, then check MOT history, service records, mileage, accident repair signs, warning lights and test-drive behaviour. If you are buying your first car, also check insurance, running costs and likely repair costs before deciding.

You can also browse our complete Guides section for more UK car advice covering maintenance, MOT tests, diagnostics, warning lights and repair costs.

Mechanic-style rule:

Do not inspect a used car looking for reasons to buy it. Inspect it looking for reasons not to buy it. A genuinely good car should still look good after proper checks.

Risk assessment

Used car risk assessment framework

Professional buyers do not judge a used car on mileage alone. They assess the overall risk profile of the vehicle using service history, MOT history, ownership records, condition, seller behaviour and test drive results.

Use this framework before making an offer. A car does not need to be perfect, but the price should match the risk. A cheap car with several high-risk signs can quickly become more expensive than a better-maintained car.

Area Low Risk Medium Risk High Risk
MOT history Clean history with normal wear Occasional advisories Repeated failures, rust or safety advisories
Service history Full and believable records Some gaps or missing invoices No records or unclear servicing
Mileage Consistent yearly pattern High but believable mileage Mileage gaps or suspicious changes
Seller Transparent and patient Limited details but cooperative Rushes you or avoids questions
Test drive Smooth, quiet and stable Minor issues to investigate Warning lights, smoke, overheating or knocks
Bodywork Consistent paint and panel gaps Minor cosmetic repairs Signs of poor accident repair or flood damage
Decision guide

Should you buy this used car?

A used car buying decision should be based on evidence, not excitement. Use the checks below to decide whether the car is worth buying, worth negotiating on or worth walking away from.

Buy

MOT history is sensible, service records are believable, paperwork matches, test drive is smooth and price reflects condition.

Negotiate

The car is fundamentally sound but needs tyres, brakes, servicing, battery work or minor repairs that should affect the price.

Walk away

Paperwork is missing, VIN does not match, seller avoids checks, warning lights stay on, smoke appears or serious faults are hidden.

Simple rule:

If two or more major areas raise concerns, slow down and investigate properly before committing to the purchase.

Buyer checklist

Used car buying checklist summary

Essential before viewing

  • ✓Check the MOT history before arranging a viewing.
  • ✓Check the mileage pattern and look for gaps.
  • ✓Ask about service history, invoices and timing belt or chain work.
  • ✓Ask if the engine will be cold when you arrive.
  • ✓Check insurance cost before travelling.

Essential when viewing

  • ✓Check V5C, VIN, registration and seller details.
  • ✓Look for warning lights before and after starting.
  • ✓Check tyres, leaks, bodywork and accident repair signs.
  • ✓Cold start the engine and listen carefully.
  • ✓Take a proper test drive on different roads.
Walk away warning:

Walk away if the seller avoids paperwork, refuses inspection, rushes you, hides faults, blocks a proper test drive, or says warning lights “only need resetting” without proof.

Red flags

10 used car red flags that should make you walk away

Some problems are not worth negotiating over. If the seller cannot explain the issue clearly or the car shows several red flags together, it is usually safer to walk away.

  • !VIN or registration details do not match: this is a serious paperwork warning.
  • !Seller refuses proper inspection: a genuine seller should allow reasonable checks.
  • !Multiple warning lights stay on: this may indicate expensive diagnostic faults.
  • !Heavy smoke from cold start: smoke can point to engine, turbo, injector or coolant issues.
  • !Coolant level keeps dropping: possible leak, overheating or head gasket risk.
  • !Evidence of clocked mileage: worn interior, odd MOT mileage or missing history.
  • !Repeated corrosion advisories: rust can become expensive very quickly.
  • !Fresh paint hiding poor repairs: check panel gaps, overspray and colour mismatch.
  • !No service records at all: price must reflect the higher risk.
  • !Pressure selling: rushing, deposit pressure or “another buyer is coming” tactics are warning signs.

Test drive checks

What to watch for on a used car test drive

A test drive often reveals problems you will not spot while the car is parked. Listen for knocking, whining, grinding, clutch judder, steering shake, brake vibration, hesitation, smoke, overheating or dashboard warning lights.

  • ✓Check the car from cold if possible.
  • ✓Make sure it starts cleanly without smoke or warning lights staying on.
  • ✓Test braking, steering, clutch bite, gear changes and acceleration.
  • ✓Listen over bumps for suspension knocks or rattles.
  • ✓Check for overheating, smells, smoke or power loss.

Negotiation

Using faults, advisories and service history to negotiate price

Most used cars need something. The key is identifying realistic repair costs and using them fairly during negotiation. Do not negotiate based on guesses alone; use visible faults, MOT advisories, missing service records and test-drive symptoms.

Issue found Typical UK guide cost Negotiation impact Buyer action
Tyres near limit £70–£180 per tyre Medium Price replacement into offer
Brake pads or discs worn £100–£500+ Medium Ask when last replaced
Battery weak £80–£250 Low–Medium Check starting and charging
Service overdue £150–£400+ Medium Budget for immediate service
Suspension knock £150–£600+ High Inspect before buying
Oil or coolant leak £80–£800+ High Trace source before paying
Corrosion advisory £200–£2,000+ Very High Inspect properly or walk away
Negotiation rule:

If the seller refuses to discuss clear repair costs, do not force the deal. There will always be another car.

First car advice

Buying your first used car

A first car should be easy to insure, simple to maintain, affordable to repair and safe enough for everyday driving. Do not buy only because the car looks good or has a cheap asking price.

Look for clean service history, sensible mileage, good tyres, no warning lights, long MOT, low insurance group and a test drive that feels smooth. Avoid cars with hidden accident damage, repeated MOT failures or sellers who rush you.

Workshop experience

Workshop experience: common used car buying mistakes

In real used car checks, the biggest mistakes buyers make are focusing on mileage and appearance while ignoring service history, warning lights, tyre wear patterns, MOT history and evidence of poor repairs. Some of the cleanest-looking cars can still hide expensive faults.

A fresh MOT is helpful, but it is not a full mechanical inspection. The MOT test does not tell you whether the clutch is nearly worn out, whether the timing chain is noisy, whether the car has poor service history, or whether the battery and charging system are weak.

The best approach is to check the car in layers: paperwork first, MOT history second, bodywork and tyres third, then cold start, test drive and final price negotiation. If something feels rushed, hidden or unclear, walk away.

Mechanic tips

Best mechanic-style advice before buying a used car

  • ✓Check the MOT history before viewing the car, not after.
  • ✓View the car in daylight and avoid rushed evening viewings.
  • ✓Start the engine from cold if possible.
  • ✓Do not ignore warning lights, smoke, coolant smells, oil leaks or poor starting.
  • ✓Check tyres carefully because uneven wear can reveal suspension or alignment issues.
  • ✓Never accept “it only needs resetting” without proof.
  • ✓Walk away if the seller avoids paperwork, inspection or a proper test drive.

FAQs

Used car buying questions

Common questions UK drivers ask before buying a used car.

What should I check before buying a used car in the UK?

Check MOT history, service history, V5C, VIN, mileage records, warning lights, tyre condition, brakes, suspension, accident repair signs, fluid leaks and how the car behaves on a proper test drive.

Is a fresh MOT enough when buying a used car?

No. A fresh MOT only shows the car met minimum MOT standards at the time of inspection. It does not prove the car has been serviced properly, has no hidden faults or will not need repairs soon.

Should I buy a used car with MOT advisories?

It depends on the advisory. Minor advisories may be manageable, but rust, suspension wear, tyres, brakes and leaks should be priced properly.

Is 100,000 miles too much for a used car?

Not automatically. A well-maintained 100,000-mile car can be better than a neglected low-mileage car.

How can I check if a used car is stolen or written off?

Check the V5C, VIN, plates, MOT history and use a vehicle history check before buying.

What should make me walk away from a used car?

Walk away from missing paperwork, pressure selling, warning lights, hidden damage, mileage doubts, unpaid finance risk or sellers who refuse checks.

Can a car pass an MOT and still have problems?

Yes. An MOT does not fully check clutch wear, service quality, timing chain noise, future repair risk or hidden accident damage.

Is no service history a deal-breaker?

Not always, but it increases risk and should affect the price. Missing history is more serious on complex, high-mileage or diesel cars.

How much should I keep aside after buying a used car?

Keep money aside for servicing, tyres, brakes, battery replacement, MOT work and unexpected repairs. A cheap used car can become expensive quickly if maintenance has been ignored.

Should I buy from a dealer or private seller?

Dealers may offer more legal protection and preparation, while private sellers may be cheaper. Either way, check paperwork, MOT history, service history, vehicle condition and test drive behaviour before buying.
