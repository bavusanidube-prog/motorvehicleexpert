How Long Can You Drive With an MOT Advisory?

There is no fixed number of days or miles that applies to every MOT advisory. Some conditions can be monitored while the vehicle remains roadworthy, while safety-related deterioration involving tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, corrosion or other critical components may need much faster attention. This guide explains how to judge repair urgency properly rather than simply waiting for the next MOT.

Quick Answer

How Long Can You Drive With an MOT Advisory?

There is no universal MOT advisory time limit. An advisory is not a countdown measured in days or miles. It identifies a condition that could become more serious in the future and should be monitored and repaired if necessary.

If the vehicle passed its MOT and the item remains only an advisory, the advisory itself does not prohibit you from driving. But you are still responsible for making sure the car remains roadworthy every time it is used.

This is why two vehicles carrying apparently similar advisories can require completely different decisions. A slowly developing non-critical condition may reasonably be monitored, whereas a tyre close to its legal limit, deteriorating brake component, significant steering or suspension concern, or progressing structural corrosion may justify much earlier inspection.

No Fixed Limit

Do not count days

MOT rules do not give every advisory a fixed mileage or number of days before repair.

Current Condition

Roadworthiness comes first

What matters while driving is whether the vehicle remains safe, legal and roadworthy now.

Repair Priority

Safety defects come first

Tyres, brakes, steering, suspension and structural defects deserve greater urgency than low-risk deterioration.

Use these four questions instead of asking for a mileage limit

1. What was advised?

Read the exact MOT wording and identify the component and condition.

2. Is it safety-critical?

Give greater priority to anything affecting braking, steering, grip, suspension or structural strength.

3. Is it deteriorating?

New noise, vibration, leakage, movement or rapid wear means the original advisory should be reassessed.

4. Is it roadworthy now?

Never use an older MOT pass as proof that a vehicle which has since deteriorated is still safe to drive.

Advisory does not mean Minor, Major or Dangerous

These are different MOT outcomes. An Advisory is something that could become more serious and should be monitored or repaired if necessary. A Minor defect still passes but should be repaired as soon as possible. Major and Dangerous defects result in an MOT failure.

Do not wait for the next MOT if the car deteriorates

If braking, steering, handling, tyre condition, structural integrity, visibility or another safety-critical area becomes unsafe, arrange inspection or repair based on the vehicle's present condition — not the date of its next MOT.

Mechanic-style rule

Instead of asking “How many miles have I got left?”, ask “How much serviceable condition is left, how quickly is it deteriorating and what happens if it fails?” That produces a much safer repair decision.

Advisory Meaning

What Does an MOT Advisory Actually Mean?

An MOT advisory records a condition that did not result in an MOT failure at the time of the test but could become more serious in the future. The correct response is to monitor the condition and repair it when necessary rather than assuming it can automatically be ignored until the next MOT.

The important words are at the time of the test. An MOT tester assesses the vehicle as it is presented on that day. If the condition later worsens, the earlier advisory does not permanently protect the vehicle from becoming unsafe, illegal or capable of failing another MOT.

Advisory

Monitor and repair if necessary

The condition could become more serious in the future but has not prevented the vehicle from passing the MOT.

Minor

Pass — repair soon

A Minor defect does not significantly affect vehicle safety or the environment, but it should still be repaired as soon as possible.

Major

MOT failure

A Major defect may affect vehicle safety, put other road users at risk or have an environmental impact. It results in an MOT failure and requires immediate repair.

Dangerous

Do not drive

A Dangerous defect presents a direct and immediate road-safety risk or serious environmental impact. The vehicle fails and should not be driven until repaired.

An Advisory and a Minor defect are not the same thing

Both can appear on a passing MOT result, but they have different meanings. An Advisory identifies a developing condition that should be monitored and repaired if necessary. A Minor defect is an identified defect which should be repaired as soon as possible.

Why advisories are useful

Advisories give the owner an opportunity to act before a developing defect reaches the rejection threshold. A worn suspension bush, corroding brake pipe, tyre approaching its legal limit or structural corrosion can be considerably easier to deal with while it is still at an early stage.

Early warning

The tester has identified deterioration that deserves future attention even though the vehicle has passed.

Maintenance planning

Advisories help you decide what needs inspection, budgeting or preventive repair before it develops further.

MOT history evidence

Repeated advisories can reveal whether a developing condition has been left unresolved over successive MOT tests.

Mechanic insight

Do not judge urgency from the word “advisory” alone. Read the complete defect description. A slight suspension bush wear advisory and a tyre very close to its legal tread limit may both say “advisory”, but the practical time available before repair can be very different.

Driving Time

Is There a Time Limit for Driving With an MOT Advisory?

No single statutory time limit applies to every MOT advisory. You should not interpret an advisory as meaning you have seven days, thirty days, six months or a guaranteed number of miles before repair becomes necessary.

The usable time remaining depends on the component, the amount of deterioration already present, how the vehicle is being used and how quickly the condition changes after the MOT.

Condition

How worn is it now?

A defect only just beginning to deteriorate may offer more monitoring time than a component already close to a legal or safety threshold.

Usage

How is the car being driven?

High mileage, heavy loads, poor roads, repeated short journeys, winter salt and hard braking can accelerate different types of wear.

Progression

Is it getting worse?

New symptoms or visible deterioration can turn a condition that was reasonable to monitor into one that requires prompt inspection.

Why mileage estimates can be misleading

A statement such as “you have another 5,000 miles” can create false confidence unless it is based on an actual inspection and measurement. Different components deteriorate in different ways. Tyre tread may wear predictably for a period and then accelerate because of alignment or pressure problems, while corrosion can be considerably worse on hidden surfaces than it appears externally.

Question Better approach Why it matters
“How many months can I drive?” Check the current defect condition Calendar time alone does not show how quickly the part is deteriorating.
“How many miles are left?” Measure or inspect the affected component Remaining service life depends on wear, usage and the particular component.
“It passed, so can I leave it?” Decide whether monitoring or repair is appropriate Passing on test day does not guarantee continued roadworthiness.
“It has no symptoms, so is it fine?” Consider the MOT finding as well as symptoms Many safety-related defects can progress before the driver notices a clear symptom.
Never create an artificial deadline from the MOT expiry date

An advisory does not mean the component is guaranteed to remain serviceable until the certificate expires. If the condition reaches an unsafe or illegal state earlier, it needs attention earlier.

A useful monitoring interval is condition-based

After an advisory, inspect or measure the component often enough to know whether the deterioration is stable or progressing. Safety-critical systems justify closer monitoring than cosmetic or low-risk deterioration.

Roadworthiness

MOT Advisory vs Vehicle Roadworthiness

The MOT result and the vehicle's current roadworthiness are related but they are not identical. An MOT test assesses the vehicle at a particular point in time. Roadworthiness is an ongoing requirement while the vehicle is being used.

This distinction is crucial when deciding how long to continue driving after receiving an advisory. The fact that the vehicle passed the MOT does not mean every component is guaranteed to stay above the legal or safety threshold for the life of the certificate.

MOT Test

Snapshot on test day

The result records the condition identified during the MOT inspection at that point in time.

Roadworthiness

Ongoing responsibility

The car must continue to be safe and legally roadworthy when it is used after the MOT.

Deterioration

Conditions can change

Wear, damage, corrosion or leakage can worsen after the test and change the vehicle's current safety position.

Example: why an MOT pass is not permanent proof of condition

On MOT day

A tyre may be legal but recorded as worn close to the legal limit. The car can pass because the tyre still meets the required standard when inspected.

Several weeks later

Continued driving may wear the tread further. If it falls below the legal requirement, the old MOT pass does not make that tyre legal.

Roadworthiness should override your MOT calendar

The correct decision is therefore based on what the vehicle is like now. If a developing fault begins to affect braking, steering, tyre condition, stability, visibility, structural strength or another safety-related function, waiting for the next annual test is the wrong approach.

A valid MOT certificate is not a defence for an unsafe vehicle

Treat the MOT certificate as evidence that the vehicle met the test requirements when examined. Continue checking the vehicle afterwards and act if its condition deteriorates.

Think condition, not certificate

A vehicle can have a current MOT and still develop an unsafe tyre, brake fault, suspension failure, fluid leak or other problem afterwards. The current physical condition is what determines whether continued driving is sensible.

Legal Driving

Is It Legal To Drive With an MOT Advisory?

An advisory by itself does not make the vehicle illegal to drive. Advisories can appear on a passing MOT certificate. What matters after the test is whether the vehicle continues to satisfy the applicable legal requirements and remains roadworthy.

Advisory Only

Vehicle remains roadworthy

The advisory itself does not prohibit driving. Monitor the condition and arrange repair according to its severity and rate of deterioration.

Condition Worsens

Reassess immediately

If the advised item subsequently becomes unsafe or fails a legal requirement, you cannot rely on its previous advisory status.

Dangerous

Do not continue driving

A Dangerous MOT defect is a direct and immediate road-safety risk. The official MOT category says not to drive the vehicle until it has been repaired.

Advisory is not permission to ignore a legal limit

This is particularly important with components that have clear legal or safety limits. A tyre may have been advised while it was still above the tread requirement, for example, but continued wear can later put it below the legal threshold.

The same principle applies more broadly. A brake pipe recorded earlier as showing deterioration may later become excessively corroded, leak or approach failure. Suspension or structural corrosion can also progress from a developing concern into a condition that meets Major or Dangerous MOT criteria.

Do not confuse “passed the MOT” with “safe to keep driving regardless of deterioration”

If the car develops a serious braking problem, dangerous tyre, unstable steering, severe suspension defect or structural weakness after the MOT, stop treating the issue as an advisory management question. It has become a current safety question.

When should you stop and arrange inspection?

Braking changes

Weak braking, major pulling, grinding, a sudden pedal change or evidence of brake fluid loss requires prompt attention.

Steering or stability changes

Significant looseness, instability, severe knocking or a sudden change in vehicle control should not be treated as something to monitor casually.

Visible serious deterioration

An illegal or damaged tyre, substantial fluid leakage, structural weakness or another obviously unsafe condition warrants inspection before continued normal use.

Practical rule

If you are deciding whether a known advisory is still acceptable to drive with, ask whether you would be comfortable presenting the vehicle for a safety inspection in its present condition. If the answer is uncertain, arrange inspection rather than guessing.

Repair Priority

How To Prioritise an MOT Advisory Without Guessing

The most useful way to decide repair urgency is to combine safety consequence, current severity, deterioration rate and remaining serviceable condition. That is more reliable than giving every advisory the same deadline.

1. Identify the exact defect

Read the MOT wording carefully and identify the specific component, location and type of deterioration.

2. Assess consequence

Ask what could happen if the component deteriorates further: loss of grip, braking, steering, stability, structure or merely inconvenience.

3. Measure condition

Where possible, use actual evidence such as tread depth, remaining friction material, component play, corrosion extent, leakage or physical inspection.

4. Decide action

Repair immediately, arrange prompt inspection, schedule planned repair or monitor the condition according to the evidence.

Practical MOT advisory priority system

Priority 1

Inspect urgently

Use this level where deterioration could quickly affect braking, steering, grip, structural strength or vehicle control, or where symptoms suggest the original condition has worsened.

Examples can include a tyre close to its legal limit, progressing brake pipe corrosion, significant braking concerns or serious structural deterioration.

Priority 2

Plan repair soon

Use this level for established wear or deterioration that is currently serviceable but is likely to progress and create a future repair or MOT failure.

Examples can include some brake wear, suspension bush wear, developing exhaust corrosion or a monitored leak depending on its actual severity.

Priority 3

Monitor with evidence

Suitable only where the condition is genuinely low risk, stable and not close to a legal or safety threshold.

Monitoring means checking the condition periodically — not forgetting about it until the next MOT.

What you find Practical response Why
New safety-related symptoms Inspect before continued normal driving The condition may have progressed beyond what was recorded at the MOT.
Component close to a legal or rejection limit Repair or recheck promptly Little serviceable margin may remain.
Safety-critical deterioration but no symptoms Arrange inspection and plan repair Absence of symptoms does not prove the condition is stable.
Low-risk condition clearly stable Monitor and schedule repair if necessary Some advisories can reasonably be managed rather than repaired immediately.
Condition unknown or wording unclear Ask a competent garage to inspect it Guessing remaining life from the MOT wording alone can be misleading.

Safety consequence matters more than repair price

A cheap repair can be urgent and an expensive repair can sometimes be planned. Repair priority should therefore be based first on safety, legality and deterioration — not simply the amount the garage is likely to charge.

Why corrosion deserves component-specific assessment

The MOT inspection manual makes clear that the safety significance of corrosion depends on both its extent and the function of the affected area. Corrosion in an important load-bearing section can be far more significant than more extensive corrosion on a non-structural panel.

Never upgrade an advisory into your own diagnosis

The MOT wording tells you what the tester recorded; it does not always tell you remaining component life. Where the consequences of failure are serious, inspect the actual component before deciding how long to continue driving.

Best repair-priority rule

Prioritise the item most capable of making the vehicle unsafe first, then the item most likely to cross a legal or MOT threshold, then developing faults that could become substantially more expensive if left unattended.

Tyre Advisories

How Long Can You Drive With a Tyre MOT Advisory?

A tyre advisory deserves close attention because the remaining margin between a legal tyre and an illegal tyre can sometimes be relatively small. There is no fixed mileage that applies after a tyre advisory. The correct decision depends on measured tread depth, tyre damage, wear pattern, inflation pressure, vehicle use and how quickly the tyre is continuing to wear.

For most cars and light vans, tyre tread must be at least 1.6 mm across the central three-quarters of the tread around the entire circumference. A tyre that was legal when tested can therefore become illegal before the next MOT if it continues to wear.

Measure

Check actual tread depth

Do not estimate remaining tyre life from the advisory alone. Measure tread depth at several points around the tyre and across the relevant tread area.

Inspect

Look beyond tread depth

Check for cuts, bulges, exposed cords, cracking, abnormal shoulder wear and other damage that may change the safety decision.

Diagnose

Find the cause of uneven wear

Alignment, incorrect pressure, suspension wear or damaged components can accelerate tyre wear and shorten the time available before replacement.

Do not use the next MOT date as the tyre replacement date

If an advised tyre reaches the legal limit, becomes damaged or otherwise unsafe before the next MOT, it needs attention then. The earlier MOT pass does not extend the legal life of the tyre.

Mechanic-style tyre rule

Measure, inspect and monitor. A tyre advisory becomes much more useful when you know the actual remaining tread rather than simply knowing that the tester recorded wear.

Brake Advisories

How Long Can You Drive With a Brake MOT Advisory?

Brake advisories should be judged by the exact component and remaining condition rather than by a fixed time or mileage. Brake pads, discs, rigid pipes, flexible hoses and other braking components have different MOT thresholds and different failure consequences.

Because braking is safety-critical, an advisory involving the braking system deserves a higher repair priority than many cosmetic or low-consequence defects. If braking performance, pedal feel or component condition changes, the original advisory should be reassessed promptly.

Brake Pads

Remaining material matters

Pads that are approaching their wear limit should be monitored or replaced based on their measured condition and wear rate. Do not wait for grinding before authorising repair.

Brake Discs

Condition matters, not one number alone

Disc condition should be assessed for significant wear, corrosion, cracking, contamination, security and overall serviceability.

Brake Pipes

Corrosion can become serious

Rigid brake pipe corrosion deserves careful inspection because substantial deterioration can progress towards leakage or failure of the hydraulic system.

Important brake-disc distinction

The MOT manual states that a brake disc must be significantly worn before rejection. Being below a manufacturer's recommended minimum thickness is not, by itself, an automatic MOT reason for rejection. That does not mean the manufacturer's service limit should be ignored when deciding whether the disc should be replaced.

Stop treating it as a routine advisory if braking changes

Grinding, a substantial change in pedal feel, fluid loss, severe pulling or obviously reduced braking performance requires prompt inspection. The issue may have progressed beyond the condition seen during the previous MOT.

Suspension Advisories

How Long Can You Drive With a Suspension MOT Advisory?

Suspension advisories vary considerably in urgency because “suspension” covers springs, shock absorbers, bushes, joints, arms, rods, struts, subframes and other components. The exact wording of the advisory therefore matters more than the category name alone.

A developing bush or joint wear issue may remain serviceable while being monitored, whereas increasing movement, a damaged spring, insecure component, severe corrosion or loss of damping can require much faster attention.

Bushes & Joints

Monitor movement

Wear should be assessed for actual play and whether it is progressing. Excessive wear reaches the Major defect threshold, while a joint or bearing likely to become detached is Dangerous.

Springs & Arms

Check security and integrity

Fracture, severe corrosion or insecure suspension components deserve prompt assessment because they can affect wheel location and vehicle control.

Shock Absorbers

Leakage severity matters

A light fluid film is not the same as severe leakage. Condition, damping performance, attachment and bush wear all affect the MOT and repair decision.

Shock absorber advisories need careful interpretation

The current MOT manual specifically states that slight seepage causing a film of fluid on a shock absorber is not a reason for rejection. That is very different from a shock absorber showing severe leakage or failing to function properly.

Shock absorber condition MOT position Practical meaning
Slight seepage forming a film Not a reason for rejection Monitor the condition and check whether leakage progresses.

Severe leakage or damaged so it does not function Major Damping performance or component condition has become unacceptable.
Negligible damping effect Major The shock absorber is effectively no longer controlling suspension movement adequately.

Increasing suspension symptoms change the decision

Strong knocking, instability, excessive movement, steering changes or obvious deterioration should trigger inspection even if the previous MOT wording was only advisory.

Corrosion Advisories

How Long Can You Drive With a Structural Corrosion Advisory?

Structural corrosion cannot be given a sensible universal driving period because the safety significance depends on both how much corrosion exists and where it is located. A relatively small weakened area can matter greatly if it is part of an important load-bearing structure or prescribed area.

This is why describing rust only as “light”, “moderate” or “heavy” is not enough. A mechanic or tester must consider what the affected section actually does and whether its strength or continuity is being compromised.

Location

Where is the rust?

Corrosion near suspension, steering, braking or restraint mountings can be much more important than similar-looking rust on a non-structural panel.

Strength

Is load-bearing capacity affected?

Structural integrity matters more than appearance. Perforation, substantial thinning or loss of continuity can change repair urgency significantly.

Progression

Is the corrosion spreading?

Road salt, trapped moisture and failed coatings can allow rust to progress between MOT tests, including in areas that are difficult to inspect externally.

Prescribed areas are particularly important

MOT corrosion assessment gives particular attention to important load-bearing parts and supporting structure around mountings for testable items such as brakes, steering, suspension and restraint systems. A comparatively small corroded area can therefore have major safety significance.

Rust can be worse than the visible surface suggests

Box sections, subframes, sills and other structures can corrode internally or behind covers. If the MOT history repeatedly records structural corrosion, arrange proper inspection rather than assuming the visible surface tells the whole story.

Oil Leak Advisories

How Long Can You Drive With an Oil Leak MOT Advisory?

An oil leak advisory should be judged by the amount of fluid being lost, the source of the leak, whether the level is dropping between checks and whether escaping oil is contaminating hot, rotating or safety-critical components.

A small developing seepage may remain manageable while its level and source are monitored. A leak that becomes substantially worse, causes frequent topping-up, reaches the road surface or threatens lubrication of the engine or gearbox requires a different response.

Monitor

Check fluid level

A leak is much easier to manage safely when you know whether the fluid level is stable or falling between checks.

Diagnose

Identify the source

Engine oil, gearbox oil, power steering fluid and other fluids have different consequences and repair priorities.

Escalate

Watch for rapid loss

A leak that suddenly becomes heavier, creates warning lights or causes a low fluid level should not be treated as an old routine advisory.

Signs the leak needs faster attention

  • Fluid level drops noticeably between regular checks.
  • Fresh oil is appearing regularly underneath the vehicle.
  • Oil reaches the exhaust or another hot component.
  • Oil contaminates brakes, belts, tyres or other components.
  • A warning light or low-pressure warning appears.
  • Burning oil smell or visible smoke develops.
  • The leak becomes noticeably heavier than when the MOT was carried out.
Do not solve an oil leak by topping up indefinitely

Maintaining the correct level can protect the engine temporarily, but topping up does not repair the source. A leak can accelerate or contaminate another component, so persistent loss should be diagnosed.

Exhaust Advisories

How Long Can You Drive With an Exhaust Corrosion Advisory?

Exhaust corrosion itself is not automatically an MOT failure. Repair urgency depends on whether deterioration affects security, creates a major leak, increases noise, affects emissions or allows exhaust gases to enter the passenger compartment.

An externally corroded silencer or pipe may remain serviceable for a period, but thin metal can deteriorate quickly once perforation begins. Listen for changing exhaust noise and inspect the mounting points as well as the pipe or silencer body.

Corroded

Inspect remaining integrity

Surface corrosion and structural weakening are not the same. Check whether the metal remains secure and serviceable.

Blowing

Listen for leakage

Increasing exhaust noise, ticking or blowing can indicate that corrosion has progressed into a leak.

Security

Check mountings and joints

A pipe or silencer becoming loose is a different safety issue from corrosion that remains securely contained.

Warning Symptoms

Signs an MOT Advisory May Have Become More Serious

The MOT certificate cannot tell you how the vehicle will change after the test. Symptoms developing later are therefore one of the clearest reasons to reassess an advisory rather than continuing to rely on its original wording.

Some faults remain quiet until they are advanced, so absence of symptoms is not proof that every advisory is harmless. But when symptoms do appear, they should be treated as new evidence about the vehicle's current condition.

Braking

Braking feels different

  • Grinding or scraping.
  • Noticeable pulling under braking.
  • Significant pedal change.
  • Brake fluid loss.
Steering & Suspension

Control feels different

  • Strong knocking or clunking.
  • Loose or vague steering.
  • Vehicle instability.
  • Excessive bouncing or poor damping.
Tyres

Wear is progressing

  • Tread approaching the legal minimum.
  • Rapid or uneven wear.
  • Cuts, bulges or visible damage.
  • Repeated pressure loss.
Corrosion

Structure looks weaker

  • Rust spreading significantly.
  • Flaking metal or perforation.
  • Weak or distorted mounting areas.
  • Previously repaired areas deteriorating.
Fluids

Leakage increases

  • Larger patches beneath the vehicle.
  • Fluid level falling more quickly.
  • Warning lights appearing.
  • Burning smell or smoke.
Exhaust

Noise or fumes appear

  • Exhaust suddenly becomes louder.
  • Blowing or ticking develops.
  • System hangs lower or becomes loose.
  • Exhaust fumes are noticeable inside the car.

What should you do when symptoms appear?

1. Stop relying on the old MOT wording

The condition has changed since the inspection, so reassess the vehicle as it is now.

2. Identify the affected system

Determine whether the symptom relates to tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, structure, fluid loss or exhaust.

3. Judge immediate safety

If braking, steering, tyre integrity or vehicle stability may be compromised, avoid unnecessary driving.

4. Arrange inspection

Have a competent garage inspect the actual component and decide whether repair is required before further normal use.

Stop driving if the vehicle appears dangerous

A current MOT does not make an unsafe car safe. GOV.UK states that drivers are responsible for making sure their vehicle is always roadworthy, and a vehicle can be unsafe even while it has a current MOT certificate.

The most important lesson from an advisory

An MOT advisory is best treated as a maintenance warning, not a permission slip to continue driving until the next annual test. Monitor the actual component and move the repair forward whenever safety margin, legality or deterioration justifies it.

MOT Rules

When Does an MOT Advisory Become an MOT Failure?

An MOT advisory does not have a fixed expiry period. It becomes more important when the underlying condition deteriorates far enough to meet a defined MOT defect threshold, becomes illegal between tests or makes the vehicle unsafe to use.

The current MOT system separates findings into Advisory, Minor, Major and Dangerous outcomes. Understanding these categories prevents a common mistake: assuming that anything written on an MOT certificate is simply another type of advisory.

Advisory

Pass

The condition could become more serious in the future. Monitor it and repair it if necessary.

Minor

Pass

The defect does not have a significant effect on safety or the environment, but it should be repaired as soon as possible.

Major

Fail

The defect may affect safety, put other road users at risk or have an environmental impact. It should be repaired immediately.

Dangerous

Fail — do not drive

The defect presents a direct and immediate road-safety risk or serious environmental impact. The vehicle should not be driven until repaired.

MOT result Vehicle passes? Official action What it means for this guide
Advisory Yes Monitor and repair if necessary Decide urgency from the actual condition rather than an invented time allowance.
Minor Yes Repair as soon as possible Do not confuse a Minor defect with an Advisory.
Major No Repair immediately The defect has crossed an MOT failure threshold.
Dangerous No Do not drive until repaired This is no longer an advisory-management situation.

How an advisory can progress

1. Early deterioration

The tester notices wear, corrosion or another developing condition which does not currently justify failure.

2. Continued use

Mileage, age, weather, load and normal operation continue to affect the component after the MOT.

3. Condition worsens

Wear increases, corrosion spreads, leakage develops or component performance deteriorates.

4. Threshold is crossed

At a later inspection the same area may meet Minor, Major or Dangerous criteria depending on its severity.

An advisory does not automatically progress through every category

There is no required sequence of Advisory → Minor → Major → Dangerous. The category at any later MOT depends on the condition actually found and the specific inspection criteria for that component.

Do not wait for an MOT failure threshold before repairing a safety-critical fault

MOT rejection criteria are minimum inspection standards. Good maintenance means repairing safety-related deterioration when condition and risk justify it, not deliberately running every component until it becomes an MOT failure.

Failed MOT Rules

What If the Advisory Has Become a Major or Dangerous MOT Defect?

Once the vehicle has actually been tested and a defect is recorded as Major or Dangerous, the vehicle has failed its MOT. The rules for taking a failed vehicle away are different from the rules for driving a vehicle which merely has advisories.

Current MOT Valid

No Dangerous defects

GOV.UK states that you can take a failed vehicle away if its existing MOT is still valid and no Dangerous problems were listed.

Dangerous Defect

Repair before driving

A vehicle with a Dangerous defect should not be driven until the dangerous condition has been repaired.

Roadworthiness

Still required

Even where the rules allow the failed vehicle to be taken away, it must still meet minimum roadworthiness standards.

A valid old MOT does not automatically make a failed vehicle roadworthy

The previous certificate may still be within its validity period, but the new test has provided current evidence about the vehicle. If the condition makes the vehicle unsafe or below minimum roadworthiness standards, do not rely on the earlier certificate as permission to continue driving normally.

If the MOT has already expired

GOV.UK allows an untaxed MOT situation to have specific journey exceptions, including driving to a pre-arranged MOT test or to have failed defects repaired where the relevant conditions are met. That does not remove the requirement for the vehicle to meet minimum roadworthiness standards.

MOT still valid

A failed vehicle may be taken away when the existing MOT remains valid and no Dangerous defect has been recorded, provided it remains roadworthy.

MOT expired

Permitted journeys can include travel to have failed defects repaired or to a pre-arranged MOT test, subject to the applicable rules.

Vehicle not roadworthy

Roadworthiness remains the controlling safety requirement. Recovery is the safer option where the vehicle cannot meet it.

Failed MOT and MOT expiry are separate issues

A vehicle can fail a new MOT while an earlier certificate is still within its validity period. Whether it can then be driven depends on the new defect result, the validity of the existing MOT and the vehicle's actual roadworthiness.

Insurance & Roadworthiness

Can Driving With an MOT Advisory Affect Insurance?

An MOT advisory does not automatically invalidate your car insurance. The more important questions are whether the vehicle remains roadworthy, whether you have answered the insurer's questions accurately and whether the condition of the vehicle later becomes relevant to an accident or claim.

This distinction matters because an advisory is simply part of the MOT record. It is not automatically the same as an unroadworthy vehicle, an undisclosed modification or a guaranteed reason for an insurer to refuse a claim.

Advisory

Not automatic invalidation

The mere existence of an MOT advisory does not automatically cancel or invalidate a motor insurance policy.

Disclosure

Answer questions accurately

For consumer insurance, you should take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation when answering an insurer's questions.

Policy Terms

Read your own policy

Notification requirements and vehicle-condition terms vary, so check the wording of your own insurance contract rather than relying on a blanket rule.

There is no blanket rule to volunteer every MOT advisory

Consumer insurance law places a duty on the consumer to take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation to the insurer. Whether reasonable care has been taken can depend on matters such as how clear and specific the insurer's questions were.

That is different from claiming that every MOT advisory must automatically be reported to every insurer. Instead, answer questions accurately and follow the notification requirements contained in your own policy.

Advisory and modification are different

An MOT advisory records the condition of something inspected on the vehicle. A modification is a change to the vehicle from its standard specification. Insurers may ask separate questions about modifications and those questions should be answered accurately.

Why roadworthiness can matter during a claim

If a previously advised defect deteriorates and the vehicle is involved in an accident, an insurer may investigate the vehicle's condition. The important issue is not simply that an advisory existed in the MOT history, but whether the actual defect and the vehicle's roadworthiness are relevant to the circumstances of the loss.

Tyre condition

A historic tyre advisory is different from a tyre that later becomes unsafe or illegal and is relevant to an accident.

Brake condition

An old brake advisory does not itself prove causation, but serious deterioration can become relevant where braking performance is part of the incident.

Maintenance evidence

Inspection reports, invoices and maintenance records can help establish what was checked or repaired after the MOT.

Do not assume a valid MOT settles an insurance roadworthiness question

GOV.UK makes clear that a vehicle can become unsafe even while it has a current MOT. Current condition therefore remains important after the test.

Unsure what your insurer requires?

Read the policy wording and the questions you were asked. If a requirement about known defects, vehicle condition or another relevant matter is unclear, ask the insurer directly and retain a record of the answer.

Repair Decisions

Should You Repair an MOT Advisory Now or Monitor It?

The best repair decision is based on evidence, not fear and not an arbitrary countdown. Some advisories justify immediate or prompt action, while others can reasonably be monitored when there is adequate serviceable margin and no developing safety concern.

A useful garage inspection should answer four questions: what is wrong, how severe is it now, how quickly is it likely to deteriorate and what are the consequences if it is left unresolved?

Higher Priority

Repair or inspect promptly

Appropriate where there is little remaining safety margin, rapid deterioration, new symptoms or a component whose failure could significantly affect control of the vehicle.

Planned Repair

Schedule before deterioration

Suitable for established wear that is currently serviceable but is likely to create a larger repair bill or future MOT failure if left indefinitely.

Monitor

Recheck the condition

Appropriate only where the defect is genuinely low risk, stable and has sufficient margin before a safety or legal threshold is reached.

Garage diagnostic workflow

1. Confirm

Locate the exact component and confirm that the MOT advisory accurately describes the condition still present.

2. Measure

Measure tread, friction material, play, corrosion, leakage or another relevant condition wherever practical.

3. Diagnose

Establish why the deterioration occurred so that replacing one worn component does not leave the underlying cause unresolved.

4. Prioritise

Decide whether the vehicle needs immediate repair, planned repair or documented monitoring.

What should you ask the garage?

Can you show me the defect?

Seeing the actual component makes an MOT advisory easier to understand and helps distinguish minor deterioration from a rapidly progressing fault.

How much margin remains?

Ask for useful measurements where possible rather than a vague statement that the component is simply “worn”.

What happens if I wait?

A good repair recommendation should explain the likely safety, legal, MOT and cost consequences of delaying the work.

Inspection result Sensible decision Reason
Unsafe or below a legal standard Repair before continued use The question is now roadworthiness, not advisory timing.
Close to a safety or rejection threshold Repair promptly Little usable margin remains before further deterioration creates a more serious problem.
Serviceable but clearly deteriorating Plan the repair Early intervention can prevent a failure or more expensive secondary damage.
Stable and low risk Monitor with a recheck plan Immediate replacement may not be necessary if adequate serviceable condition remains.
Condition cannot be confidently assessed Investigate further Uncertainty should not be converted into an invented mileage estimate.
Repair earlier where delay creates secondary damage

Waiting can sometimes turn a relatively small repair into a larger one. Worn brake pads can damage discs, poor suspension condition can accelerate tyre wear, an oil leak can contaminate other components and untreated corrosion can spread into a more difficult structural repair.

A cheap repair should not automatically be delayed

Wipers, bulbs and other inexpensive items may be simple to fix immediately. Repair priority should reflect safety and practical maintenance value, not just the price of the part.

Next MOT Risk

Will an MOT Advisory Become a Failure at the Next MOT?

It can, but it is not inevitable. An advisory records a condition that could become more serious. Whether it becomes a future MOT failure depends on what happens to that component before the next test.

Repairs, low mileage or slow deterioration may prevent the condition progressing. Continued wear, corrosion, leakage, damage or lack of maintenance can move it closer to — or beyond — an MOT rejection threshold.

Lower Risk

Repaired after the MOT

If the cause has been properly repaired and the component is now serviceable, the old advisory does not mean it must appear again.

Investigate

Same advisory repeats

Repeated wording over several MOTs can indicate that the underlying condition has remained unresolved.

Higher Risk

Advisory is getting worse

Increasing wear, corrosion, movement or leakage makes a future failure more likely and justifies earlier inspection.

Advisory patterns worth noticing

Repeated tyre advisories

Recurring tyre wear can point to normal replacement needs or an unresolved pressure, alignment or suspension issue.

Repeated corrosion

Structural or brake-pipe corrosion that returns year after year deserves close attention because corrosion normally progresses unless properly treated or repaired.

Growing advisory list

A rising number of suspension, brake, tyre and corrosion advisories can suggest increasing maintenance requirements as the vehicle ages.

Read the MOT history as a timeline

The official MOT history service allows you to review previous test results. Rather than looking only at the latest certificate, compare the wording and mileage between tests to see whether a condition first appeared recently, repeated for several years or eventually progressed to a failure.

1. Find first appearance

Identify when the advisory was first recorded.

2. Compare mileage

See how far the vehicle travelled between repeated findings.

3. Check progression

Look for wording that suggests increasing wear, deterioration or eventual failure.

4. Look for repair evidence

Ask for invoices or inspection records rather than assuming an advisory disappeared because it was repaired.

A missing advisory on the next MOT is useful but not complete repair evidence

The condition may have been repaired, may no longer have justified an advisory or may have been assessed differently at the later inspection. Service and repair invoices provide stronger evidence of what work was actually carried out.

Do not deliberately wait for a failure

A future MOT failure is not the only reason to repair an advised component. Safety, legality, breakdown risk, secondary damage and repair cost can all justify dealing with a problem before the next annual test.

Used Car Buying

Should You Buy a Car With MOT Advisories?

A used car with MOT advisories is not automatically a bad purchase. Many vehicles accumulate normal wear-related advisories as they age. The important questions are what the advisories involve, whether they repeat, whether the underlying defects have been repaired and how much future work is likely to be required.

Treat the MOT history as a maintenance timeline rather than simply checking whether the latest certificate says Pass. A car that passes every year but repeatedly carries the same brake, suspension, corrosion or tyre warnings may still need significant expenditure soon after purchase.

Lower Buying Risk

Minor isolated advisory

A single low-risk wear item with clear evidence of later repair is usually less concerning than a long history of unresolved safety-related defects.

Investigate

Repeated advisory

The same wording across several tests may indicate that the problem has been monitored rather than repaired. Ask what work, if any, has been completed.

Higher Buying Risk

Multiple safety advisories

Several tyre, brake, suspension, structural or brake-pipe advisories together can indicate substantial maintenance needs and should be priced before purchase.

Read the advisory history in sequence

1. Find the first advisory

Identify when the condition first appeared and record the mileage.

2. Look for repetition

Check whether the same component or area appears again at later MOT tests.

3. Look for escalation

See whether an advisory later became a Minor, Major or Dangerous defect.

4. Check repair evidence

Match MOT history against invoices, service records and inspection documentation.

A fresh MOT does not erase the vehicle's history

A recent pass is useful, but it should not stop you examining repeated corrosion, brake, tyre or suspension findings from earlier years. Those records can reveal how the car has been maintained and where future costs may appear.

Use repair costs during negotiation

If the car has legitimate wear-related advisories, obtain realistic repair estimates before agreeing the price. Tyres, brakes, suspension components, exhaust work and corrosion repairs can materially change whether the vehicle represents good value.

Frequently Asked Questions

How Long Can You Drive With an MOT Advisory? FAQs

These answers cover the most common questions UK motorists ask about MOT advisories, driving time, roadworthiness, repair urgency, insurance and future MOT failures.

How long can you drive with an MOT advisory?

There is no fixed number of days or miles that applies to every MOT advisory. An advisory means an issue could become more serious in the future, so how long you can continue driving depends on the actual defect, its current condition, how quickly it is deteriorating and whether the vehicle remains roadworthy.

Is it legal to drive with an MOT advisory?

An MOT advisory does not by itself prevent you from driving because advisories can be recorded on a vehicle that passes its MOT. However, the vehicle must remain roadworthy and comply with the law whenever it is driven.

Does an MOT advisory mean the car is safe until the next MOT?

No. An MOT result reflects the condition of the vehicle at the time of the test. A defect recorded as an advisory can deteriorate afterwards, so a current MOT certificate does not guarantee that the vehicle will remain roadworthy until the next test.

Can you ignore an MOT advisory until next year?

You should not automatically leave an advisory until the next MOT. Some conditions change slowly, while tyres, brakes, suspension components, corrosion and leaks can deteriorate enough to require repair much sooner.

Can an MOT advisory become an MOT failure?

Yes. A condition that was only advisory at one MOT can later deteriorate until it meets the criteria for a Minor, Major or Dangerous defect. Major and Dangerous defects result in an MOT failure.

Which MOT advisories should be checked first?

Advisories involving tyres, brakes, steering, suspension, brake pipes, structural corrosion, visibility or another safety-critical system should normally receive priority because deterioration can affect roadworthiness or vehicle control.

How long can you drive with a tyre advisory?

There is no safe universal mileage for a tyre advisory. Check the actual tread depth, damage, pressure and wear pattern. For most cars and light vans the legal minimum tread depth is 1.6 mm across the central three-quarters of the tread and around the entire circumference.

How long can you drive with a brake advisory?

Brake advisories should not be judged by a fixed number of miles. The brakes should be inspected according to the exact defect, remaining material, corrosion, leakage, braking performance and rate of wear because braking defects can become safety-critical.

How long can you drive with a suspension advisory?

Suspension advisories vary considerably. Minor deterioration may remain serviceable for some time, while increasing play, damaged components, poor damping or instability requires faster attention. Have the exact component inspected rather than relying on a fixed mileage estimate.

How long can you drive with a corrosion advisory?

There is no fixed driving period for a corrosion advisory. The risk depends on where the corrosion is located, how extensive it is and whether an important load-bearing or safety-related area is being weakened.

Can you drive with a shock absorber advisory?

It depends on the exact condition. Slight fluid seepage forming a film on a shock absorber is not itself an MOT rejection, while severe leakage, negligible damping or an insecure shock absorber can meet MOT failure criteria and requires more urgent attention.

Can you drive with an oil leak advisory?

A small monitored oil leak may not immediately make a vehicle unsafe, but the leak should be assessed for severity, oil level loss, contamination of other components, environmental impact and fire risk. A worsening or heavy leak should not be ignored.

Can you drive with an exhaust corrosion advisory?

Exhaust corrosion does not automatically mean the vehicle must stop being driven. The system should be checked for security, major leaks, excessive noise and fumes because deterioration into those conditions can result in an MOT failure or create a safety risk.

What symptoms mean an MOT advisory needs urgent attention?

Seek prompt inspection if you develop weak or abnormal braking, unstable steering, severe pulling, unusual suspension movement, rapidly losing tyre tread, warning lights, significant fluid loss, exhaust fumes in the cabin or any other symptom suggesting the vehicle may no longer be roadworthy.

Can MOT advisories affect insurance?

An MOT advisory does not automatically invalidate car insurance. The more important issues are whether the vehicle remains roadworthy, whether insurer questions have been answered accurately and whether a defect becomes relevant to an accident or claim.

Should I repair an MOT advisory before the next test?

Often yes, particularly where the defect is safety-related or clearly deteriorating. Repairing developing faults before the next MOT can reduce the risk of breakdown, unsafe condition, larger repair bills and a future MOT failure.

Should I buy a used car with MOT advisories?

A used car with advisories can still be a reasonable purchase, but check what the advisories involve, whether they repeat in the MOT history, whether repairs have been completed and whether expected costs are reflected in the purchase price.

What should I do after receiving an MOT advisory?

Read the exact advisory, identify the affected component, assess its current condition and repair urgency, watch for symptoms and keep inspection or repair evidence. Safety-critical or rapidly deteriorating defects should be dealt with sooner rather than waiting for the next MOT.
