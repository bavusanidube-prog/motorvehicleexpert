Quick Answer

What Causes a Car to Knock When Turning?

The most common causes of a knocking noise when turning are CV-joint wear, suspension bushes, ball joints, strut top mounts, track rod ends, anti-roll bar links, wheel bearings, steering components or loose wheel fittings. The exact cause depends heavily on when and how the noise occurs.

A repeated click or knock on full lock under power makes an outer CV joint more likely. A dull clunk over bumps or during weight transfer points more towards suspension bushes, joints or links. A knock that begins immediately after a pothole or wheel work deserves faster inspection because wheel, tyre, steering or suspension security may be involved.

Mechanic Diagnostic Method

How Mechanics Diagnose a Knocking Noise When Turning

A knocking noise when turning should be diagnosed by reproducing the exact condition that makes the sound appear and then changing one variable at a time. Steering angle, vehicle speed, acceleration, braking, road bumps and recent repair history all help narrow the fault.

The aim is to decide whether the knock is mainly coming from a driveshaft joint, steering component, suspension joint, wheel bearing, brake assembly or wheel fitting before parts are replaced.

1. Reproduce the noise

Confirm whether it happens on full lock, gentle steering, over bumps, while braking, during acceleration or only at parking speeds.

2. Identify the corner

Decide whether the sound seems to come from the front left, front right, rear or centre of the vehicle.

3. Compare steering angle

A noise that only appears near full lock points in a different direction from one that appears during small steering corrections.

4. Add acceleration

If the knock becomes a repeated click while power is applied through a tight turn, the outer CV joint becomes much more relevant.

5. Compare road bumps

If the noise is worse on uneven roads, inspect suspension links, bushes, ball joints and mountings for play.

6. Apply the brakes

A knock that changes under braking can involve suspension movement, brake hardware, wheel movement or loose components.

7. Inspect recent work

If the noise started after tyres, brakes, suspension or wheel work, check anything disturbed during the repair.

8. Confirm physical play

The final diagnosis should be supported by visible wear, abnormal movement, damaged rubber, loose fasteners or roughness in the suspected component.

The timing of the knock is more useful than the word “knock”

A knock only on full lock under power suggests a different system from a knock over bumps or a clunk that appears while braking and turning.

Why Turning Matters

Why Turning Changes the Load on Steering, Suspension and CV Joints

When a car turns, several forces change at the same time. The front wheels move through an angle, the suspension loads one side differently, driveshaft joints articulate, tyre contact patches change and steering joints move through their working range.

This is why a worn component may stay quiet while driving straight ahead but knock as soon as the steering is turned. The extra movement or load exposes clearance that is not obvious in straight-line driving.

Driveshaft Load

CV Joint Angle Increases

Outer CV joints operate at greater angles during tight steering, which is why worn joints often become noisy on full lock.

Suspension Load

Weight Transfers Side to Side

Bushes, ball joints and mounts are loaded differently as the vehicle changes direction.

Steering Load

Joints Move Through Their Range

Track rod ends, rack joints and top mounts move as the steering angle changes and can reveal play or binding.

Wheel Load

Bearing Load Changes

Cornering changes lateral force through the wheel bearing, which can alter humming, rumbling or roughness.

Brake Assembly

Components Shift Under Load

Loose pads, caliper hardware or shields can move slightly as steering and braking forces change.

Tyre Contact

Scrub Increases at Low Speed

Wide tyres, high steering angle and certain surfaces can create tyre scrub that should not be confused with a heavy metallic knock.

Driveshaft Cause

Outer CV Joint Knocking or Clicking When Turning

The outer CV joint allows the driveshaft to transfer power while the wheel is steering and moving with the suspension. When the joint wears, the internal tracks and bearings can develop clearance that becomes most noticeable at high steering angles.

Typical Sound

Repeated clicking, clacking or knocking from one front corner.

Typical Condition

Tight low-speed turns, especially while accelerating out of a parking space or junction.

Useful Visual Clue

A split CV boot with grease around the driveshaft, wheel or inner arch strongly supports CV-joint wear.

A CV joint usually repeats the noise

A worn outer CV joint often produces a rhythmic click or knock as the wheel rotates through a tight steering angle, rather than one isolated clunk.

Can Suspension Bushes Cause Knocking When Turning?

Yes. Suspension bushes isolate movement between metal components while controlling wheel position. As rubber deteriorates or separates from its sleeve, the suspension arm can move more than intended and produce a dull knock or clunk.

Knock During Weight Transfer

Worn bushes often knock when braking, accelerating or changing direction because the suspension arm shifts.

Uneven Tyre Wear

Excessive bush movement can alter wheel geometry and contribute to uneven tyre wear.

Steering Instability

Severe wear can make the vehicle feel vague or less stable during braking and cornering.

Full-Lock Diagnosis

Knocking or Clicking on Full Lock

A repeated knock or click at full steering lock is one of the strongest clues for outer CV-joint wear, particularly on front-wheel-drive vehicles.

The joint is operating at a large angle while also transferring drive torque, which exposes internal wear more clearly than straight-line driving.

  • ✓ Noise is worse while accelerating through a tight turn.
  • ✓ Repeated clicking or knocking comes from one front corner.
  • ✓ Grease is visible around the driveshaft or inner wheel arch.
  • ✓ The noise is most obvious in car parks or tight manoeuvres.
Full-lock noise is not automatically a CV joint

Top mounts, steering joints and tyre scrub can also become more noticeable near full lock. The pattern should be confirmed before a driveshaft is replaced.

Low-Speed Diagnosis

Low-Speed Knocking When Parking or Manoeuvring

Parking-speed manoeuvres place high steering angle and low-speed suspension load through the front of the vehicle. This can make worn joints and mountings easier to hear than at higher speeds.

Direction Clues

Knocking When Turning Left vs Turning Right

Turning direction can provide useful information because load shifts across the vehicle, but it does not reliably identify the failed side by itself.

Noise can travel through the subframe, suspension and body, and different faults respond differently to lateral load. Treat direction as one clue rather than a complete diagnosis.

Louder Turning Left

The right side of the vehicle generally carries more cornering load, but CV joints, steering joints and mounts can behave differently. Inspect both sides.

Louder Turning Right

The left side generally carries more cornering load, but the noisy side should still be confirmed physically.

Tell the garage exactly which direction changes the noise

Direction, speed, steering angle and whether power is being applied can significantly shorten diagnostic time.

Professional Diagnosis

Professional Diagnostic Workflow for Knocking When Turning

A good diagnosis should reproduce the knock before any parts are replaced. Steering and suspension noises can travel through the subframe, body and floor, so the component that sounds closest to the driver is not always the component actually causing the noise.

The technician should first establish whether the noise is controlled mainly by steering angle, road speed, acceleration, braking, suspension movement or a combination of these conditions. That behaviour determines which parts deserve inspection first.

1. Confirm the complaint

Ask when the knock occurs, which side it appears to come from, how long it has been present and whether anything happened shortly before it started.

2. Check recent history

Pothole impacts, kerb strikes, tyre changes, brake repairs and suspension work can significantly change the order of inspection.

3. Perform a controlled road test

Reproduce the knock using gentle turns, tight turns, braking, acceleration and suitable uneven road surfaces.

4. Compare full lock

Repeated clicking or knocking under power at high steering angle increases suspicion of an outer CV joint.

5. Compare suspension movement

A dull clunk over small bumps directs attention towards links, bushes, ball joints, arms and top mounts.

6. Compare braking load

If braking changes the knock, inspect suspension-arm movement, wheel security and brake components as well as steering joints.

7. Raise the vehicle

Inspect the wheel, tyre, brakes, steering joints, suspension joints, bushes, CV boots and wheel bearing.

8. Confirm before repair

The suspected component should explain both the physical wear found and the exact condition that produced the knock during the road test.

A correct diagnosis should explain why the noise happens

Finding a slightly worn component is not enough if its behaviour does not match the complaint. The best diagnosis links the road-test symptom directly to physical evidence.

Road-Test Procedure

How a Mechanic Road-Tests a Knocking Noise When Turning

The road test should reproduce the fault safely without unnecessarily loading a potentially damaged steering or suspension component. Each test condition should be changed separately so the technician knows which input affected the noise.

Test 1

Straight Road Baseline

Establish whether the vehicle knocks, vibrates, pulls or produces wheel-bearing noise while travelling straight ahead.

Test 2

Gentle Left and Right Turns

Compare the sound as lateral load transfers from one side of the vehicle to the other.

Test 3

Tight Low-Speed Turns

Useful for reproducing CV-joint clicks, top-mount noises and steering-related knocks.

Test 4

Gentle Acceleration While Turning

Applying drive torque helps reveal worn outer CV joints and excessive drivetrain movement.

Test 5

Braking While Turning

This changes suspension-arm, brake and wheel loads and can expose bush movement or insecure components.

Test 6

Uneven Road Surface

Small bumps can make anti-roll bar links, bushes, top mounts and ball-joint wear easier to reproduce.

Do not deliberately stress a vehicle with obvious steering or wheel insecurity

If the steering is severely loose, a wheel is visibly moving, the tyre is damaged or the vehicle became unstable after an impact, inspection should take priority over an extended road test.

Workshop Inspection

What a Mechanic Checks on the Ramp

Once the road test has narrowed the likely fault area, the vehicle should be inspected physically. The technician is looking for abnormal movement, damaged rubber, looseness, roughness, impact damage and anything that could allow a component to move under steering or suspension load.

Wheel and Tyre

Check tyre damage, wheel damage, correct seating, wheel fittings, clearance and evidence of impact.

Outer CV Joint

Inspect the boot for splits, grease loss and contamination, then check the joint for excessive wear where appropriate.

Driveshaft

Check joints, shaft condition and excessive movement that could produce clunks under changing drive load.

Ball Joint

Check for excessive play, damaged boots and movement outside the intended joint range.

Track Rod Ends

Inspect inner and outer steering joints for free play, damaged boots and insecure connections.

Suspension Bushes

Look for splitting, separation, excessive movement or signs that the arm is shifting under load.

Anti-Roll Bar Links

Check link joints and mounting points for looseness or play that could create repetitive knocking.

Strut Top Mount

Observe for excessive movement, rough bearing action, spring wind-up or abnormal noise while steering.

Wheel Bearing

Check for roughness, excessive play and evidence that the bearing noise matches the road-speed complaint.

Steering Rack

Inspect rack mountings, inner joints and steering response for movement that should not be present.

Brake Components

Check caliper security, pad hardware, shields and anything that may move or contact during steering and braking.

Fasteners and Mountings

Recently disturbed suspension, brake and wheel fasteners deserve particular attention if the noise began after repair work.

Physical play should be interpreted correctly

Different suspension and steering designs have different normal movement. The technician should distinguish normal articulation from excessive free play before condemning a component.

Physical Checks

What Mechanics Physically Check Before Replacing Parts

A professional inspection should confirm why a component can produce the reported knock. Visual deterioration alone does not always prove the noise source, and some worn joints can look acceptable until they are loaded correctly.

  • ✓ Steering rack mounting security
  • ✓ Column or intermediate-joint play
  • ✓ Power-steering assistance where relevant

Suspension Checks

  • ✓ Ball-joint play
  • ✓ Lower-arm bush movement
  • ✓ Anti-roll bar link wear
  • ✓ Strut top mount movement
  • ✓ Spring condition and seating

Wheel and Bearing Checks

  • ✓ Wheel-bearing play and roughness
  • ✓ Wheel damage or distortion
  • ✓ Tyre bulges or impact damage
  • ✓ Wheel-fitting condition
  • ✓ Signs of abnormal tyre contact

Drivetrain and Brake Checks

  • ✓ CV-boot condition
  • ✓ Outer CV-joint wear clues
  • ✓ Driveshaft movement
  • ✓ Caliper and brake-hardware security
  • ✓ Disc shield or backing-plate contact
Do not assume the first worn component found is automatically the noise source

Older vehicles can have several moderately worn components. The repair should target the part whose condition and behaviour actually match the customer's knocking complaint.

Driving Safety

Is It Safe to Drive With a Knocking Noise When Turning?

Whether it is safe to continue driving depends on the cause of the knock and whether vehicle control is affected. A light anti-roll bar link noise may allow cautious driving to an inspection, while a loose wheel, worn steering joint or badly worn ball joint can create a much more serious risk.

The most important safety checks are steering feel, wheel stability, braking behaviour, tyre condition and whether the knock appeared suddenly after impact or recent repair work.

Lower Concern

Light Occasional Knock

If the noise is quiet and stable, steering feels normal and there are no other symptoms, cautious driving to an inspection may be reasonable.

Needs Inspection

A repeatable CV-joint or suspension-related noise should be diagnosed before wear becomes more severe.

Driving Risk: Medium

Knock With Steering Changes

Driving May Be Possible When...

  • ✓ The steering still feels normal.
  • ✓ The vehicle does not pull strongly.
  • ✓ No wheel movement is visible.
  • ✓ The tyres appear undamaged.
  • ✓ The noise is stable rather than rapidly worsening.

Stop and Arrange Inspection When...

  • ! The steering feels loose or unpredictable.
  • ! The vehicle pulls heavily to one side.
  • ! The wheel visibly moves or feels unstable.
  • ! The knock started after wheel or suspension work.
  • ! The tyre or wheel was damaged by a pothole or kerb.
  • ! The noise becomes a heavy metallic clunk.
Wheel security takes priority over diagnosis

If the knocking began immediately after tyre or wheel work, do not assume it is suspension wear. Wheel fitting and seating should be checked first because an insecure wheel can become dangerous very quickly.

Prevention

How to Reduce the Risk of Steering and Suspension Knocks

Steering and suspension components are wear items, so not every knock can be prevented. Regular inspection and early repair can, however, reduce the chance that small movement develops into a larger or more expensive fault.

Avoid Severe Pothole Impacts

Reduce speed safely where road conditions are poor and inspect the vehicle if a substantial impact occurs.

Maintain Correct Tyre Pressures

Correct pressures help protect tyres and reduce unnecessary wheel and suspension loading.

Check Uneven Tyre Wear

Irregular wear can be an early clue that wheel geometry or suspension condition needs attention.

Inspect Split CV Boots Early

A damaged boot can allow grease loss and contamination, accelerating CV-joint wear.

Act on New Steering Play

Free play should be investigated before it develops into more significant steering or tyre-wear problems.

Use Servicing as an Inspection Opportunity

Steering joints, suspension bushes, ball joints, tyres and wheel bearings can all be inspected during routine maintenance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Car Knocking Noise When Turning FAQs

These questions cover the most common causes, safety concerns, MOT issues and diagnostic checks associated with knocking while steering.

Why does my car knock when turning?

A knocking noise when turning can come from worn CV joints, suspension bushes, ball joints, strut top mounts, track rod ends, anti-roll bar links, wheel bearings, steering components, brake movement or loose wheel fittings.

Why does my car knock on full lock?

Knocking or repeated clicking on full lock commonly points towards outer CV joint wear, especially when the noise appears during slow tight turns while power is being applied.

Why does my car knock when turning left?

Turning left changes the load through the wheels, bearings, suspension, steering and CV joints. The fault may be on either side because noise can travel through the body, so the noisy component should be confirmed by inspection rather than diagnosed from direction alone.

Why does my car knock when turning right?

Turning right changes lateral load through the suspension, steering, wheel bearings and driveshafts. A fault may become louder under this load, but the source should be confirmed with a road test and physical inspection.

Can a CV joint cause knocking when turning?

Yes. A worn outer CV joint commonly produces repeated clicking or knocking during tight turns, especially while accelerating. A split CV boot and grease around the wheel or driveshaft are useful supporting clues.

Can suspension bushes cause knocking when turning?

Yes. Worn or separated suspension bushes can knock as loads change during turning, braking, acceleration or travel over uneven roads.

Can a ball joint cause a knocking noise when turning?

Yes. Excessive ball-joint wear can create knocking or clunking as steering and suspension loads change. Significant play can also affect steering accuracy, tyre wear and MOT condition.

Can a strut top mount knock when turning?

Yes. A worn strut top mount or bearing can knock, pop, creak or twang as the steering turns and the suspension spring and strut assembly move.

Can a track rod end cause knocking when turning?

Yes. A worn track rod end can knock as steering load changes and may also cause looseness, poor alignment, uneven tyre wear or imprecise steering.

Can a wheel bearing knock when turning?

Wheel bearings more commonly hum, drone or rumble, but a badly worn bearing can produce other noises and turning can change the sound as lateral load changes.

Can a pothole cause knocking when turning?

Yes. A pothole can damage a tyre, wheel, lower arm, suspension bush, ball joint, track rod end, strut mount or wheel bearing and can also disturb wheel alignment.

Why did my car start knocking after tyre or wheel work?

A new knock after tyre or wheel work should be checked promptly. Wheel fittings, wheel seating, tyre clearance and anything disturbed during the work should be inspected before normal driving continues.

Is it safe to drive with a knocking noise when turning?

It depends on the cause. A light occasional noise may allow cautious driving to inspection, but avoid driving if the steering feels loose, the car pulls strongly, the knock is heavy or worsening, the wheel feels unstable or the noise began after an impact or recent wheel work.

When should I stop driving with a knocking noise?

Stop driving if knocking is accompanied by loose or unpredictable steering, severe vibration, strong pulling, obvious wheel movement, tyre damage, grinding, a heavy metallic clunk or concern that a wheel or suspension component is insecure.

Can a knocking noise when turning fail an MOT?

The noise itself is a symptom, but the underlying defect can affect the MOT if it involves excessive play, worn suspension or steering joints, damaged springs, insecure components, wheel-bearing play or unsafe wheel fittings.

How does a mechanic diagnose knocking when turning?

A mechanic reproduces the noise and compares steering angle, road speed, acceleration, braking and road bumps before inspecting CV boots and joints, wheel bearings, suspension bushes, ball joints, top mounts, track rod ends, steering components, brakes and wheel fittings.

How much does it cost to fix knocking when turning in the UK?

Repair cost depends on the fault. Anti-roll bar links and some bushes may be relatively inexpensive, while driveshafts, wheel bearings, suspension arms, struts or steering-rack repairs can cost several hundred pounds or more.

What is the best first check for knocking when turning?

First identify exactly when the knock occurs, such as full lock, gentle turns, acceleration, braking or bumps. Then check for obvious tyre or wheel damage, loose wheel fittings, CV-boot damage or grease before arranging a steering and suspension inspection.
