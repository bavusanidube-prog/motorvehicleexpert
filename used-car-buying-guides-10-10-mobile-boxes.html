Quick Answer

What Should You Check Before Buying A Used Car?

Before buying a used car in the UK, check the MOT history, service history, V5C document, VIN number, mileage records, dashboard warning lights, tyre condition, brakes, suspension, accident damage, fluid leaks and how the vehicle behaves during a proper test drive.

A good used car should make sense across the advert, seller answers, paperwork, MOT history, service history, mileage, cold start, test drive and final asking price. If one part of the story does not match, slow down before paying a deposit.

Mechanic Tip:

A fresh MOT is useful, but it is not a full mechanical inspection. It does not prove the clutch, timing chain, turbocharger, battery, gearbox, service history or accident repair quality is good.

Buyer Risk Dashboard

Used Car Buying Risk Dashboard

This section is designed as mobile-friendly cards instead of a wide table. Check each area and judge whether the car is low risk, worth negotiating on, or too risky to buy.

MOT History

Low risk: Clean record with sensible mileage.

Medium risk: Occasional advisories or one repaired failure.

High risk: Repeated failures, corrosion, brake or suspension defects.

Service History

Low risk: Full history with invoices.

Medium risk: Partial records with explainable gaps.

High risk: No proof of servicing or vague seller answers.

Mileage Pattern

Low risk: Consistent year-on-year increase.

Medium risk: Small gaps that can be explained.

High risk: Suspicious drops, missing years or heavy interior wear.

Warning Lights

Low risk: No lights after start-up.

Medium risk: Historic warning with invoice proof.

High risk: Engine, oil, airbag, ABS, brake or temperature warning active.

Seller Behaviour

Low risk: Transparent and allows checks.

Medium risk: Limited knowledge but cooperative.

High risk: Rushes you, avoids paperwork or refuses inspection.

Test Drive

Low risk: Smooth, quiet and stable.

Medium risk: Minor age-related concerns.

High risk: Smoke, overheating, slipping clutch, knocking or power loss.

Simple rule:

One medium-risk area can be manageable. Three or more medium-risk areas should trigger a deeper inspection. Any high-risk issue should be investigated properly before you agree a price.

Buyer risk check

How to judge if a used car is risky

A professional used car check is not about one perfect score or one table. It is about whether the whole story makes sense. The advert, seller answers, MOT history, service records, mileage, paperwork, cold start and test drive should all point in the same direction.

A car does not need to be perfect to be worth buying. Older cars often have wear, advisories or cosmetic marks. The question is whether those issues are honest, understood and reflected in the price.

Low risk

Clear MOT history, believable service records, matching paperwork, smooth test drive, no warning lights, sensible tyre wear and a seller who answers questions properly.

Medium risk

Some MOT advisories, partial service history, light cosmetic repairs, tyres or brakes due soon, or small faults that can be checked and priced before buying.

High risk

Repeated MOT failures, structural corrosion, missing paperwork, warning lights, smoke, overheating, suspicious mileage or a seller who avoids inspection.

Buyer rule:

Do not buy because one thing looks good. Buy only when the car makes sense across history, condition, paperwork, test drive and price.

Decision guide

Should you buy, negotiate or walk away?

A used car buying decision should be based on evidence, not excitement. Use the checks below to decide whether the car is worth buying, worth negotiating on or worth walking away from.

Buy

The MOT history is sensible, service records are believable, paperwork matches, the test drive is smooth and the price reflects the condition.

Negotiate

The car is fundamentally sound but needs tyres, brakes, servicing, battery work or minor repairs that should fairly affect the price.

Walk away

Paperwork is missing, VIN does not match, the seller avoids checks, warning lights stay on, smoke appears or serious faults are being hidden.

Simple rule:

If two or more major areas raise concerns, slow down and investigate properly before committing to the purchase.

Red flags

10 used car red flags that should make you walk away

Some problems are not worth negotiating over. If the seller cannot explain the issue clearly or the car shows several red flags together, it is usually safer to walk away.

  • !VIN or registration details do not match: this is a serious paperwork warning.
  • !Seller refuses proper inspection: a genuine seller should allow reasonable checks.
  • !Multiple warning lights stay on: this may indicate expensive diagnostic faults.
  • !Heavy smoke from cold start: smoke can point to engine, turbo, injector or coolant issues.
  • !Coolant level keeps dropping: possible leak, overheating or head gasket risk.
  • !Evidence of clocked mileage: worn interior, odd MOT mileage or missing history.
  • !Repeated corrosion advisories: rust can become expensive very quickly.
  • !Fresh paint hiding poor repairs: check panel gaps, overspray and colour mismatch.
  • !No service records at all: price must reflect the higher risk.
  • !Pressure selling: rushing, deposit pressure or “another buyer is coming” tactics are warning signs.

Test drive checks

What to watch for on a used car test drive

A test drive often reveals problems you will not spot while the car is parked. Listen for knocking, whining, grinding, clutch judder, steering shake, brake vibration, hesitation, smoke, overheating or dashboard warning lights.

  • ✓Check the car from cold if possible.
  • ✓Make sure it starts cleanly without smoke or warning lights staying on.
  • ✓Test braking, steering, clutch bite, gear changes and acceleration.
  • ✓Listen over bumps for suspension knocks or rattles.
  • ✓Check for overheating, smells, smoke or power loss.

Price negotiation

How to use faults and advisories when negotiating

Most used cars need something. The professional approach is to separate normal age-related wear from faults that should reduce the price. A good negotiation is based on clear evidence, not guessing or arguing.

Small negotiation points

Weak battery, minor service due, small cosmetic marks or one worn tyre. These can justify a modest price adjustment.

Strong negotiation points

Brake wear, several tyres near the limit, suspension knocking, overdue servicing, oil leaks or repeated MOT advisories.

Walk-away points

Structural corrosion, coolant loss, heavy smoke, warning lights, mismatched paperwork, suspicious mileage or hidden accident damage.

Negotiation rule:

If the seller will not allow proper checks or refuses to discuss clear repair concerns, do not force the deal. There will always be another car.

First car advice

Buying your first used car

A first car should be easy to insure, simple to maintain, affordable to repair and safe enough for everyday driving. Do not buy only because the car looks good or has a cheap asking price.

Look for clean service history, sensible mileage, good tyres, no warning lights, long MOT, low insurance group and a test drive that feels smooth. Avoid cars with hidden accident damage, repeated MOT failures or sellers who rush you.

Workshop experience

Workshop experience: common used car buying mistakes

In real used car checks, the biggest mistakes buyers make are focusing on mileage and appearance while ignoring service history, warning lights, tyre wear patterns, MOT history and evidence of poor repairs. Some of the cleanest-looking cars can still hide expensive faults.

A fresh MOT is helpful, but it is not a full mechanical inspection. The MOT test does not tell you whether the clutch is nearly worn out, whether the timing chain is noisy, whether the car has poor service history, or whether the battery and charging system are weak.

The best approach is to check the car in layers: paperwork first, MOT history second, bodywork and tyres third, then cold start, test drive and final price negotiation. If something feels rushed, hidden or unclear, walk away.

A common example is a vehicle sold with a long MOT but tyres already close to the legal limit, brake discs heavily worn and suspension advisories repeated for more than one year. The car may look cheap in the advert, but after tyres, brakes and suspension repairs, the real cost can be much higher than expected.

Another frequent workshop finding is poor service history behind a clean-looking vehicle. A car can look tidy and drive reasonably well during a short test drive, but missed oil changes can increase the risk of timing chain noise, turbo wear, sludge build-up, DPF issues and engine management faults later.

Coolant loss is another warning sign that buyers often underestimate. A seller may top up coolant before the viewing, but signs such as a low expansion tank, sweet coolant smell, overheating, white exhaust smoke or pressure in the cooling system should never be ignored.

Warning lights are another major red flag. A seller may describe an engine management light, ABS light or airbag light as a simple reset, but without diagnostic evidence and repair invoices, the buyer is taking the risk. Active warning lights should be diagnosed before money changes hands.

FAQs

Used car buying questions

Common questions UK drivers ask before buying a used car.

What should I check before buying a used car in the UK?

Check MOT history, service history, V5C, VIN, mileage records, warning lights, tyre condition, brakes, suspension, accident repair signs, fluid leaks and how the car behaves on a proper test drive.

Is a fresh MOT enough when buying a used car?

No. A fresh MOT only shows the car met minimum MOT standards at the time of inspection. It does not prove the car has been serviced properly, has no hidden faults or will not need repairs soon.

Should I buy a used car with MOT advisories?

It depends on the advisory. Minor advisories may be manageable, but rust, suspension wear, tyres, brakes and leaks should be priced properly.

Is 100,000 miles too much for a used car?

Not automatically. A well-maintained 100,000-mile car can be better than a neglected low-mileage car.

How can I check if a used car is stolen or written off?

Check the V5C, VIN, plates, MOT history and use a vehicle history check before buying.

What should make me walk away from a used car?

Walk away from missing paperwork, pressure selling, warning lights, hidden damage, mileage doubts, unpaid finance risk or sellers who refuse checks.

Can a car pass an MOT and still have problems?

Yes. An MOT does not fully check clutch wear, service quality, timing chain noise, future repair risk or hidden accident damage.

Is no service history a deal-breaker?

Not always, but it increases risk and should affect the price. Missing history is more serious on complex, high-mileage or diesel cars.

How much should I keep aside after buying a used car?

Keep money aside for servicing, tyres, brakes, battery replacement, MOT work and unexpected repairs. A cheap used car can become expensive quickly if maintenance has been ignored.

Should I buy from a dealer or private seller?

Dealers may offer more legal protection and preparation, while private sellers may be cheaper. Either way, check paperwork, MOT history, service history, vehicle condition and test drive behaviour before buying.

What repair costs should I budget for after buying a used car?

Budget for servicing, tyres, brake pads, brake discs, battery replacement, MOT repairs, suspension work and unexpected diagnostics. Higher-risk cars may also need clutch, timing belt, timing chain, turbo, DPF, cooling system or gearbox repairs.

Is MOT history more important than mileage?

MOT history can be more useful than mileage alone because it shows mileage patterns, failures, advisories, corrosion warnings, tyre wear, brakes and suspension issues.

Should I walk away from a car with warning lights?

Be very cautious if warning lights stay on during a viewing or test drive. Engine, oil, temperature, ABS, airbag, brake or steering warnings can indicate expensive faults.

How many MOT advisories are too many?

One or two minor advisories may be manageable, but repeated advisories for tyres, brakes, suspension, corrosion, oil leaks or structural areas should make you inspect carefully and price the repair risk.
