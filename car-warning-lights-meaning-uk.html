Quick answer

What Do Car Warning Light Colours Mean?

Red warning lights normally need the fastest response. They can indicate a condition that may threaten vehicle safety or cause serious mechanical damage if driving continues. Oil-pressure, overheating and serious braking warnings are examples that should never be casually ignored.

Amber or yellow normally means a fault or condition needs attention. Some steady amber warnings may allow cautious onward driving if the vehicle feels completely normal, but diagnosis should not be delayed. Stop instead if the warning is flashing or accompanied by serious symptoms.

Green and blue lights are usually status indicators. They commonly confirm that a normal system such as main beam, exterior lighting, indicators or another vehicle function is active. They are not normally asking you to arrange a repair.

Warning-light colour logic

How Car Warning-Light Colours Work

Dashboard warning-light colours are designed to help the driver judge priority, but the colour itself does not identify the mechanical fault. Two amber warnings can represent completely different systems, and two red warnings can require different responses depending on what the vehicle is doing at the same time.

The most useful way to interpret a dashboard warning is to combine five pieces of information: the colour, the symbol, whether it is steady or flashing, when it appeared and whether the vehicle has any supporting symptoms.

1. Colour

How urgent is the warning?

Red normally demands the fastest response. Amber or yellow usually means attention or diagnosis is needed. Green and blue are commonly informational.

2. Symbol

Which system is involved?

The shape of the warning tells you whether the concern relates to oil, charging, braking, engine management, steering, tyres, emissions or another monitored system.

3. Behaviour

Steady, flashing or intermittent?

The same symbol can mean something more serious when it flashes, appears repeatedly or is triggered under a particular driving condition.

4. Timing

When did it appear?

A warning that appears only during starting, after a repair, under braking, during hard acceleration or after the engine becomes hot gives useful diagnostic information.

5. Symptoms

What is the car doing?

Overheating, smoke, heavy steering, poor braking, misfiring, unusual noise or power loss can make a warning far more urgent than the light alone.

Decision

What should happen next?

Use the combined pattern to decide whether to stop, carry out a simple safe check, drive cautiously to a place of repair or arrange diagnostic testing.

A dashboard light is a warning signal, not a replacement-part instruction

A battery symbol does not automatically mean the battery is defective, an ABS light does not automatically mean the ABS pump has failed and an engine-management light does not automatically identify a particular sensor. The warning tells you which system needs investigation; diagnosis confirms why it was triggered.

🟠 Fault or attention required

What Do Amber or Yellow Car Warning Lights Mean?

Amber and yellow dashboard lights usually indicate that the vehicle has detected a fault, abnormal operating condition or maintenance requirement that needs attention. They generally allow more time to respond than a red warning, but they should not be ignored simply because the vehicle still drives normally.

The correct decision depends on the particular system and whether the light is steady or flashing. Some amber warnings allow cautious onward driving; others can become urgent if they are accompanied by misfiring, severe power loss, overheating, braking changes or another serious symptom.

🟢 Green & 🔵 blue indicators

What Do Green and Blue Dashboard Lights Mean?

Green and blue dashboard lights are usually status indicators rather than fault warnings. They commonly confirm that a normal vehicle function is active.

This is why judging urgency purely from dashboard colour can be misleading. A blue main-beam symbol can be completely normal while a flashing amber engine-management light can require urgent attention.

Exterior lighting

Lights switched on

Green symbols commonly show dipped beam, side lights, fog lights or other exterior-lighting functions.

Main beam

Blue high-beam indicator

The blue headlamp symbol normally confirms that main beam is active. It is not itself a fault warning.

Indicators

Direction signal active

Green direction arrows normally flash when the indicators or hazard warning lights operate.

Cruise control

Driver-assistance status

A green cruise-control or speed-limiter symbol commonly indicates that the system is selected or actively controlling speed.

EV / hybrid status

Ready or operating mode

Hybrid and electric vehicles can use green status messages to show that the vehicle is ready to drive even when no combustion engine is running.

Warning behaviour matters

Flashing vs Steady Warning Lights: Why the Pattern Matters

A dashboard warning does not always mean the same thing when it is steady, flashing or intermittent. The behaviour of the warning can reveal whether the system is reporting a stored fault, actively intervening or warning that a condition has become more urgent.

Steady warning

Fault or condition remains present

A steady warning commonly means the control system has detected a condition that remains active or has met the threshold for illuminating the dashboard lamp.

The vehicle may still drive normally, but diagnosis should be based on the actual system involved rather than waiting for the warning to become worse.

Flashing warning

Meaning or urgency can change

Flashing can indicate active intervention or a more urgent operating condition. A flashing engine-management light can indicate a severe misfire, while flashing traction-control lamps can simply mean the system is actively correcting wheel slip.

The exact symbol is essential before deciding what the flashing means.

Intermittent warning

Look for the trigger

A light that appears only over bumps, during hard acceleration, while braking, when the engine is hot or during cold starting can reveal an electrical, mechanical or operating-condition pattern.

Appears then disappears

The fault may still be stored

A warning going out does not always mean the problem has disappeared. Intermittent faults can leave diagnostic trouble codes or freeze-frame data stored in a control module.

Flashing engine-management light with misfire symptoms

If the engine-management light is flashing while the engine is shaking, misfiring or losing power, reduce load and stop as soon as it is safe. Continued severe misfire can send unburned fuel into the exhaust and overheat or damage the catalytic converter.

Flashing does not always mean a fault

Some systems deliberately flash their dashboard indicator while operating. Traction or stability-control lights, for example, can flash normally when the system is reducing wheel spin. What matters is whether the lamp keeps flashing after the event, remains permanently illuminated or appears with other symptoms.

Multiple warnings

Why Have Several Warning Lights Come On at the Same Time?

Several dashboard warnings appearing together can look as though multiple unrelated systems have failed at once. Sometimes that is possible, but in many cases the lights share a common electrical, sensor, voltage or communication cause.

Modern vehicles use interconnected control modules. ABS, stability control, steering assistance, engine management, transmission control and driver assistance can share information such as wheel speed, battery voltage, engine torque and vehicle speed. One missing input can therefore affect several systems.

Low system voltage

One electrical problem, many warnings

A weak battery, poor connection or charging problem can reduce module voltage enough to trigger several warning lamps during starting or immediately afterwards.

Shared sensor

One missing input affects several systems

A wheel-speed signal, steering-angle input or another shared sensor may be used by ABS, traction control, stability control and other functions. One fault can therefore generate several warnings.

Communication fault

Modules cannot exchange information

Modern control units communicate across vehicle networks. A wiring, power-supply or module-communication problem can create apparently unrelated dashboard warnings.

Primary mechanical fault

One fault creates secondary warnings

A serious engine fault, overheating event or charging failure may cause other systems to reduce assistance or disable functions as a protective response.

After battery work

Temporary calibration warnings

Battery disconnection or replacement can occasionally leave steering, stability or window systems requiring relearning or normal driving before some stored warnings disappear.

Do not guess

Diagnose the first common cause

If six warning lights appear together, replacing six components is not a sensible starting point. Check voltage, common power supplies, grounds, shared inputs and diagnostic codes first.

Mechanic diagnostic rule: look for the common denominator

When several systems complain at the same moment, ask what they share. Battery voltage, an earth connection, wheel-speed data, network communication or a recently disturbed electrical connection can explain a group of warnings more logically than several simultaneous component failures.

A dashboard light becomes much more useful diagnostically when you consider what the vehicle is doing at the same time. Two cars can display the same warning but require very different responses because one has no other symptoms while the other is overheating, misfiring or losing braking assistance.

Stop and allow the engine to cool

A warning accompanied by a rising temperature gauge, steam or coolant smell should be treated as a cooling-system problem first rather than simply a dashboard-electronics issue.

Symptoms can override the apparent urgency of the colour

An amber warning accompanied by severe misfire, smoke or major power loss can require more urgent action than a red indicator that simply confirms the parking brake is applied. Identify the symbol and assess the vehicle behaviour together.

Stop-driving decision

When Should You Stop Driving for a Red Warning Light?

The safest approach is to stop as soon as reasonably possible where the warning indicates a condition that could cause rapid mechanical damage or reduced vehicle control.

Stop as soon as safe

Oil-pressure warning

Continuing to run an engine without adequate oil pressure can turn a minor fault into severe engine damage very quickly.

Stop as soon as safe

Engine overheating

Do not continue driving an overheating engine in the hope that the temperature will fall. Cylinder-head and head-gasket damage can follow.

Stop if braking is affected

Brake warning

A brake warning becomes particularly serious if accompanied by low fluid, unusual pedal feel, pulling, fluid leakage or reduced braking performance.

Stop if steering is affected

Steering warning

If assistance disappears or the vehicle becomes difficult to steer, continuing unnecessarily can increase risk.

Stop if electrical power is collapsing

Charging warning

A charging failure can eventually stop the engine or disable electrical assistance. Multiple warnings, dimming lights or heavy steering can indicate voltage is falling.

Use context

Not every red icon means breakdown

A seat-belt reminder, open-door warning or applied parking-brake indicator can also be red. Read the exact symbol rather than responding to colour alone.

Safe-to-drive decision

Can You Keep Driving With an Amber Warning Light?

Sometimes, but only after considering the exact system and vehicle behaviour. A steady amber warning on an otherwise normally operating car may allow cautious onward driving to a safe place or garage, but that is not the same as saying the fault is safe to ignore indefinitely.

Usually lower urgency

Steady warning, car feels normal

If the vehicle drives normally, temperatures are stable and there are no unusual noises, smells or handling changes, cautious onward driving may be reasonable for some amber faults.

Diagnose promptly

Warning remains after restart

A warning that remains on or repeatedly returns should be diagnosed rather than ignored until the next service or MOT.

Stop instead

Serious symptoms appear

Stop if the amber light is accompanied by major power loss, heavy misfire, smoke, overheating, poor braking or another symptom suggesting immediate damage or safety risk.

“Amber” is not a repair deadline

There is no universal number of miles that every amber warning can safely be driven. The actual system, fault, symptoms and rate of deterioration determine urgency.

Shared electrical cause

How Battery Voltage Can Trigger Multiple Warning Lights

Vehicle control modules depend on a stable electrical supply. If battery voltage falls too low during cranking or the alternator fails to maintain charging voltage after the engine starts, electronic systems may begin storing faults or shutting functions down.

This is one reason a vehicle can suddenly display ABS, steering, traction, airbag, engine and other warnings together even though those components did not all fail simultaneously.

Weak battery

Voltage drops during cranking

A tired battery may still start the engine while allowing voltage to fall far enough for sensitive modules to record undervoltage or communication faults.

Charging fault

Voltage falls after starting

If the alternator is not charging correctly, the car begins operating from battery reserve. As voltage falls, multiple systems may report faults or become unavailable.

Poor connection

Battery may not be the real fault

Loose or corroded terminals, poor earth straps or high resistance in charging cables can create voltage problems even when the battery and alternator themselves are capable of working correctly.

Battery light after fitting a new battery?

A new battery does not repair a failed charging system. If the battery warning remains illuminated after replacement, alternator output, belt drive, cables, connections and charging control still need testing.

Start-up warning pattern

Why Warning Lights Come On When You Start the Car

Many dashboard lamps are supposed to illuminate when the ignition is first switched on. This is part of the normal system self-check and confirms that the warning device itself can operate.

The useful diagnostic distinction is whether the lamp goes out normally, remains illuminated or appears only after the engine starts.

Normal self-check

Lights illuminate then go out

Many engine, ABS, airbag and other warning devices briefly illuminate before disappearing once the control system completes its checks.

Stays on

System has detected a fault

A lamp that remains illuminated after the normal self-check may indicate a stored or current malfunction requiring diagnosis.

Several lights remain

Check starting voltage

Multiple electronic warnings immediately after a difficult start should prompt battery-voltage, terminal and charging-system checks before unrelated components are condemned.

Battery warning remains

Check charging output

The battery lamp should normally go out once the charging system begins operating correctly. If it stays on, charging-system diagnosis is required.

Engine cranks slowly

Starting system deserves attention

Slow cranking together with multiple warnings can indicate low battery state of charge, poor electrical connections or excessive starter current draw.

Do not blame the dashboard lights

Clicking with no cranking points the diagnosis toward the battery, connections, starter circuit or starter motor rather than the warning lamps themselves.

Post-repair diagnosis

Why Has a Warning Light Appeared After a Repair or Service?

When a warning light appears immediately after work has been carried out, the recent repair becomes an important diagnostic clue. This does not prove the garage caused the fault, but the first sensible step is to check what was disconnected, moved, reset, drained or replaced.

Starting diagnosis with the work just performed is often faster and more logical than treating the new warning as an unrelated failure.

Connector disturbed

Something may not be fully connected

Sensors and actuators are often unplugged during repair work. A loose connector, damaged pin or wiring pulled under tension can trigger a warning immediately afterwards.

Battery disconnected

Stored faults or relearning

Disconnecting a battery can clear learned values or create temporary low-voltage records. Some systems may require recalibration or normal driving before all functions return.

Fluid level changed

Recheck the service work

Oil, coolant and brake-fluid warnings appearing after related work deserve an immediate level and leak check once the vehicle is in the correct safe condition for inspection.

Sensor or hose disturbed

Air and intake systems matter

Intake hoses, vacuum lines, airflow sensors and electrical connectors disturbed during servicing can create engine-management faults if they are not refitted correctly.

Service reminder

Reset may not have been completed

A service reminder is different from a genuine fault warning. If the vehicle was serviced but the maintenance counter was not reset, the reminder can remain visible even though the work was completed.

  • Service Light On After Service
Existing fault

Timing does not always prove causation

A fault can coincidentally appear after service work. Good diagnosis checks the recent work first without automatically assuming that every later problem was caused by it.

A useful post-repair question

Ask: what changed immediately before the warning appeared? Battery replacement, brake work, cooling-system repairs, engine servicing, wheel removal or electrical diagnosis can each narrow the first inspection area.

Do not simply clear a new warning after repair

If a light appeared because a connector, fluid level, charging circuit or other part of the repair is incorrect, deleting the code only removes the evidence temporarily. Confirm the underlying cause before clearing stored faults.

Workshop-style diagnosis

How a Mechanic Diagnoses Dashboard Warning Lights

Good warning-light diagnosis does not begin by clearing codes or ordering the component named in a fault description. It starts by establishing what happened, when it happened and whether the warning matches an actual mechanical, electrical or safety-system symptom.

A useful diagnostic process moves from simple evidence to more specific testing. This reduces the risk of replacing good parts because a stored fault code was interpreted too literally.

Step 1

Identify the exact warning

Record the symbol, colour, any dashboard message and whether the light is steady, flashing or intermittent. Manufacturer wording can provide useful context.

Step 2

Confirm when it happens

Note whether the warning appears during starting, after the engine warms up, while braking, during acceleration, after repair work or only under a particular load or road condition.

Step 3

Check vehicle behaviour

Look for overheating, misfiring, poor braking, heavy steering, power loss, unusual noise, smoke, vibration or any change that alters the urgency of the warning.

Step 4

Carry out basic physical checks

Where safe, inspect relevant fluid levels, battery terminals, visible wiring, connectors, hoses, belts, tyre pressures and obvious signs of leakage or damage before moving to deeper testing.

Step 5

Scan all relevant modules

A proper diagnostic scan may need to interrogate engine, ABS, airbag, steering, transmission and body modules rather than reading engine codes only.

Step 6

Test before replacing

Use voltage readings, live data, circuit checks, pressure measurements, mechanical inspection or other appropriate tests to prove why the control unit recorded the fault.

The warning light is often the beginning of diagnosis, not the end

A dashboard lamp tells you that a monitored condition needs attention. The underlying cause can be a component, wiring, power supply, ground, mechanical problem, fluid condition, shared sensor or communication fault. Confirm the cause before authorising parts.

OBD & diagnostic scanners

What Fault Codes Can — and Cannot — Tell You

Diagnostic trouble codes are extremely useful, but a fault code normally describes what the control unit detected, not automatically which component should be replaced.

A code relating to a sensor circuit, for example, can be caused by the sensor itself, damaged wiring, a poor connector, missing reference voltage, a bad ground, a mechanical condition affecting the sensor reading or another fault elsewhere in the system.

Code

Identifies the detected problem

The code narrows the diagnosis toward a system, circuit or operating condition.

Freeze-frame data

Shows when the fault occurred

Where available, stored operating data can show engine speed, load, temperature and other conditions present when a fault was recorded.

Live data

Shows what the system sees now

Sensor values and calculated data can help identify readings that are implausible, missing or inconsistent with the actual vehicle condition.

Circuit testing

Confirms electrical integrity

Voltage, ground, continuity and signal checks can distinguish a failed component from damaged wiring or a poor connection.

Mechanical testing

Electronics cannot prove everything

Compression, pressure, fluid, braking and mechanical faults may still require physical measurement even when diagnostic codes are present.

Final confirmation

Repair the cause, then verify

After repair, clear relevant codes where appropriate, operate the vehicle under the conditions that previously triggered the fault and confirm that the warning does not return.

Never use a fault code as an automatic instruction to buy a part

A code containing the words “sensor”, “solenoid”, “circuit” or “module” does not prove that the named part has failed. Testing the circuit and operating conditions first can prevent expensive misdiagnosis.

UK MOT

Can Dashboard Warning Lights Cause an MOT Failure?

Yes, some warning-light faults can cause an MOT failure, but there is no rule that every illuminated dashboard symbol fails the test. The MOT decision depends on the system being inspected, the age and type of vehicle and whether the warning device indicates a defined malfunction.

Normal status indicators such as main beam or a deliberately disabled passenger-airbag indicator should not be confused with a malfunction warning. The tester applies the criteria for the particular system.

Engine MIL

Can be a Major defect

On vehicles where the engine malfunction indicator is within the MOT inspection requirements, an applicable MIL that is inoperative or indicating a malfunction is a Major defect.

  • Will an Engine Management Light Fail an MOT?
ABS

Malfunction warning is Major

An ABS warning device that does not work correctly or indicates a system malfunction is classed as a Major defect.

  • Can an ABS Light Fail an MOT?
Airbag / SRS

SRS malfunction is Major

An SRS malfunction indicator showing a system malfunction is a Major defect. A lamp indicating that the passenger airbag has deliberately been switched off is not treated as the same fault.

  • Can an Airbag Light Fail an MOT?
Electronic steering

EPS malfunction can be Major

An electronic power-steering malfunction indicator showing a system fault is a Major defect. Inoperative power assistance can also be a Major defect.

TPMS

Vehicle applicability matters

TPMS inspection applies to applicable M1 vehicles first used on or after 1 January 2012. A system malfunction can be a Major defect, but a lamp indicating previous tyre-pressure loss does not automatically prove that the TPMS itself has malfunctioned.

  • Can a Tyre Pressure Light Fail an MOT?
Dashboard messages

Message alone is not the defect

A text message appearing on a dashboard display is not automatically an MOT defect by itself, although it can help the tester establish whether the relevant monitored system is indicating a malfunction.

Do not clear warning lights simply to present the vehicle for MOT

Erasing a stored code does not repair an ABS sensor, SRS circuit, emissions fault, steering malfunction or TPMS problem. If the underlying condition remains, the warning can return and the relevant MOT defect can still be present.

MOT rules are system-specific

The MOT inspection manual defines separate requirements for engine emissions warnings, ABS, SRS, electronic power steering, TPMS and other systems. That is why the exact symbol and vehicle applicability matter more than the warning colour alone.

How Much Does It Cost to Diagnose or Fix a Warning Light?

There is no single “warning-light repair cost” because the lamp is only the visible symptom. The underlying repair could be a loose connection, weak battery, wheel-speed sensor, alternator, wiring fault, emissions problem or expensive electronic module.

The sensible starting cost is therefore diagnosis. Once the cause is confirmed, the repair estimate becomes meaningful.

Basic diagnosis

Scan + initial assessment

Used-car buying

Should You Buy a Used Car With a Warning Light On?

A warning light does not automatically make a used car a bad purchase, but it should never be accepted on the seller's unsupported claim that “it only needs a sensor”.

Until the fault has been properly diagnosed, the repair exposure is unknown. A £100 repair and a four-figure repair can sometimes begin with a similar dashboard warning.

Ignition on

Watch the self-check

Before starting the engine, check that expected warning lamps illuminate normally. A missing lamp can be as important as one that stays on.

Engine started

Check what remains illuminated

Engine, ABS, SRS, steering or other malfunction warnings remaining after the normal self-check deserve investigation.

Test drive

See whether the warning returns

Some faults only appear after the vehicle moves, exceeds a particular speed, warms up or operates under load.

Recent code clearing

Be cautious

A dashboard with no warnings at the beginning of a viewing does not prove there are no faults. Recently cleared codes can return once the relevant monitor or driving condition runs again.

MOT history

Look for recurring system faults

Previous failures and advisories can help reveal whether the current warning relates to an older unresolved problem or repeated pattern.

Independent inspection

Diagnose expensive warnings before buying

ABS, airbag, engine, gearbox, steering, DPF and multiple electrical warnings justify proper diagnosis before agreeing a price.

“It only needs clearing” is not a diagnosis

If a seller says a dashboard warning only needs resetting, ask why it is illuminated and what test has confirmed the cause. If the repair is truly simple, it is reasonable to ask why it has not already been completed.

Frequently asked questions

Car Warning-Light Colours FAQs

What do the different car warning light colours mean?

Warning-light colours help indicate status and urgency. Red commonly means urgent attention, amber or yellow usually means a fault or condition that needs checking, and green or blue normally indicates that a system or vehicle function is operating. Colour is only the starting point because the exact symbol, whether it is flashing and how the vehicle behaves also matter.

Does a red warning light always mean stop driving immediately?

Not every red symbol has exactly the same meaning, but red warnings should always be taken seriously. Stop as soon as it is safe where the warning involves oil pressure, overheating, braking, serious steering problems or another condition that could make continued driving unsafe or cause rapid mechanical damage.

Can you drive with an amber warning light on?

Sometimes. A steady amber warning may allow cautious onward driving when the vehicle feels completely normal, but the fault should still be diagnosed. Stop instead if the warning is accompanied by overheating, smoke, severe power loss, misfiring, poor braking, heavy steering or another serious symptom.

What do green and blue dashboard lights mean?

Green and blue dashboard lights are usually status indicators rather than fault warnings. They commonly show that functions such as exterior lighting, main beam, indicators or cruise control are active. Always check the exact symbol because manufacturer displays can vary.

Is a flashing warning light more serious than a steady light?

It can be. Flashing can change the meaning or urgency of a warning. A flashing engine-management light, for example, can indicate an active severe misfire that may damage the catalytic converter. The correct response depends on the particular symbol and the symptoms occurring at the same time.

Why have several warning lights come on at the same time?

Several warning lights appearing together do not always mean that several separate components have failed. A low system voltage, charging fault, communication problem, shared sensor input or one primary fault can sometimes trigger warnings across multiple systems. Diagnosis should look for a common cause before individual parts are replaced.

Can a weak battery cause several dashboard warning lights?

Yes. Low battery voltage or unstable charging voltage can cause control modules to record faults and may illuminate several warning lights, particularly during starting. Battery condition, charging voltage, terminals and electrical connections should be checked before assuming that every displayed system has failed.

Why did warning lights appear after starting the car?

Many warning lights illuminate briefly during the normal ignition self-check and then go out. Lights that remain on or appear immediately after starting can relate to battery voltage, charging, a stored system fault, fluid pressure, sensor inputs or another condition that needs investigation.

Why has a warning light appeared after a repair or service?

A warning appearing after repair work should be assessed in the context of what was disturbed or disconnected. Possible causes include a connector left unplugged, low battery voltage, a disturbed sensor or hose, an incorrect fluid level, a fault that was already present or a service reminder that was not reset correctly.

Does the battery warning light mean the battery needs replacing?

Not necessarily. The battery symbol usually warns about the charging system rather than diagnosing the battery itself. Possible causes include alternator output, the drive belt, voltage regulation, charging cables, poor connections or the battery. The system should be tested before parts are replaced.

What should I do if a warning light comes on with other symptoms?

Treat the combination of the warning and the vehicle behaviour more seriously than the light alone. Stop where appropriate if there is overheating, smoke, a burning smell, severe misfire, significant power loss, poor braking, heavy steering, unusual mechanical noise or another symptom that could indicate immediate safety or damage risk.

Should I clear a warning light with a diagnostic scanner?

Only after the underlying fault has been understood and repaired where necessary. Clearing a diagnostic trouble code does not repair a damaged sensor, wiring fault, misfire, charging problem, ABS fault or other underlying condition. The warning may simply return.

Does a fault code tell you which part to replace?

Usually not. A fault code identifies a circuit, system or operating condition detected by a control module. The actual cause may be the named component, but it may also be wiring, connectors, power supply, ground, mechanical conditions or another input affecting the system. Testing should confirm the cause before replacement.

Can dashboard warning lights cause an MOT failure?

Yes, some can. There is no blanket rule that every dashboard light fails an MOT. The result depends on the system, vehicle applicability and the defect. Applicable engine malfunction, ABS, SRS, electronic power steering and TPMS malfunction indications can affect the MOT, while many normal status lights do not represent faults.

Will an ABS or airbag warning light fail an MOT?

An ABS warning device indicating a system malfunction and an SRS malfunction indicator showing a system fault can be classed as Major MOT defects where the relevant inspection requirements apply. A passenger-airbag disabled indicator is not the same as an SRS malfunction warning.

Will a tyre-pressure warning light fail an MOT?

The MOT checks TPMS on applicable M1 vehicles first used on or after 1 January 2012. A malfunctioning or obviously inoperative applicable TPMS can be a Major defect. A warning caused only by low tyre pressure is not automatically the same as a system malfunction.

How much does it cost to diagnose a dashboard warning light?

Diagnostic cost depends on the vehicle, fault and testing required. A basic code read can be relatively inexpensive, while electrical tracing, intermittent faults, module communication problems or mechanical testing can require considerably more workshop time. A proper diagnosis should identify the cause before an expensive component is authorised.

What is the best first step when a dashboard warning light comes on?

Identify the exact symbol, note its colour and whether it is steady or flashing, read any dashboard message and pay attention to how the vehicle behaves. Check simple items only where it is safe to do so, then use the warning pattern and any supporting symptoms to decide whether to stop, inspect the vehicle or arrange diagnostic testing.
